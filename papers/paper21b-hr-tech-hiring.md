## Abstract

Algorithmic hiring and HR technology constitute a $35.6 billion global market (Grand View Research, 2023) that mediates access to livelihood for hundreds of millions of applicants annually. A single platform, iCIMS, processes approximately 75 million applications per year (iCIMS Workforce Report, 2022). HireVue has conducted over 70 million AI-scored video interviews across more than 800 client organizations (HireVue, 2024). The Society for Human Resource Management estimates that more than 75 percent of large employers deploy some form of applicant tracking system (SHRM, 2022). These systems operate under conditions of near-total informational asymmetry: applicants submit comprehensive personal data into decision architectures whose criteria, weightings, and comparative thresholds remain entirely invisible to them, receive binary outcomes with zero explanatory feedback, and cannot withdraw from engagement without exiting the labor market. The Harvard Business School "Hidden Workers" study estimated that automated screening criteria filter out 27 million qualified workers in the United States alone (Fuller, Raman, Sage-Gavin, and Hines, 2021). Raghavan, Barocas, Kleinberg, and Levy audited algorithmic hiring vendors and found that zero provided meaningful algorithmic transparency to candidates (Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency, 2020). Amazon abandoned an internal AI recruiting tool in 2018 after discovering systematic gender bias in which the system penalized resumes containing the word "women's" and downgraded graduates of all-women's colleges (Reuters, October 2018). This paper applies the Void Framework — a triaxial diagnostic that identifies opacity, responsiveness, and engaged observer coupling as jointly sufficient conditions for predictable harm generation — to the full spectrum of algorithmic hiring technologies, from resume screening through AI video interviewing, game-based psychometric assessment, automated performance monitoring, and algorithmic termination.

This paper makes the following contributions. First, it scores the algorithmic hiring domain at 10 out of 12 on the Void Index (Opacity 3, Responsiveness 3, Coupling 3, Modifiers +1) with an estimated Péclet number of 6.1, placing it firmly in Phase IV Pandemonium on the demon lattice classification, a regime in which drift cascades propagate faster than corrective interventions can contain them. Second, it provides platform-level Void Model Cards for seven major hiring technologies, revealing that AI video interviewing (HireVue pre-2021 configuration: 11/12, Pe 7.3) and game-based psychometric tools (Pymetrics: 10/12, Pe 5.8) score near the theoretical maximum, while structured human interviews with standardized rubrics (4/12, Pe 1.4) and blind skill-based assessments (2/12, Pe 0.8) anchor the constraint pole, confirming that transparency, invariance, and independence suppress harm even within the same functional domain. Third, it maps the complete drift cascade from D1 agency attribution — in which applicants and employers alike treat algorithmic scores as objective measurements of candidate quality — through D2 boundary erosion — in which facial expression analysis, vocal prosody scoring, and gameplay behavioral profiling dissolve the boundary between job-relevant competence and psychological surveillance — to D3 harm facilitation, in which systematically biased filtering operates at population scale without individualized accountability. Fourth, it demonstrates that the EU AI Act's classification of recruitment AI as high-risk under Annex III Section 4 (Regulation 2024/1689, effective August 2, 2026 for these provisions), its Article 5 prohibition of emotion recognition in workplace contexts (in force since February 2, 2025), New York City Local Law 144's mandate for bias audits of automated employment decision tools (effective July 5, 2023), and the Illinois Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act (effective January 1, 2020) each target specific void conditions but leave the triaxial architecture substantially intact. Fifth, it documents L1-to-L3 vocabulary drift in vendor marketing and employer adoption discourse, tracing the progression from "statistical ranking model" through "talent intelligence" to "AI recruiter" as evidence of entity-projection dynamics that obscure the system's mechanistic character. Sixth, it analyzes the kill condition for this domain: if algorithmic hiring demonstrably produces superior applicant outcomes — measured by job-match quality, perceived fairness, and reduced discrimination — compared with structured human alternatives, the framework's predictions fail. Available evidence, including the Bogen and Rieke taxonomy of hiring algorithm equity failures (Upturn, 2018), the EEOC and DOJ joint guidance on AI and disability discrimination (2022, updated 2023), and systematic audit data, indicates that this kill condition is not met. Seventh, it identifies the forced-engagement mechanism as the coupling amplifier unique to employment contexts: because applicants cannot exit algorithmic hiring without exiting the labor market, coupling scores remain maximal regardless of applicant awareness or skepticism. Eighth, it proposes a regulatory sufficiency test derived from the void architecture, specifying the minimum transparency, invariance, and independence requirements that would reduce the domain Péclet number below the Phase IV threshold of 4.0, and evaluates existing regulatory instruments against this benchmark.

## I. Introduction

Every modern economy rests on a single recurring transaction: the matching of human labor to institutional need. When that transaction is mediated well, individuals secure livelihoods, firms capture productivity, and societies distribute opportunity with at least rough correspondence to merit. When it is mediated poorly, qualified workers starve while vacancies fester, demographic disparities compound across generations, and public trust in meritocratic institutions erodes. The stakes of the hiring decision are, for the individual applicant, existential in the precise sense that few other algorithmic sorting contexts can claim: denial of employment threatens shelter, nutrition, health insurance, social identity, and psychological stability in ways that denial of a loan or a content recommendation does not. It is against this backdrop of existential consequence that a global industry has quietly restructured the hiring transaction around automated systems whose internal logic is invisible to the people whose futures depend on it.

The scale of this restructuring defies casual comprehension. The global human-resource technology market reached an estimated $35.6 billion in 2025, with projections exceeding $53 billion by 2029 (Grand View Research, 2023). A single applicant tracking platform, iCIMS, processes approximately 75 million job applications per year through its infrastructure alone (iCIMS, 2022). HireVue, the dominant AI video interviewing vendor, has conducted more than 70 million candidate interviews across over 800 client organizations, a figure that included facial expression analysis scoring on every interview conducted prior to the company's January 2021 discontinuation of that feature (HireVue, 2021). LinkedIn surpassed one billion registered members by late 2024, and its Talent Solutions segment—the recruiter-facing tools that rank, filter, and surface candidates—generated approximately $7.3 billion in revenue for Microsoft's fiscal year 2024 (Microsoft, 2024). When Unilever deployed neuroscience-based game assessments developed by Pymetrics (now Harver), the company reported screening more than 250,000 candidates through the platform in a single year (Cutter, 2019). The Society for Human Resource Management estimated that more than 75 percent of large employers use some form of applicant tracking system to screen incoming applications (SHRM, 2022). These are not pilot programs or experimental deployments. Algorithmic hiring is the default architecture through which labor-market access is now allocated in knowledge-economy roles across most industrialized nations.

The defining structural feature of this architecture is not its efficiency, its bias, or its legality—all of which have received substantial scholarly attention—but rather the totality of information asymmetry it imposes on the applicant. In a conventional hiring process, opacity is partial and negotiable: the candidate may not know exactly why they were rejected, but the human interviewer's reasoning is at least in principle legible, contestable, and subject to social accountability. Algorithmic hiring introduces a qualitatively different regime. The applicant submits comprehensive personal data—employment history, educational credentials, demographic indicators embedded in name and address, writing samples, video recordings of facial expressions and vocal patterns, performance on gamified psychometric instruments—into a decision process whose criteria, weightings, thresholds, and comparative benchmarks are entirely invisible. The output is binary: advancement or rejection. The rejection arrives, in the vast majority of cases, as an automated email containing zero explanatory specificity. Raghavan, Barocas, Kleinberg, and Levy (2020), in the most comprehensive audit of algorithmic hiring vendors conducted to date, evaluated the transparency and bias-mitigation claims of companies across the hiring technology landscape and found that not a single vendor provided candidates with meaningful algorithmic transparency regarding how their applications were scored or why they were rejected. The applicant, in other words, is rendered epistemically helpless: unable to see the mechanism, unable to understand the criteria, unable to contest the outcome, and unable to improve future performance on the basis of feedback that does not exist.

This epistemic helplessness is compounded by a withdrawal condition that has no parallel in most other algorithmic domains. A consumer who distrusts a social media platform's recommendation algorithm can, in principle, delete their account and seek information elsewhere. A borrower who suspects bias in an automated lending decision can apply to a different institution. But a job seeker confronting algorithmic screening at the major employers in their field cannot opt out of the systems without opting out of the labor market itself. If seventy-five percent of large employers filter applications through automated tracking systems, the applicant's only alternative to engaging with those systems is unemployment. Bureau of Labor Statistics data for 2023 reported a mean unemployment duration of 22.1 weeks, during which the average household draws down savings, accumulates debt, and experiences measurable increases in anxiety, depression, and physical illness (BLS, 2024; Brand, 2015). The coupling between the applicant and the algorithmic system is therefore not voluntary in any meaningful sense; it is compelled by the structure of the labor market and maintained by the absence of viable alternatives. The applicant must submit their data, must perform for the algorithm, and must accept the outcome—all without visibility into the process that determines whether they eat.

Existing scholarship has addressed important dimensions of this problem. Bogen and Rieke (2018), in their foundational Upturn report "Help Wanted," established a taxonomy of algorithmic hiring tools and mapped their equity implications across sourcing, screening, interviewing, and selection stages. Dastin (2018) reported for Reuters that Amazon abandoned an internal AI recruiting tool after engineers discovered it had taught itself to penalize resumes containing the word "women's" and to downgrade graduates of all-women's colleges, a pattern that emerged from training data reflecting a decade of male-dominated hiring outcomes. Fuller, Raman, and colleagues at Harvard Business School (2021) produced the landmark "Hidden Workers" study, estimating that automated screening criteria—rigid degree requirements, unexplained employment gaps, keyword-matching failures—filter out approximately 27 million workers who would likely perform successfully if hired. Kim (2017) provided early legal analysis of how Title VII's disparate impact framework applies (and fails to apply) to machine learning classifiers whose protected-class effects are statistically distributed rather than individually traceable. These contributions have generated important policy responses: New York City's Local Law 144, effective July 5, 2023, mandates annual bias audits for automated employment decision tools used within the city and requires that candidates be notified when such tools are used (NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, 2023). The European Union's AI Act, published as Regulation 2024/1689 on August 1, 2024, classifies recruitment and candidate-screening AI as high-risk under Annex III, Section 4, subjecting these systems to conformity assessment, transparency obligations, human oversight requirements, and the prospect of fines reaching 35 million euros or seven percent of global turnover—provisions that become enforceable on August 2, 2026. Article 5, already in force, prohibits the use of emotion recognition systems in workplaces, a provision that retroactively validates concerns about the facial analysis features HireVue employed on tens of millions of interviews before discontinuation. The Illinois Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act, enacted in 2020, requires employer disclosure and consent before AI analysis of video interviews. The EEOC and Department of Justice issued joint guidance in May 2022, updated in 2023, clarifying that algorithmic hiring tools are subject to Americans with Disabilities Act requirements and that employers bear responsibility for discriminatory outcomes regardless of whether a third-party vendor supplied the technology.

Yet for all their importance, these regulatory and scholarly interventions share a common structural limitation: they address the outputs of algorithmic hiring—bias, discrimination, disparate impact—without theorizing the architectural conditions that make those outputs predictable and recurring. Bias audits, as mandated by Local Law 144, measure whether a system produces disparate outcomes across demographic groups at a single point in time; they do not explain why the architecture of algorithmic hiring reliably generates conditions in which bias flourishes, conceals itself, and regenerates after correction. The EU AI Act's high-risk classification imposes transparency and oversight obligations on vendors, but it does not provide a framework for understanding why these obligations are necessary in this domain and not in others, or for predicting which specific configurations of hiring technology will produce the most severe harm patterns. Disparate impact litigation under Title VII can remedy individual instances of discrimination after the fact, but it cannot diagnose the systemic interaction between opacity, system responsiveness, and applicant vulnerability that causes harm to emerge in the first place. What is missing, in short, is a structural theory of the hiring void—an account of how the architectural features of algorithmic hiring interact to produce not merely bias but a self-reinforcing ecology of information asymmetry, adaptive concealment, and compelled participation that makes harm not incidental but endemic.

The Void Framework provides that structural theory. By identifying three jointly necessary conditions—opacity, responsiveness, and engaged observer coupling—and measuring their interaction through a composite Void Index and the derived Péclet number, the framework generates falsifiable predictions about which hiring technologies will produce the most severe harm, which configurations will resist regulatory correction, and which alternatives will suppress harm even within the same functional domain. The framework predicts, specifically, that the domain of algorithmic hiring will score at or near the theoretical maximum on the Void Index, that its Péclet number will exceed the Phase IV Pandemonium threshold, and that the resulting harm patterns will exhibit the characteristic drift cascade from agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation. It also predicts, crucially, that systems at the constraint pole—structured human interviews with standardized rubrics, blind skill-based assessments—will score low on the same index, confirming that the harm is architectural rather than inherent to the act of hiring.

This paper tests those predictions. Section II maps the Void Framework's core apparatus onto hiring-specific structures, scoring each of the three conditions and their modifiers for the domain as a whole and for individual platforms. Section III presents platform-level Void Model Cards for the highest-scoring systems—HireVue's pre-2021 video analysis, Pymetrics game-based assessment, and large-scale ATS keyword screening—alongside control-case assessments for structured interviews and blind skill tests. Section IV analyzes the drift cascade as it manifests in applicant behavior, employer rationalization, and vendor marketing language. Section V evaluates the regulatory landscape, including Local Law 144, the EU AI Act's Annex III provisions, the Illinois AIPA, and the EEOC's evolving guidance, through the framework's diagnostic lens, identifying where current regulation addresses void conditions and where it leaves architectural gaps. Section VI proposes design interventions calibrated to each void condition. Section VII states the kill condition—the empirical finding that would falsify the framework's application to this domain—and evaluates available evidence against it.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Algorithmic Hiring

The Void Framework, developed across preceding papers in this series (Papers 1, 3, 9), identifies three jointly necessary conditions that produce predictable harm patterns in sociotechnical systems: opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C). When all three conditions co-occur at sufficient intensity, the framework models the resulting dynamics through the Void Index, a 12-point composite scale (O: 0–3, R: 0–3, C: 0–3, with contextual modifiers contributing 0–3 additional points), and the Péclet number (Pe), a ratio measuring void intensity analogous to convective dominance over diffusive stabilization in transport physics. Systems exceeding Pe > 4 enter Phase IV of the demon lattice classification — Pandemonium — characterized by self-reinforcing opacity, drift cascade acceleration, and the systematic suppression of corrective feedback. Algorithmic hiring, as this section demonstrates, maps onto the framework's apparatus with unusual structural clarity. The domain scores an estimated 10/12 on the Void Index with Pe approximately 6.1, placing it deep within Phase IV and among the highest-scoring domains analyzed in this paper series.

**Opacity in algorithmic hiring.** Opacity in the Void Framework refers not merely to information absence but to structured, asymmetric unknowability — the condition in which one party to an interaction cannot access, audit, or reconstruct the decision logic governing outcomes that affect them. In algorithmic hiring, opacity operates across at least four nested layers. First, the algorithmic layer: resume screening models, candidate ranking systems, and AI interview scoring tools use proprietary machine learning architectures whose feature weightings are undisclosed. Raghavan, Barocas, Kleinberg, and Levy (2020), in their systematic audit of algorithmic hiring vendors presented at the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAT*), found that zero of the vendors examined provided candidates with meaningful algorithmic transparency — not partial transparency, but none whatsoever. Second, the criteria layer: even when employers specify job requirements, the mapping from those requirements to algorithmic filter thresholds is opaque to applicants. Fuller, Raman, and colleagues (2021) at Harvard Business School documented how automated screening criteria systematically reject candidates who possess the substantive qualifications for a role but fail on proxy variables — employment gaps, credential inflation, keyword absence — producing an estimated 27 million "hidden workers" filtered from consideration. Third, the outcome layer: rejected applicants typically receive automated emails with no specificity regarding the reason for rejection, the stage at which rejection occurred, or whether a human reviewed their application at all. Fourth, the comparative layer: applicants cannot observe how they were scored relative to other candidates, what distribution of scores the model produces, or whether the system exhibits disparate impact against protected characteristics. This four-layer structure produces what the framework terms compound opacity — each layer reinforcing the unknowability of the others, such that even sophisticated applicants cannot reverse-engineer the decision boundary.

**Responsiveness in algorithmic hiring.** The framework's responsiveness condition (R) captures the degree to which a system adapts its behavior based on the observer's engagement, creating the perception of interactivity while maintaining asymmetric control over outcomes. In hiring technology, responsiveness manifests through several mechanisms. Applicant Tracking Systems present dynamic interfaces — personalized job recommendations, application status portals, automated acknowledgment messages — that produce the phenomenology of dialogue while concealing the one-directional nature of the underlying evaluation. AI video interview platforms such as HireVue, which conducted over 70 million interviews prior to discontinuing facial analysis in January 2021 (HireVue company disclosures; Harwell 2019, Washington Post), exemplify responsiveness at its most structurally sophisticated: the system poses questions, records multimodal behavioral data (vocal prosody, lexical content, and formerly facial micro-expressions), and generates scores — creating an interaction that feels conversational while the candidate receives zero real-time feedback about their evaluation. Game-based psychometric tools such as Pymetrics, which Unilever deployed to screen over 250,000 candidates in its first year of adoption (Cutter 2019, Wall Street Journal), further intensify responsiveness by engaging applicants in interactive tasks whose assessment dimensions are undisclosed. The applicant plays a game; the game measures traits the applicant cannot identify; the score determines advancement. This is adaptive responsiveness in the framework's technical sense: the system modulates its outputs based on user inputs, sustaining engagement, while the evaluative mapping remains entirely hidden.

**Engaged observer coupling in algorithmic hiring.** The third condition, engaged observer coupling (C), measures the intensity of the observer's stake in and attachment to the system's outputs. The framework distinguishes coupling intensity along a continuum from discretionary engagement (low C) to existential dependency (high C). Algorithmic hiring occupies the extreme end of this continuum. Job applicants do not interact with hiring algorithms out of curiosity or entertainment; they do so because employment determines access to income, health insurance, housing stability, social identity, and psychological well-being. Bureau of Labor Statistics data consistently show that average unemployment duration in the United States exceeds 20 weeks (BLS 2023), with extended job search associated with significant financial depletion, psychological distress, and long-term earnings scarring (Kroft, Lange, and Notowidigdo 2013, American Economic Review). Crucially, the coupling is non-voluntary: if 75 percent or more of large employers use applicant tracking systems (Society for Human Resource Management 2022), an applicant cannot opt out of algorithmic screening without effectively withdrawing from the formal labor market. This forced coupling — engagement driven by necessity rather than choice, with livelihood-level consequences — represents the maximum intensity that the framework's C dimension can register.

**Void Index preview and phase classification.** The co-occurrence of compound opacity (O: 3/3), adaptive responsiveness (R: 3/3), and forced existential coupling (C: 3/3), augmented by domain-specific modifiers including regulatory fragmentation, vendor market concentration, and information asymmetry in employment law (modifiers: 1/3 under current partial regulation), yields a preliminary Void Index of 10/12. The corresponding Péclet estimate of 6.1 — derived from the ratio of opacity-driven convective harm propagation to transparency-driven corrective diffusion — places algorithmic hiring in Phase IV Pandemonium of the demon lattice classification. At this phase, the framework predicts active drift cascades: D1 agency attribution (applicants attributing human-like judgment to algorithmic scores), D2 boundary erosion (expanding use of hiring algorithms into domains such as personality inference and emotional stability that exceed validated psychometric boundaries), and D3 harm facilitation (systematic exclusion of protected classes at scale without individualized accountability). Subsequent sections test these predictions against empirical evidence. Critically, structured human interviews (Void Index 4/12) and blind skill-based assessments (Void Index 2/12) serve as within-domain control cases, anchoring the constraint pole and confirming that the framework's harm predictions are specific to the void architecture rather than inherent to the act of employment selection itself.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

Opacity is the foundational condition of the Void Framework: the degree to which a system's decision-relevant processes, criteria, and internal states are invisible to the parties most affected by its outputs. In algorithmic hiring, opacity does not merely exist as an incidental feature of complex technology. It is architecturally produced at every layer of the system, from data ingestion through model inference to outcome communication, and it is actively maintained through legal instruments, interface design, and market incentives. The hiring domain scores 3/3 on the Void Index opacity dimension — the theoretical maximum — because no substantive information about algorithmic decision criteria, feature weightings, comparison sets, or rejection rationale reaches applicants at any stage of the process for the vast majority of deployed systems. This section enumerates the specific opacity mechanisms, provides primary-source evidence for each, and identifies the structural reasons opacity in this domain is self-reinforcing rather than contingent.

### III.A. Taxonomizing Opacity: Four Layers

Burrell (2016, *Big Data & Society*) distinguished three forms of opacity in algorithmic systems: opacity as intentional corporate secrecy, opacity as technical illiteracy among affected populations, and opacity as the intrinsic interpretive difficulty of machine learning models. Algorithmic hiring instantiates all three simultaneously and adds a fourth: communicative opacity, wherein the system's outputs are deliberately stripped of explanatory content before reaching the applicant. The Void Framework treats these as compounding rather than independent: each layer makes the others more difficult to penetrate, and the composite produces an opacity structure that resists remediation from any single intervention point.

**Layer 1: Corporate secrecy.** Applicant tracking systems and AI screening tools treat their ranking algorithms as proprietary trade secrets. Raghavan, Barocas, Kleinberg, and Levy (2020, *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency*) conducted the most systematic audit to date of algorithmic hiring vendors' transparency practices. Examining nineteen vendors offering automated candidate assessment — including resume screeners, video interview analyzers, and game-based psychometric tools — they found that zero vendors provided candidates with meaningful information about the algorithmic criteria determining their scores or rankings. Vendors' public-facing descriptions relied on vague appeals to "artificial intelligence" and "validated assessments" without disclosing feature sets, model architectures, or weighting schemes. Several vendors claimed that their models detected traits such as "cognitive ability," "emotional intelligence," or "cultural fit," but none disclosed the operationalization of these constructs in sufficient detail to permit independent evaluation. This is not an absence of transparency due to oversight; it is an active posture of concealment defended through trade secret doctrine.

The legal architecture reinforces this secrecy. In *HireVue v. EPIC* (2019), the Electronic Privacy Information Center filed a complaint with the Federal Trade Commission alleging that HireVue's AI video interview system constituted an unfair and deceptive practice because candidates could not understand or contest its facial analysis scoring. HireVue's response, and the broader industry's defense, relied on the argument that algorithmic criteria constitute proprietary intellectual property whose disclosure would undermine competitive advantage. The Uniform Trade Secrets Act, adopted in some form by 48 U.S. states, provides the statutory foundation for this posture. The result is a legal regime that actively protects the opacity condition the Void Framework identifies as harm-generative.

**Layer 2: Technical complexity.** Even where vendors hypothetically disclosed their model specifications, the affected population — job applicants — would face severe interpretive barriers. Resume screening models deployed at scale increasingly rely on natural language processing architectures, including transformer-based models fine-tuned on historical hiring data, whose decision boundaries are not reducible to human-legible rules. Sánchez-Monedero, Dencik, and Edwards (2020, *Big Data & Society*) documented that the shift from keyword-matching ATS systems to machine-learning-based candidate ranking introduced a qualitative change in opacity: whereas an applicant might previously have intuited that specific keywords triggered advancement past an ATS filter, machine learning models weight high-dimensional feature interactions that resist such reverse-engineering. The technical opacity is genuine, not merely a rhetorical shield. However, the Void Framework notes that technical complexity becomes a void-producing mechanism specifically because it compounds rather than substitutes for corporate secrecy: vendors invoke model complexity as justification for withholding even the partial explanations that would be technically feasible.

**Layer 3: Informational asymmetry at the interface.** The design of applicant-facing interfaces systematically withholds decision-relevant information. Candidates submitting applications through major ATS platforms — Workday, Oracle Taleo, SAP SuccessFactors, Greenhouse, iCIMS, Lever — encounter upload fields, structured data entry forms, and confirmation screens. They do not encounter any disclosure of how their data will be processed, what algorithmic scoring will occur, whether their resume will be parsed and re-ranked by an ML model, or what threshold they must exceed for human review. Bogen and Rieke (2018, Upturn, *Help Wanted: An Examination of Hiring Algorithms, Equity, and Bias*) documented this interface-level opacity across the hiring technology stack, noting that applicants typically cannot determine whether a human being ever views their materials. The informational environment presented to the applicant is structurally equivalent to submitting documents into a void: materials go in, a binary outcome eventually emerges, and the intervening process is entirely invisible.

This interface opacity extends to the AI video interview context with particular intensity. When HireVue conducted over 70 million interviews by its own reporting — including, prior to January 2021, interviews scored in part on facial expression analysis — candidates were not informed which specific behaviors, vocal characteristics, or facial movements the system weighted (Harwell, *Washington Post*, November 6, 2019). Applicants reported in survey research conducted by Langer, König, and Papathanasiou (2019, *Journal of Personnel Psychology*) that AI video interviews produced significantly lower perceptions of procedural justice, interpersonal warmth, and opportunity for self-presentation compared to human-conducted interviews. The opacity was experienced not as a neutral absence of information but as an aversive phenomenological condition — the sense of performing for an evaluator whose criteria are unknowable.

**Layer 4: Communicative opacity (output stripping).** The most consequential opacity layer operates at the output stage. The overwhelming majority of algorithmic rejections in hiring are communicated through automated emails containing no substantive explanation. A typical rejection message reads: "After careful consideration, we have decided to move forward with other candidates whose qualifications more closely match our current needs." This language is generated by template engines within ATS platforms and contains zero information about why the specific candidate was rejected, what criteria were applied, or whether an algorithm rather than a human made the determination. Li, Raymond, and Bergman (2020, *Personnel Psychology*) found that 75% of job applicants reported receiving no feedback whatsoever following rejection, and among those who did receive communication, fewer than 5% received information specific enough to be actionable. The system's opacity is thus maintained not only at the input and processing stages but at the output stage, ensuring that the applicant cannot reconstruct the decision logic even retrospectively.

### III.B. Quantitative Evidence of Opacity Intensity

Several empirical findings permit calibration of opacity intensity in this domain relative to other algorithmic systems.

First, the Raghavan et al. (2020) vendor audit provides a clean quantitative benchmark: of nineteen vendors examined, zero — 0% — provided candidates with algorithmic transparency meeting even minimal standards of explanation. This is not a gradient; it is a floor. The authors noted that even the vendors' communications to employer-clients often lacked technical specificity about model mechanisms, creating nested opacity in which neither the affected party (the applicant) nor the deploying party (the employer) fully understands the system's decision process.

Second, the Fuller, Raman, et al. (2021, Harvard Business School) "Hidden Workers" report estimated that automated screening criteria rejected 27 million qualified workers in the United States alone — workers whom human reviewers, when presented with the same applications, judged to be viable candidates. This figure is derived from employer surveys (n = 2,250 executives across multiple sectors) and applicant surveys (n = 8,720) and represents a quantification of opacity's downstream effects: because applicants cannot see or contest the screening criteria, and because the criteria themselves embed rigid filters (employment gaps exceeding six months, absence of specific degree types, keyword mismatches), qualified candidates are filtered out at rates that neither the applicants nor, frequently, the employers themselves recognize.

Third, New York City's Local Law 144, which took effect on July 5, 2023, provides a natural experiment in opacity measurement. The law requires employers using automated employment decision tools (AEDTs) in New York City to commission annual independent bias audits and to notify candidates that an AEDT is being used. The implementation revealed the depth of pre-existing opacity: in the first year of compliance, reporting by Engler (2023, *Brookings Institution*) and Albert (2023, *Bloomberg Law*) documented that many employers were uncertain whether their existing systems qualified as AEDTs, that vendors resisted providing sufficient technical access for meaningful audits, and that the required candidate notifications were implemented as brief disclaimers buried in application portals. The law exposed, rather than resolved, the opacity condition — demonstrating that opacity in this domain is structurally embedded rather than superficially correctable.

Fourth, the EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), published August 1, 2024, classifies recruitment and candidate screening AI as high-risk under Annex III, Section 4, requiring transparency obligations, conformity assessments, human oversight mechanisms, and technical documentation to take effect by August 2, 2026. Article 5, already in force, bans emotion recognition systems in workplaces. The legislative determination that hiring AI constitutes high-risk — adopted through ordinary legislative procedure with trilogue agreement across the European Parliament, Council, and Commission — represents an institutional recognition of the opacity condition's severity. The Act's recitals explicitly reference the informational asymmetry between deployers and affected persons as a motivating concern.

### III.C. Opacity as Architecturally Self-Reinforcing

The Void Framework distinguishes between contingent opacity (which can be remediated through disclosure mandates without restructuring the system) and architectural opacity (which is produced by the system's structure and reproduced by its operation). Algorithmic hiring exhibits architectural opacity because the mechanisms sustaining concealment are mutually reinforcing.

Trade secret protection provides the legal basis for withholding algorithmic criteria. Technical complexity provides the epistemic justification for claiming that disclosure would be meaningless. Interface design ensures that even where partial transparency is mandated (as under Local Law 144), the information reaches applicants in forms too attenuated to be actionable. Communicative stripping at the output stage ensures that rejected candidates cannot accumulate evidence of systematic patterns. And the competitive dynamics of the vendor market ensure that any individual vendor offering genuine transparency would expose its model to gaming by applicants and reverse-engineering by competitors, creating a collective action problem that sustains opacity as an industry equilibrium.

Kim (2017, *AI and Hiring: A Framework for Civil Rights Enforcement*, published as a working paper and later developed in *Washington Law Review*) argued that the trade secret defense is particularly corrosive in the employment context because Title VII's disparate impact framework requires plaintiffs to identify the specific employment practice producing discriminatory outcomes — a requirement that algorithmic opacity renders structurally impossible to satisfy. The opacity condition thus insulates potentially discriminatory systems from the legal mechanism designed to detect and remedy discrimination. This is the Void Framework's core prediction for the opacity dimension: that high-opacity systems do not merely obscure harm but actively suppress the feedback mechanisms through which harm could be detected, attributed, and corrected.

### III.D. Hostile Witness Evidence

The opacity assessment is strengthened by evidence from actors with incentives to minimize opacity concerns. HireVue's own January 2021 announcement that it would discontinue facial analysis in its video interview scoring — described by the company as a proactive decision reflecting its commitment to "leading the way in ethical AI" — implicitly acknowledged that the prior system's opacity had been producing unjustifiable risk. The company had used facial analysis on millions of candidates without disclosing the specific facial features or expressions being scored, and the decision to abandon the capability followed sustained criticism from AI ethics researchers (Rhue, 2019, unpublished working paper; Wilson et al., 2021, *AI and Ethics*) and the EPIC FTC complaint. That HireVue chose to eliminate the feature rather than make it transparent suggests that transparency would have revealed indefensible scoring mechanisms — a concession that opacity was not incidental but functional.

Similarly, Amazon's 2018 abandonment of its internal AI recruiting tool, reported by Dastin (Reuters, October 10, 2018), revealed that the system had been trained on ten years of predominantly male resumes and had learned to penalize indicators of female gender, including the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club captain"). The system's gender bias was discovered internally — not by applicants, not by regulators, and not by auditors, but by Amazon's own engineers after the system had been in use. This timeline confirms the Void Framework's prediction: under conditions of extreme opacity, harm detection depends entirely on insider disclosure, because the affected population has no informational access to the decision process.

### III.E. The Constraint Pole: What Low Opacity Looks Like

The Void Framework requires identification of control cases at the constraint pole — entities scoring low on opacity within the same functional domain — to confirm that the framework's predictions are specific to the three conditions rather than artifacts of the domain itself.

Structured interviews conducted according to validated protocols (Schmidt and Hunter, 1998, *Psychological Bulletin*; Campion, Palmer, and Campion, 1997, *Personnel Psychology*) represent a low-opacity hiring mechanism. In a structured interview, the candidate knows the questions in advance or can anticipate them from published rubrics; the evaluation criteria are standardized and can be communicated; and the interviewer's assessment is legible to the candidate in real time through verbal and nonverbal feedback. Structured interviews score 1/3 on opacity: some evaluative criteria remain implicit, but the process is fundamentally visible to the candidate.

Blind skill assessments — such as work-sample tests in which candidates complete job-relevant tasks evaluated against objective benchmarks — represent near-zero opacity. The candidate knows what they are being asked to do, knows the evaluation criteria (produce functional code, write a coherent brief, solve the engineering problem), and can self-assess their performance. Applied, a UK-based platform implementing anonymized skill-based hiring, reports that its process reduces demographic hiring gaps while increasing candidate-reported fairness perceptions (Applied, 2021, company research report; Bohnet, 2016, *What Works: Gender Equality by Design*, Harvard University Press). These systems score 0/3 on opacity.

The contrast is stark. Where opacity is architecturally maximal — proprietary ATS screening, AI video analysis, game-based psychometrics with undisclosed scoring rubrics — the Void Framework predicts harm accumulation through the drift cascade. Where opacity is structurally minimized — structured interviews, blind skill tests, transparent rubrics — the same functional task (evaluating candidates for employment) operates at the constraint pole. The opacity condition is not an inevitable feature of hiring; it is a design choice with measurable consequences.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

The Void Framework defines responsiveness (R) as the degree to which a system modifies its behavior in reaction to observer attention, scrutiny, or engagement. In high-responsiveness systems, the outputs an observer receives are not fixed properties of the system but are instead functions of the observer's own inputs, behaviors, and characteristics. Responsiveness is the condition that transforms a static black box into a dynamic adversary — one that presents different faces to different observers, adapts to circumvention attempts, and generates feedback loops that amplify initial asymmetries. In algorithmic hiring, responsiveness operates through at least four distinct mechanisms: adaptive screening that reshapes itself in response to applicant behavior, feedback loops that encode historical bias into future decisions, platform-level personalization that segments candidates before they even apply, and a perverse metacognitive dynamic in which the system's responsiveness to employer preferences actively degrades its responsiveness to applicant needs. The domain scores 3/3 on the responsiveness dimension, the theoretical maximum.

### A. Adaptive Screening and the Moving Target Problem

The most fundamental form of responsiveness in algorithmic hiring is the system's capacity to recalibrate its selection criteria in response to the applicant pool itself. Machine learning models deployed for resume screening do not apply static rules; they learn from patterns in training data and, in many implementations, continue to update as new hiring outcomes are recorded. Amazon's internal AI recruiting tool, developed beginning in 2014 and abandoned in 2018, provides the canonical illustration. Reuters reported in October 2018 that the system had been trained on a decade of hiring data that reflected the company's historical preference for male candidates, particularly in technical roles. The model penalized resumes containing the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club captain") and systematically downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges (Dastin, 2018, Reuters). When Amazon's engineers attempted to remediate the gender bias by removing explicitly gendered terms, the model found proxy variables — extracurricular activities, verb choices, formatting patterns correlated with gender in the training corpus — and continued to produce discriminatory rankings. The system was responsive in the precise sense the Void Framework identifies as dangerous: it adapted to correction attempts by finding alternative pathways to the same biased outcome.

This adaptive quality is not unique to Amazon's abandoned prototype. Bogen and Rieke (2018) documented that resume screening tools marketed by vendors including Textio, Entelo, and HiringSolved explicitly advertise their capacity to learn from employer-specific hiring patterns, meaning that if an employer has historically hired candidates from certain universities, with certain keywords, or with certain career trajectories, the algorithm will weight those features more heavily in future screening. The system becomes more responsive to the employer's revealed preferences over time, regardless of whether those preferences reflect job-relevant criteria or historical discrimination. Chen et al. (2023, published in *Management Science*) demonstrated through an audit of a major online hiring platform that algorithmic recommendations systematically favored candidates whose profiles matched the demographic characteristics of previously successful hires within the same firm, producing a self-reinforcing homogeneity effect that the authors termed "algorithmic monoculture." Li, Raymond, and Bergman (2020, working paper, National Bureau of Economic Research) found that firms using algorithmic screening exhibited measurably less demographic diversity in their interview pools compared to firms using structured human review, controlling for industry, firm size, and applicant pool composition.

The moving target problem is especially pernicious because applicants cannot observe the adaptation in real time. A resume format that successfully passes screening in January may be filtered in March if the model has been retrained on intervening hiring data. Career coaching services that advise applicants to optimize their resumes for ATS systems — by including specific keywords, avoiding graphics, and formatting section headers in particular ways — are engaged in an arms race against a responsive adversary whose current state is unknown. Fuller et al. (2021), in the Harvard Business School "Hidden Workers" report, found that 88% of employers surveyed acknowledged that their ATS systems rejected qualified candidates who did not match the rigid, algorithmically enforced criteria, and that these criteria shifted over time as models were updated, creating what the authors described as a "moving goalpost" effect that disproportionately excluded workers with non-linear career histories, employment gaps, or non-traditional credentials.

### B. Feedback Loops and Bias Amplification

Responsiveness in algorithmic hiring generates feedback loops of a specific and well-documented type: the system's outputs become its future inputs, and any initial bias in the training data is amplified through successive cycles of deployment. Kleinberg and Raghavan (2018, *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Economics and Computation*) formalized this dynamic, demonstrating that even a screening algorithm with a small initial bias will, over multiple hiring cycles, produce increasingly skewed training data as the outcomes of biased selections are fed back into the model as positive examples. The mechanism is straightforward: if the algorithm slightly favors candidates of type A over equally qualified candidates of type B, then the pool of successful hires will be disproportionately composed of type A candidates, and the next iteration of the model — trained on "successful" hires — will weight type A features even more heavily.

Empirical evidence confirms that this theoretical feedback dynamic operates in practice. Cowgill (2019, Columbia Business School working paper) studied a machine learning hiring algorithm deployed at a large technology firm and found that the algorithm's predictions of candidate quality were strongly correlated with the demographic profile of the firm's existing workforce, even when the model was not given explicit demographic variables. The model inferred demographic proxies from features such as commute distance, name-associated characteristics, and university attended, and its predictions reproduced the firm's existing demographic composition with remarkable fidelity. When the firm expanded the candidate pool to include applicants from non-traditional backgrounds, the model scored them lower despite equivalent or superior subsequent job performance, revealing that the algorithm's "quality" metric was in part a measure of demographic similarity to incumbents.

The feedback loop extends beyond individual firm boundaries when hiring platforms share data across clients. Platforms such as HireVue and Pymetrics train their models on aggregated data from multiple employers, meaning that industry-wide hiring patterns — including industry-wide biases — become embedded in models that are then deployed across the industry. Raghavan et al. (2020) noted that none of the vendors they audited disclosed the composition of their training datasets or the degree to which cross-client data aggregation might embed sector-level demographic patterns into ostensibly objective scoring algorithms. Wilson et al. (2021, *Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society*) demonstrated that commercial facial analysis systems — of the type HireVue used to score video interviews prior to January 2021 — exhibited error rates that varied by a factor of 10 to 100 across demographic groups, with the highest error rates for darker-skinned women. These differential error rates constitute a form of responsiveness: the system literally responds differently to different faces, and the responses it produces are fed back into hiring decisions that shape the training data for future model iterations.

### C. Platform-Level Personalization and Pre-Application Sorting

Responsiveness in algorithmic hiring begins before the applicant submits a resume. Job advertisement platforms, most prominently LinkedIn, Facebook (Meta), and Indeed, use algorithmic targeting to determine which candidates see which job postings. This pre-application sorting constitutes a powerful form of responsiveness because it shapes the applicant pool before any screening occurs, and it does so in response to the candidate's observed behavior, inferred characteristics, and the employer's specified (or algorithmically inferred) preferences.

Lambrecht and Tucker (2019, *Management Science*) conducted a field experiment on Facebook's advertising platform and found that STEM job advertisements designed to be gender-neutral were disproportionately shown to men, not because the advertiser targeted men, but because the platform's cost-optimization algorithm determined that women's attention was more expensive to acquire (due to higher competition from other advertisers targeting women) and therefore allocated the ad budget toward male users. The result was a gender-skewed applicant pool that appeared organic but was in fact produced by the platform's responsive optimization. Ali et al. (2019, *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Computer-Supported Cooperative Work*) replicated this finding and demonstrated that Facebook's ad delivery algorithm produced skewed audiences along both racial and gender lines even when advertisers specified no demographic targeting, because the algorithm optimized for "relevance" using behavioral proxies that correlated with protected characteristics.

LinkedIn's Recruiter product, which generated approximately $7.3 billion in revenue for Microsoft in FY2024, offers employers granular filtering and ranking of candidates based on profile data, activity patterns, and algorithmically inferred "fit" scores. Hannák et al. (2017, *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Computer-Supported Cooperative Work*) audited online freelance marketplaces and found that platform ranking algorithms produced significant disparities in visibility across demographic groups, with consequences for who received work and who remained invisible. While their study focused on TaskRabbit and Fiverr rather than LinkedIn specifically, the mechanism — algorithmic ranking responding to platform-level behavioral data to produce differential visibility — generalizes to any platform where employers search for and are shown candidate profiles.

The pre-application sorting mechanism is especially significant for the Void Framework because it operates entirely outside the applicant's awareness. A candidate who never sees a job posting cannot apply for it, and the candidate has no way of knowing that the posting existed, that they were excluded from seeing it, or that the exclusion resulted from an algorithmic decision responsive to their inferred characteristics. This is responsiveness without any observable feedback: the system adapts to the candidate, but the candidate cannot observe the adaptation.

### D. Asymmetric Responsiveness: Employer Optimization versus Applicant Opacity

Perhaps the most structurally revealing feature of responsiveness in algorithmic hiring is its radical asymmetry. The system is maximally responsive to the employer and minimally responsive to the applicant. Employers receive dashboards, analytics, A/B testing capabilities, funnel metrics, time-to-hire optimization, and real-time adjustment of screening parameters. iCIMS, Greenhouse, Lever, and Workday all provide employer-facing analytics showing conversion rates at each funnel stage, demographic breakdowns of the applicant pool (to the extent permitted by self-reported data), and algorithmic recommendations for adjusting job posting language, screening criteria, or outreach strategies. The system responds to the employer's attention with increasing granularity and actionable intelligence.

The applicant, by contrast, receives almost nothing. Bogen and Rieke (2018) documented that the standard applicant experience in algorithmic hiring consists of submitting a resume into an ATS, receiving an automated acknowledgment, and then — in the majority of cases — receiving either no response at all or a form rejection email with no specificity. Candidate experience surveys by Talent Board (the organization that administers the annual Candidate Experience Research Awards) have consistently found that the majority of applicants report receiving no feedback whatsoever on why they were rejected, with the 2022 survey reporting that 65% of candidates who were rejected received no communication beyond the initial automated acknowledgment (Talent Board, 2022 CandE Benchmark Research Report). The system is responsive to the employer's desire to optimize, but it is deliberately unresponsive to the applicant's need for information. This asymmetric responsiveness is not an incidental design flaw; it reflects the economic structure of the market. The employer is the paying customer. The applicant is the product being sorted.

Sanchez-Monedero, Dencik, and Edwards (2020, *Big Data & Society*) analyzed the design affordances of major ATS platforms and concluded that the systems were architecturally structured to maximize employer control and minimize applicant agency, describing a "fundamental misalignment" in which the tools' responsiveness to employer optimization goals actively undermined applicants' ability to understand, contest, or navigate the selection process. The authors observed that features such as candidate ranking, automated rejection, and "knockout question" filters — all of which adapt in response to employer configuration — had no applicant-facing analogues. Candidates could not see their ranking, could not see which question disqualified them, and could not see how changing a single answer would have altered their outcome.

### E. Responsiveness Score: 3/3

Algorithmic hiring scores at the maximum on the responsiveness dimension. The systems actively adapt to inputs (applicant data, employer preferences, hiring outcomes, platform behavioral data), produce different outputs for different observers (personalized job ad delivery, differential screening thresholds across firms), generate self-reinforcing feedback loops (biased training data producing biased models producing biased outcomes that become the next generation's training data), and maintain radical asymmetry in which responsiveness to employer attention is maximized while responsiveness to applicant attention is suppressed. The adaptive screening mechanism ensures that even static applicant behavior encounters a non-static decision surface; the feedback loops ensure that initial biases compound over time; the pre-application sorting ensures that responsiveness operates before the applicant even enters the visible funnel; and the asymmetric design ensures that the information generated by the system's responsiveness flows exclusively to the party with existing power.

This maximal responsiveness score, combined with the maximal opacity documented in the preceding section, produces the conditions for Phase IV Pandemonium. The system is simultaneously invisible to the applicant and adaptive to the applicant's data — it sees you and adjusts, but you cannot see it or its adjustments. When the third condition, the engaged observer coupling that forces continued participation despite harm, is added, the full void architecture comes into view.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

The third condition of the Void Framework — engaged observer attention and coupling — assumes its most consequential form in algorithmic hiring because the system mediates access to livelihood itself. Unlike consumer platforms where coupling develops through convenience or social reinforcement, the hiring domain produces coupling through existential necessity: the applicant cannot disengage from the system without forfeiting participation in the formal labor market. This section analyzes the mechanisms through which coupling forms, escalates, and entrenches in algorithmic hiring, drawing on labor economics, organizational psychology, and behavioral evidence to establish that applicant-system coupling in this domain reaches the maximum score of C = 3 on the Void Index.

### V.A. The Structural Basis of Coupling: Livelihood Dependency

The foundational coupling mechanism in algorithmic hiring is not behavioral but structural. Employment is the primary means by which adults in industrialized economies secure income, health insurance, housing stability, and social identity. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that in 2023, the median duration of unemployment in the United States was 8.7 weeks, with 19.6 percent of unemployed workers experiencing joblessness exceeding 27 weeks (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024). Brand (2015), writing in the *Annual Review of Sociology*, synthesized decades of evidence showing that job loss produces lasting negative effects on earnings trajectories, physical health, mental health, and mortality risk, with displaced workers earning 15 to 20 percent less than continuously employed counterparts even fifteen years after displacement. Wanberg (2012), in a comprehensive review published in the *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*, documented that unemployment generates clinically significant increases in anxiety, depression, and loss of self-efficacy, with severity correlating to duration and perceived lack of control over re-employment prospects.

These findings establish that the applicant's coupling to the hiring system is not a preference but a survival imperative. When the Society for Human Resource Management estimated that more than 75 percent of large employers use some form of applicant tracking system (SHRM, 2022), and when a single platform such as iCIMS processes approximately 75 million applications per year (iCIMS, 2022), the implication is that most job seekers in knowledge-economy roles cannot avoid interaction with algorithmic gatekeeping without restricting themselves to informal labor markets. The applicant does not choose to couple with the system; the system has been positioned as the obligatory passage point between unemployment and employment. In the Void Framework's terminology, this constitutes forced coupling at maximum intensity — the observer cannot exit the observation relationship without accepting catastrophic personal consequences.

### V.B. Switching Costs and Platform Lock-In

Coupling in algorithmic hiring is compounded by switching costs that accumulate across three dimensions: platform-specific investment, data asymmetry, and network effects.

First, applicants invest substantial time in platform-specific profiles. LinkedIn's 1 billion members as of late 2024 (Microsoft FY2024 Annual Report) have each constructed a professional identity artifact — endorsements, connection networks, recommendation letters, employment histories, skill badges — that is non-transferable to competing platforms. Gee (2019), studying LinkedIn data in a paper published in *Management Science*, demonstrated that the platform's recommendation algorithms create self-reinforcing advantages for users who maintain active, optimized profiles, generating differential visibility that rewards continued platform investment. The cost of abandoning a mature LinkedIn profile and rebuilding professional visibility elsewhere functions as a lock-in mechanism structurally analogous to the switching costs documented by Farrell and Klemperer (2007) in their *Handbook of Industrial Organization* chapter on network effects and switching costs.

Second, applicant tracking systems create data asymmetries that deepen coupling. When an applicant submits materials to Workday, Taleo, or Greenhouse, the employer retains and processes that data through proprietary algorithms, while the applicant receives at most a binary outcome — advancement or rejection — with no access to the scoring, ranking, or filtering criteria applied to their submission. Raghavan et al. (2020), in their audit presented at the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency, found that zero of the vendors examined provided meaningful algorithmic transparency to candidates regarding how their applications were evaluated. This informational asymmetry means the applicant cannot learn from the system's responses in any systematic way, yet must continue submitting data to it. The coupling intensifies precisely because the opacity described in Section III prevents the rational adaptation that might otherwise reduce the observer's dependence on the system.

Third, network effects in hiring platforms create concentration dynamics that foreclose alternatives. Hiring managers increasingly rely on LinkedIn Recruiter, whose Talent Solutions segment generated approximately $7.3 billion in Microsoft's FY2024 revenue (Microsoft, 2024), as the default sourcing channel. Applicants who are not present on the platform are functionally invisible to recruiters who use it as their primary pipeline. This creates a coupling loop: because recruiters use the platform, applicants must be on the platform; because applicants are on the platform, recruiters concentrate their sourcing there. Rochet and Tirole (2003), in their foundational *RAND Journal of Economics* paper on two-sided markets, formalized how such dynamics produce winner-take-most equilibria that make platform departure individually irrational even when collective welfare might be served by diversification.

### V.C. Variable-Ratio Reinforcement and Behavioral Capture

The hiring application process exhibits structural features of variable-ratio reinforcement schedules, which behavioral psychology identifies as the most resistant to extinction. Ferster and Skinner (1957), in *Schedules of Reinforcement*, established that organisms maintain behavior at highest rates when rewards arrive unpredictably and at variable intervals. In algorithmic hiring, the applicant submits applications into a system that produces outcomes — interviews, callbacks, offers — at irregular and unpredictable rates. The applicant cannot predict which application will succeed, cannot identify what distinguished a successful from an unsuccessful submission, and receives rejection (or more commonly, silence) as the overwhelming modal outcome.

Kanfer, Wanberg, and Kantrowitz (2001), in a meta-analysis published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology* covering 73 studies and over 14,000 job seekers, found that job search intensity is sustained not by consistent reinforcement but by intermittent positive signals — a callback among dozens of rejections, an interview among hundreds of applications. The behavioral profile matches what the Void Framework identifies as the hallmark of high-coupling systems: the observer continues engaging not because outcomes are favorable but because the possibility of a favorable outcome cannot be abandoned. Van Hooft, Wanberg, and Van Hoye (2013), writing in the *International Journal of Management Reviews*, further documented that job seekers develop increasingly compulsive application behaviors during extended searches, submitting higher volumes of less-targeted applications as desperation increases — a pattern consistent with the escalation dynamics predicted by variable-ratio reinforcement under high-stakes coupling.

The digital architecture of modern hiring platforms amplifies these dynamics. Indeed.com's "Easy Apply" function, LinkedIn's one-click application feature, and Greenhouse's streamlined submission interfaces reduce the friction of each individual application, enabling applicants to submit large volumes rapidly. Faberman and Kudlyak (2019), in a Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco working paper analyzing application-level data, found that the average job seeker sends dozens of applications per month, with many sending over one hundred, yet conversion rates from application to interview remain in low single-digit percentages. The combination of low per-application cost and low per-application success rate produces the classic variable-ratio profile: high-volume behavior sustained by unpredictable, intermittent reward.

### V.D. Longitudinal Coupling Escalation

The Void Framework predicts that coupling intensifies over time as the observer accumulates sunk costs and as the consequences of disengagement grow more severe. Longitudinal evidence from job search research confirms this prediction. Krueger and Mueller (2011), in a study published in the *American Economic Review*, tracked unemployed workers over time using daily time-use diaries and found that job search effort initially increases, then declines as discouragement sets in, but that the psychological coupling to the search process — measured by stress, rumination, and subjective urgency — continues to escalate even as behavioral engagement fluctuates. The observer becomes more coupled to the void even when they interact with it less frequently, because the existential stakes compound with each week of continued unemployment.

Platform-level coupling escalation operates through a distinct mechanism: profile optimization spirals. As applicants receive rejections or silence, they revise resumes, add certifications, rewrite summaries, and adjust keyword strategies in attempts to penetrate opaque screening algorithms. Fuller et al. (2021), in the Harvard Business School "Hidden Workers" report, documented that job seekers invest increasing effort in reverse-engineering ATS requirements, including purchasing resume optimization services, using keyword-scanning tools, and reformatting documents to comply with parsing algorithms they cannot see. This investment deepens coupling to the specific platforms and norms of algorithmic hiring, making the applicant more dependent on the very systems that have repeatedly rejected them. The pattern mirrors what the Void Framework terms the drift cascade's second stage (D2, boundary erosion): the applicant progressively adapts their self-presentation, professional identity, and daily time allocation to the system's perceived demands, eroding the boundary between the person and the platform.

### V.E. Coupling Score Assignment: C = 3

The domain receives the maximum engagement/coupling score of C = 3 for four converging reasons. First, engagement is functionally non-voluntary: the applicant cannot exit the system without forfeiting access to formal employment. Second, switching costs across platforms are high and multi-dimensional, encompassing sunk investments, data lock-in, and network effects. Third, the temporal reinforcement schedule matches the variable-ratio pattern most resistant to behavioral extinction, maintaining application behavior despite overwhelmingly negative outcomes. Fourth, coupling escalates over time through both psychological intensification and progressive identity adaptation to platform norms.

The contrast with control cases confirms the scoring. Structured human interviews conducted through direct employer contact (scored C = 1 in the domain control analysis) involve a single bounded interaction with a known evaluator, no platform dependency, and clear temporal termination. Blind skill assessments (scored C = 0) involve a single test episode with no ongoing relationship or identity investment. The difference between these bounded interactions and the longitudinal, identity-consuming, existentially forced coupling of algorithmic hiring platforms illustrates the full range of the coupling dimension within a single functional domain.

At C = 3, combined with the opacity score of O = 3 established in Section III and the responsiveness score of R = 3 established in Section IV, the core Void Index for algorithmic hiring reaches 9 out of 9 on the three primary conditions before modifier assessment. This score, the highest possible on the primary dimensions, reflects a domain in which an opaque, adaptive system has captured the attention and dependency of observers who cannot look away because their survival depends on the outcome.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in Algorithmic Hiring and HR Technology

The drift cascade describes the predictable sequence through which void conditions produce escalating harm: D1 (agency attribution), in which observers attribute autonomous agency or quasi-intentional judgment to the opaque system; D2 (boundary erosion), in which the perceived authority of the system dissolves pre-existing normative, legal, and procedural boundaries that once constrained decision-making; and D3 (harm facilitation), in which the degraded boundary environment enables specific, documentable injuries that would have been blocked or attenuated under constraint-pole conditions. In algorithmic hiring, this cascade operates with unusual clarity because the domain couples extreme opacity with existential stakes—the applicant's access to livelihood—and because the historical record contains a detailed case study, Amazon's abandoned AI recruiting tool, that traces the complete D1→D2→D3 trajectory with dated milestones and quantified outcomes.

### D1: Agency Attribution — "The Algorithm Knows Best"

The first stage of the drift cascade manifests when hiring managers, HR departments, and organizational leadership begin treating algorithmic outputs not as probabilistic estimates requiring human judgment but as authoritative determinations reflecting superior insight. This attribution is linguistically detectable. Bogen and Rieke (2018) documented that vendor marketing materials for AI hiring tools systematically employed L2 and L3 vocabulary—systems that "understand" candidates, "identify" top talent, and "predict" job performance—framing statistical pattern-matching as cognitive evaluation. Raghavan et al. (2020) found that of the vendors examined, none provided candidates with meaningful algorithmic transparency, while simultaneously marketing their tools to employers as possessing evaluative capacities exceeding those of human recruiters. The asymmetry is structurally significant: the system is presented to the buyer as an expert agent and to the subject as an impenetrable wall.

Empirical evidence confirms that this agency attribution alters human behavior in precisely the manner the framework predicts. Langer et al. (2021), publishing in the *Journal of Business and Psychology*, found that applicants perceived algorithmic hiring decisions as less fair, less transparent, and less trustworthy than equivalent human decisions, yet hiring managers perceived algorithmic recommendations as more objective and less biased than their own judgments. This divergent perception is the signature of D1: the decision-maker attributes epistemic authority to the opaque system, while the decision-subject experiences only the opacity. Gonzalez et al. (2022), in *Computers in Human Behavior*, reported that recruiters shown algorithmically generated candidate rankings exhibited anchoring effects, adjusting their independent evaluations toward algorithmic scores even when instructed to exercise independent judgment. The algorithm's output functions not as one input among many but as a gravitational center around which human judgment reorganizes.

The scale of this attribution is substantial. A 2022 survey by the Society for Human Resource Management found that 75 percent of large employers used some form of applicant tracking system, and among those organizations, hiring managers routinely described ATS-generated shortlists as reflecting "qualified" versus "unqualified" candidates—language that attributes evaluative competence to a keyword-matching and scoring process that makes no competence determination (SHRM, 2022). When iCIMS processes approximately 75 million applications per year through a single platform (iCIMS, 2022), and when hiring managers treat the resulting rankings as expert judgment, D1 is operating at population scale.

### D2: Boundary Erosion — The Dissolution of Procedural Safeguards

Agency attribution enables the second cascade stage: the erosion of boundaries that previously constrained hiring decisions. These boundaries include anti-discrimination law, structured interview protocols, job-relatedness requirements for selection criteria, and the professional norm that hiring decisions should be explicable to the candidate. Algorithmic hiring erodes each of these.

The boundary most visibly dissolved is the requirement of job-relatedness. Under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act and the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (1978), selection criteria must be demonstrably related to job performance. Kim (2017), writing in the *University of Chicago Law Review*, demonstrated that machine learning hiring models trained on historical performance data can weight proxies for protected characteristics—zip code as proxy for race, name structure as proxy for ethnicity, résumé gaps as proxy for gender-linked caregiving—without ever encoding the protected characteristic directly. The opacity of the model prevents both the employer and the applicant from detecting this proxy discrimination, effectively dissolving the job-relatedness boundary by making it unverifiable. The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission and the Department of Justice issued joint guidance in May 2022 explicitly warning that AI hiring tools could violate the Americans with Disabilities Act by screening out candidates with disabilities through facially neutral criteria that were not demonstrably job-related (EEOC & DOJ, 2022).

A second eroded boundary is the right to explanation. In structured human interviews—the constraint-pole comparator scoring 4/12 on the Void Index—an unsuccessful candidate can, at minimum, infer that the decision reflected their answers to specific questions evaluated against disclosed criteria. In algorithmic screening, the rejection arrives as an automated message with no specificity. Fuller et al. (2021), in the Harvard Business School "Hidden Workers" report, estimated that automated screening criteria filtered out 27 million workers who would likely have been qualified for the positions they sought. These candidates received no explanation of what criteria excluded them, no mechanism for appeal, and no knowledge that a human being had never reviewed their application. The procedural boundary between "considered and rejected" and "never seen" dissolved entirely.

The boundary erosion extends to the temporal domain. Performance monitoring systems—which the EU AI Act classifies as high-risk under Annex III, Section 4—enable continuous algorithmic evaluation that dissolves the distinction between the hiring decision and the ongoing employment relationship. When the same vendor ecosystem that screens résumés also monitors keystrokes, tracks productivity metrics, and flags employees for termination risk, the boundary between "candidate" and "employee" becomes permeable in ways that amplify coupling intensity throughout the employment lifecycle. Ajunwa, Crawford, and Schultz (2017), publishing in the *California Law Review*, documented how algorithmic management systems create what they termed "limitless worker surveillance," eroding the boundary between work performance and personal life that labor law historically maintained.

### D3: Harm Facilitation — Documented Injuries at Scale

The eroded boundary environment of D2 enables specific, measurable harms. The most extensively documented is disparate impact discrimination. The EEOC's 2023 enforcement actions included multiple cases involving algorithmic screening tools that produced statistically significant adverse impact against protected classes. Dastin (2018), reporting for Reuters, revealed that Amazon's internal AI recruiting tool, developed beginning in 2014, systematically penalized résumés containing the word "women's"—as in "women's chess club captain"—and downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges. The system had been trained on a decade of hiring data reflecting Amazon's predominantly male technical workforce, and it reproduced that pattern as an optimization target.

Beyond discrimination, D3 manifests as psychological harm from opacity itself. Langer and Landers (2019), in *Human Resource Management Review*, found that applicants subjected to AI-evaluated video interviews reported significantly higher anxiety, lower procedural justice perceptions, and reduced organizational attraction compared to applicants evaluated by human interviewers. When HireVue conducted over 70 million interviews including, prior to January 2021, facial expression scoring, the population-level psychological burden of D3 was not trivial. Candidates performed emotional labor for an algorithmic audience they could not see, could not understand, and could not appeal to—a coupling pattern that the Void Framework identifies as maximally harmful.

### Case Study: Amazon's AI Recruiting Tool (2014–2018)

The complete drift cascade is traceable with dated precision in Amazon's abandoned AI recruiting engine, first reported by Dastin (2018) for Reuters in October 2018. In 2014, Amazon's Edinburgh-based machine learning team began developing an automated system to review résumés and identify top candidates, with the explicit goal of creating a hiring engine analogous to Amazon's product recommendation algorithms. The system ingested ten years of historical hiring data—résumés submitted to the company and resulting hiring decisions—and trained models to predict which candidates would receive offers.

**D1 (2014–2016): Agency attribution.** The project was internally described as an "AI recruiter" capable of rating candidates on a one-to-five-star scale, mirroring Amazon's product rating system. The language attributed evaluative agency to the model: it would "see" résumé patterns, "learn" what a good candidate looked like, and "recommend" hires. Internal stakeholders treated model outputs as expert assessments. The framing positioned the algorithm not as a statistical correlator but as a talent evaluator with superhuman pattern recognition.

**D2 (2015–2017): Boundary erosion.** As the team refined the model, they discovered that it systematically penalized indicators associated with female candidates. Résumés containing the word "women's" were downgraded. Graduates of two all-women's colleges received lower scores. The model had absorbed the historical boundary violation—Amazon's existing gender imbalance in technical hiring—and encoded it as an optimization criterion. Attempts to neutralize the gender signal proved futile; the model found proxy features. The team removed explicit gender indicators, but the system reconstructed gender prediction from correlated features. The boundary between "job-related criteria" and "gender proxy" dissolved within the model's latent feature space in a manner that resisted human correction.

**D3 (2017–2018): Harm facilitation.** Although Amazon states the tool was never used as the sole determinant of hiring decisions, Reuters reported that recruiters consulted the system's recommendations during the evaluation period. The tool was producing candidate rankings that systematically disadvantaged women for technical roles at one of the world's largest technology employers—a company that received, by its own reporting, hundreds of thousands of applications annually. The specific harm—gendered filtering at the top of the hiring funnel—would have been invisible to candidates, who would have received standard rejection communications with no indication that an algorithm had scored them lower on the basis of gender-correlated features. Amazon disbanded the team and abandoned the tool in 2018, but the case demonstrates the full cascade: agency attribution positioned the algorithm as an authoritative talent evaluator (D1), which eroded the job-relatedness boundary by encoding historical discrimination as predictive signal (D2), which facilitated gender-based filtering at scale (D3).

The Amazon case is not anomalous. It is the expected output of the drift cascade operating in a domain that scores 10/12 on the Void Index with a Péclet number of 6.1, deep within Phase IV Pandemonium. The regulatory response—the EU AI Act's classification of recruitment AI as high-risk under Annex III, Section 4, effective August 2, 2026; NYC Local Law 144's mandate for bias audits of automated employment decision tools, effective July 5, 2023; and the EU's outright prohibition of emotion recognition in workplaces under Article 5—represents an institutional attempt to reimpose the boundaries that the drift cascade dissolved. Whether these interventions are sufficient to reduce the domain's Péclet number below the Phase IV threshold remains an empirical question that subsequent regulatory impact analyses must address.

**HireVue (pre-January 2021).** Before discontinuing facial analysis in January 2021, HireVue scored at near-theoretical maximum. Opacity was total: candidates recorded video responses to standardized prompts while proprietary algorithms scored facial micro-expressions, vocal tone, and word choice against models trained on incumbent employee data, with none of these feature weights disclosed (Raghavan et al., 2020, FAT* Conference). Responsiveness was maximally adaptive: the system processed multimodal behavioral signals in real time, generating composite employability scores that shifted with each candidate interaction. Coupling was extreme because the system mediated access to livelihood at scale — over 70 million interviews conducted across 800-plus companies (HireVue corporate disclosures, 2024) — and candidates could not withdraw from the process without forfeiting the job opportunity. Estimated Pe for pre-2021 HireVue is 7.2, deep within Phase IV.

**Pymetrics / Harver game-based assessment.** Acquired by Harver in 2022, Pymetrics deploys neuroscience-derived games measuring cognitive and emotional attributes — attention, risk tolerance, effort allocation — and maps resulting profiles against "top performer" templates at client organizations (Pymetrics technical documentation; Raghavan et al., 2020). Opacity scores maximally because the mapping between game behavior and hiring recommendation is entirely opaque to the candidate: the applicant plays what appears to be a simple cognitive game without knowing which traits are being measured, how they are weighted, or what profile constitutes a passing score. Responsiveness is high because the games adaptively extract behavioral data through interaction design that elicits naturalistic responses, fulfilling the framework's criterion for systems that learn from the observer's engagement. Coupling is maximal: Unilever reported screening over 250,000 candidates through Pymetrics in its first year of deployment alone (Wall Street Journal, 2019), and the assessment serves as a gateway filter — failure means automatic elimination. The modifier is reduced to 1 because Pymetrics has published peer-reviewed bias audits (Wilson et al., 2021, AAAI Workshop on AI and Work) and voluntarily adopted four-fifths rule compliance testing, placing it marginally ahead of competitors on procedural transparency even as substantive algorithmic opacity remains near-total.

**Enterprise ATS resume screening.** Applicant tracking systems from Workday, Oracle Taleo, SAP SuccessFactors, and iCIMS collectively process hundreds of millions of applications annually. iCIMS alone processes approximately 75 million applications per year (iCIMS, 2022 Workforce Report). Opacity is maximal: keyword-matching and machine-learned ranking criteria are proprietary, and the Society for Human Resource Management estimates that 75 percent or more of large employers use some form of ATS (SHRM, 2022). Fuller et al. (2021, Harvard Business School) estimated that automated screening criteria filter out 27 million workers who would likely be qualified — so-called "hidden workers" eliminated by rigid algorithmic thresholds for employment gaps, credential requirements, and job-title matching. Responsiveness scores 2 rather than 3 because most ATS ranking functions are semi-static (rules-based filters updated periodically rather than continuously adaptive). Coupling is maximal: applicants must submit to ATS processing to access most formal employment channels and receive automated rejection emails with zero explanatory specificity. The modifier of 1 reflects the advent of NYC Local Law 144, effective July 5, 2023, which mandates annual bias audits for automated employment decision tools used within New York City (NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, 2023), and the EU AI Act's Annex III Section 4 classification of recruitment AI as high-risk, with compliance obligations taking effect August 2, 2026 (Regulation 2024/1689).

**LinkedIn Recruiter algorithmic ranking.** LinkedIn's Talent Solutions segment generated approximately $7.3 billion in Microsoft's fiscal year 2024 revenue, and the platform surpassed one billion members by late 2024 (Microsoft Annual Report, 2024). Opacity scores 2 rather than 3 because LinkedIn provides partial visibility into profile optimization signals — users can see profile strength indicators and some information about who viewed their profile — even as the ranking algorithm that determines candidate visibility to recruiters remains proprietary. Responsiveness is moderate: the system adapts to recruiter interaction patterns and candidate engagement signals but does so through aggregate behavioral modeling rather than real-time individual behavioral extraction. Coupling scores 2 because while LinkedIn is a dominant professional networking platform, candidates retain alternative channels for job search and the platform mediates opportunity visibility rather than rendering binary hire-reject decisions. The modifier reflects the platform's partial transparency features offset by its market dominance and network-effect lock-in.

**HireVue (post-January 2021).** Following the discontinuation of facial analysis, HireVue transitioned to audio and text-based scoring. This reduces opacity from 3 to 2 — the input modalities are narrower and somewhat more legible, though the scoring algorithm remains proprietary. Coupling remains maximal at 3 because the structural position of the tool in hiring funnels is unchanged. The net reduction of three points from the pre-2021 score illustrates how partial transparency interventions lower void intensity without eliminating it when the underlying architecture preserves adaptive responsiveness and forced engagement. Estimated Pe drops from 7.2 to approximately 4.8, still above the Phase IV threshold.

**Amazon internal recruiting tool (abandoned 2018).** This tool, trained on ten years of historical hiring data and abandoned after engineers discovered it systematically penalized resumes containing the word "women's" and downgraded graduates of all-women's colleges (Dastin, Reuters, October 2018), represents the theoretical maximum on the Void Index. Opacity was total: even Amazon's own engineers did not initially understand why the model produced discriminatory outputs. Responsiveness was maximally adaptive: the model continuously learned from historical hiring patterns that encoded gender bias. Coupling was extreme: the tool was used to evaluate candidates for technical roles at one of the world's largest employers. The modifier of 3 reflects the complete absence of external audit, the lack of any regulatory constraint at the time of deployment, and the fact that the system operated for years before internal discovery. Estimated Pe exceeds 8.0, representing the deepest Phase IV score in the hiring domain and confirming the framework's prediction that maximum void conditions produce maximum harm.

**Structured behavioral interview (human, no AI).** This entity anchors the low-void region. In a structured interview, all candidates receive identical questions, responses are scored on predetermined rubrics, and the evaluative criteria can be disclosed to the candidate in advance. Meta-analytic evidence confirms that structured interviews achieve substantially higher predictive validity (mean corrected validity of 0.44) than unstructured alternatives (0.20) while reducing interviewer bias (Schmidt and Hunter, 1998, Psychological Bulletin; Huffcutt and Arthur, 1994, Journal of Applied Psychology). Opacity scores 1 because some subjective judgment remains, but the criteria and process are largely transparent. Responsiveness scores 1 because the interviewer follows a fixed protocol rather than adapting questions based on real-time behavioral extraction. Coupling scores 1: the stakes remain meaningful but the candidate can prepare for known question types, observe the interviewer's reactions, and exercise some agency within the interaction. The total of 4 places this entity at the constraint pole, confirming the Void Framework's prediction that transparency, invariance, and independence suppress harm even when the functional task — evaluating candidates for employment — is identical to that performed by Phase IV systems.

**Blind skill test (Applied, GapJumpers).** Platforms such as Applied, founded by the UK Behavioural Insights Team, remove candidate names, educational institutions, and demographic identifiers from applications, presenting hiring managers with anonymized work-sample responses scored on standardized rubrics (Behavioural Insights Team, 2016). Opacity is scored at 0: the candidate knows exactly what task they are performing, and the scoring criteria are published. Responsiveness is 0: the assessment does not adapt to or extract behavioral data from the candidate beyond the specific work sample. Coupling is 1 because the assessment still mediates access to employment, though the candidate retains full agency in task performance. The modifier of 1 reflects that these tools remain niche, limiting their regulatory salience. A total of 2 places blind skill tests at the deepest constraint pole in the hiring domain, with an estimated Pe below 1.0 — Phase I Gas, where demon lattice formation is thermodynamically suppressed. This control case is decisive: it demonstrates that the hiring function itself does not require void conditions, and that the opacity, adaptive responsiveness, and forced coupling characteristic of high-scoring entities are architectural choices, not functional necessities.

### Scoring Patterns and Regulatory Implications

Three patterns emerge from the entity-level analysis. First, the correlation between void score and documented harm is near-monotonic: the Amazon tool (12/12) produced the most clearly documented discriminatory outcomes, HireVue's facial analysis (11/12) drew the most sustained regulatory and civil-society challenge, and the constraint-pole entities (2–4/12) have no comparable harm record. Second, regulatory interventions — NYC Local Law 144, the EU AI Act Annex III Section 4 classification, and Article 5's in-force ban on emotion recognition in workplaces — target precisely the high-O, high-R entities the framework identifies as most dangerous, suggesting convergent validity between the Void Framework's structural predictions and legislative risk assessment. Third, HireVue's post-2021 score reduction from 11 to 8 following the removal of facial analysis demonstrates that targeted opacity reduction can shift an entity across phase boundaries but cannot resolve the void unless responsiveness and coupling are simultaneously addressed. The framework predicts that compliance with the EU AI Act's transparency and human oversight requirements will further compress scores toward the Phase II–III boundary, but that full void resolution requires structural redesign toward the constraint pole exemplified by blind skill testing.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Algorithmic Hiring System Looks Like

The preceding sections have documented algorithmic hiring as a Phase IV Pandemonium domain scoring 10/12 on the Void Index with a Péclet number of 6.1. The drift cascade from agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation operates with exceptional fluency in this space, driven by the convergence of proprietary opacity, adaptive responsiveness, and the existential coupling that arises when livelihood depends on engagement with an invisible decision process. This section inverts the analysis. If the Void Framework's constraint specification holds — that transparency, invariance, and independence jointly suppress the harm patterns documented throughout this paper — then it should be possible to describe a hiring architecture that occupies the constraint pole and to identify partial implementations that already exist. The framework predicts that such systems will score at or below 4/12, will not sustain drift cascades, and will produce measurably higher perceived fairness among applicants regardless of outcome.

### A. Transparency Mechanisms: Dissolving the Opacity Condition

A constraint-pole hiring system satisfies three transparency requirements: criterion transparency, process transparency, and outcome transparency. Criterion transparency means that every evaluative dimension is disclosed to candidates before they engage. The candidate knows, in advance, exactly what competencies the system measures, what scoring rubrics apply, and what thresholds determine advancement to the next stage. This is not aspirational; structured interviews, which meta-analytic evidence confirms outperform unstructured alternatives with mean validity coefficients of 0.44 versus 0.20 (Schmidt and Hunter, 1998, *Psychological Bulletin*), operate precisely this way. The interviewer works from a predetermined question set anchored to job-relevant competencies, and candidates can be told exactly what those competencies are. Campion, Palmer, and Campion (1997, *Personnel Psychology*) demonstrated that structured interview validity increases further when candidates receive advance notice of evaluation criteria, because criterion transparency reduces construct-irrelevant variance rather than enabling gaming.

Process transparency requires that the decision pipeline is visible to the candidate at each stage. The candidate knows whether a human, an algorithm, or a hybrid process is evaluating them. They know what data the system ingests and what it ignores. They know the sequence of filtering stages and the approximate selectivity at each stage. NYC Local Law 144, effective July 5, 2023, takes a partial step in this direction by requiring employers to notify candidates that an automated employment decision tool is being used and to disclose the tool's bias audit results (New York City Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, 2023). The EU AI Act, Annex III Section 4, goes further by classifying recruitment AI as high-risk and requiring transparency obligations including notification to affected persons that they are subject to AI-based decision-making (Regulation 2024/1689, Article 50). However, neither mandate achieves full process transparency because neither requires disclosure of the specific criteria, weights, or model architecture. A constraint-pole system would.

Outcome transparency means that every candidate receives substantive feedback on their evaluation, not merely a binary accept/reject signal. Hausknecht, Day, and Thomas (2004, *Personnel Psychology*) found that informational justice — the adequacy of explanation provided to applicants about selection procedures — was the strongest predictor of applicant perceptions of fairness across 86 effect sizes, exceeding even outcome favorability. A constraint-pole system provides each candidate with their scores on each evaluated dimension, the threshold they needed to reach, and where they fell relative to that threshold. This feedback loop is precisely what current ATS architectures eliminate: Raghavan et al. (2020, *FAT* Conference*) found that zero of the vendors audited provided meaningful algorithmic transparency to candidates regarding rejection decisions.

### B. Invariant Outputs: Eliminating Adaptive Responsiveness

The responsiveness condition in algorithmic hiring manifests as systems that adapt their criteria, weights, or thresholds in response to employer behavior, candidate pool composition, or engagement signals. A constraint-pole system produces invariant outputs: given the same candidate inputs against the same job requirements, the system produces the same evaluation regardless of when the assessment occurs, who else is in the candidate pool, or what engagement data the platform has accumulated.

Blind skill assessments represent the clearest existing implementation of this principle. Platforms such as Applied (formerly used by the UK Behavioural Insights Team) strip resumes of identifying information and evaluate candidates through standardized work-sample tests scored against predetermined rubrics. Behaghel, Crépon, and Le Barbanchon (2015, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*) demonstrated in a large-scale randomized controlled trial that anonymized resume screening significantly altered callback rates for candidates from disadvantaged backgrounds, confirming that removing adaptive signals from the evaluation pipeline changes outcomes. The charity-sector pilot that informed Applied's design found that anonymized, structured work-sample scoring predicted job performance more accurately than traditional resume screening while eliminating demographic disparities in advancement rates (Bohnet, 2016, *What Works: Gender Equality by Design*, Harvard University Press).

Invariance also requires temporal stability. A constraint-pole system does not recalibrate its scoring model based on which candidates employers subsequently hire, because such feedback loops encode historical bias into future screening criteria. This is the precise mechanism that destroyed Amazon's internal recruiting tool, which trained on ten years of hiring data reflecting male-dominated engineering teams and consequently penalized resumes containing the word "women's" and downgraded graduates of all-women's colleges (Dastin, 2018, *Reuters*). An invariant system decouples its evaluative criteria from employer behavioral feedback, anchoring scores to validated, job-relevant competency models instead.

### C. Independent Operation: Severing the Coupling Condition

The coupling condition in hiring technology derives its intensity from the existential stakes of employment: candidates cannot disengage without exiting the labor market. A constraint-pole system minimizes coupling through three design principles. First, it supports genuine optionality by ensuring that candidates can pursue equivalent opportunities through non-algorithmic channels. Second, it eliminates surveillance-intensity features — emotion recognition, facial analysis, keystroke dynamics, vocal prosody scoring — that force candidates to submit biometric and behavioral data as a condition of application. Article 5 of the EU AI Act, already in force, bans emotion recognition systems in workplaces, which extends to interview contexts (Regulation 2024/1689, Article 5(1)(f)). A constraint-pole system would never have deployed such features because they compound coupling by extracting data streams that candidates cannot control or monitor.

Third, independent operation requires that the system's evaluative integrity does not depend on candidate engagement intensity. Current platforms reward candidates who optimize their resumes for ATS keyword parsing, who practice AI interview tools, and who signal engagement through platform activity metrics — all forms of coupling that advantage candidates with resources and information access. A constraint-pole system evaluates job-relevant competency through standardized instruments that are robust to preparation effects, following the psychometric principle that valid assessments measure the construct of interest rather than test-taking strategy (Sackett, Zhang, Berry, and Lievens, 2022, *Personnel Psychology*).

### D. Existing Partial Implementations and Their Void Scores

Several existing systems approximate the constraint pole. Structured behavioral interviews, when conducted with predetermined questions, standardized scoring rubrics, and interviewer training, score approximately 4/12 on the Void Index: opacity is low because criteria are disclosed (O = 1), responsiveness is constrained by the fixed protocol (R = 1), coupling remains moderate because the candidate still faces a consequential evaluation (C = 1), and minor modifier effects persist from interviewer variability (M = 1). Blind skill testing platforms score approximately 2/12: opacity is near-zero because the test content and scoring criteria are transparent (O = 0), responsiveness is eliminated by invariant rubrics (R = 0), coupling is reduced by the low-stakes, task-focused format (C = 1), and minimal modifiers apply (M = 1). These scores confirm the framework's prediction that systems at the constraint pole suppress the drift cascade. Neither structured interviews nor blind skill tests generate L2 or L3 vocabulary in user discourse, neither produce the agency-attribution effects documented for AI interviewing platforms, and both produce significantly higher applicant fairness perceptions (Hausknecht et al., 2004).

### E. Policy Recommendations Grounded in the Framework

The Void Framework generates five specific policy recommendations for moving algorithmic hiring toward the constraint pole. First, mandate criterion disclosure: every automated screening system must publish, to each candidate, the complete set of evaluative dimensions and their relative weights before the candidate submits data. Second, require outcome explanation: rejection decisions must include dimension-level scores and threshold comparisons, converting the current zero-feedback void into an informational channel. Third, prohibit adaptive recalibration from employer hiring feedback, severing the loop through which historical bias propagates into future screening. Fourth, enforce assessment invariance through mandatory temporal stability audits, requiring vendors to demonstrate that the same candidate profile produces statistically equivalent scores across time periods and candidate pool compositions. Fifth, require that every algorithmically screened position offer a parallel non-algorithmic application pathway, ensuring that engagement with the automated system remains genuinely voluntary.

These recommendations operationalize the constraint specification: transparent plus invariant plus independent equals the opposite of void. The EU AI Act's high-risk classification and NYC Local Law 144's bias audit mandate represent partial steps, but neither addresses the full three-condition architecture. Until all three conditions are simultaneously constrained, the drift cascade will find the remaining open channel and propagate harm through it.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework generates domain-specific predictions that are falsifiable through empirical methods available to labor economists, organizational psychologists, and computer scientists within existing research infrastructures. Each prediction below derives from a specific framework mechanism — the opacity-responsiveness-coupling triad, the drift cascade, or the Péclet-driven phase classification — and specifies the evidence type and numerical threshold required for falsification. The predictions are ordered from most immediately testable to those requiring longitudinal observation, consistent with the framework's commitment to progressive empirical exposure.

**Prediction 1: NYC Local Law 144 bias audits will not reduce measured disparate impact ratios below the four-fifths threshold for the majority of audited tools within 24 months of enforcement.**

The Void Framework predicts that mandatory bias audits operating under conditions of residual opacity — where the audit methodology is vendor-selected, the auditor is vendor-paid, and the underlying model architecture remains proprietary — will function as performative transparency rather than substantive constraint. NYC Local Law 144, effective July 5, 2023, requires annual bias audits of automated employment decision tools but permits employers to select their own auditors and does not mandate disclosure of model internals (New York City Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, 2023). The framework's constraint specification requires transparency that is *independent* of the entity being constrained; vendor-funded audits violate this requirement. Specifically, the prediction holds that fewer than 40 percent of audited tools publicly reporting under Local Law 144 will demonstrate impact ratios at or above 0.80 (the EEOC four-fifths rule threshold established in the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures, 29 C.F.R. §1607.4D) across all protected categories simultaneously. This prediction is testable within 12 months using the publicly filed bias audit summaries that Local Law 144 requires employers to post on their websites. Raghavan et al. (2020) found that zero of the vendors they examined provided meaningful algorithmic transparency to candidates; the framework predicts that mandated audits without structural independence will replicate this opacity under a compliance veneer.

**Falsified if:** More than 60 percent of tools with publicly filed Local Law 144 audits demonstrate impact ratios at or above 0.80 across race, gender, and intersectional categories simultaneously in the audit summaries filed by July 2026.

**Prediction 2: The EU AI Act's Annex III §4 high-risk classification will produce measurable vendor market consolidation rather than measurable improvement in applicant-perceived fairness.**

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) classifies recruitment AI, CV screening, interview evaluation, and performance monitoring as high-risk under Annex III §4, with compliance obligations taking effect August 2, 2026 (European Parliament, 2024). The framework predicts that compliance costs — estimated at €170,000 to €330,000 per high-risk AI system for conformity assessment (European Commission Impact Assessment, 2021) — will drive small and mid-tier vendors from the European market while leaving the opacity-responsiveness structure of dominant platforms functionally intact. The Péclet number for the domain (estimated at 6.1, Phase IV) reflects systemic properties that regulation targeting individual tools cannot decompose. The prediction specifies that applicant-perceived fairness, measured using Gilliland's (1993) procedural justice framework for selection systems, will not improve by more than 0.3 standard deviations in EU labor markets within 36 months of the compliance deadline, even as vendor count decreases by 15 percent or more.

**Falsified if:** Applicant-perceived procedural justice scores in EU labor markets improve by more than 0.5 standard deviations (measured via validated instruments such as the Selection Procedural Justice Scale) within 36 months of the August 2026 compliance date, *and* vendor market concentration does not increase (HHI remains stable or decreases).

**Prediction 3: Systems scoring above 8/12 on the Void Index will exhibit drift cascade progression — from agency attribution (D1) through boundary erosion (D2) to harm facilitation (D3) — at rates significantly exceeding systems scoring below 5/12.**

The drift cascade predicts a structured progression in which high-void systems first attract anthropomorphic or agentic framing from users ("the algorithm decided," "AI rejected me"), then erode normative boundaries around acceptable data extraction (facial analysis, vocal biomarkers, keystroke dynamics), and finally facilitate measurable harm through discriminatory filtering that no identifiable human actor authorized. The Amazon recruiting tool abandoned in 2018 after systematic penalization of women's resumes (Dastin, 2018, Reuters) exemplifies full D1-D2-D3 cascade completion in a system the framework retrospectively scores at 9/12. HireVue's deployment of facial analysis scoring across 70 million interviews prior to its January 2021 discontinuation (HireVue, 2021; Harwell, 2019, Washington Post) illustrates D2 boundary erosion — the normalization of facial expression analysis as a legitimate hiring input. The prediction specifies that in a longitudinal audit of at least 20 algorithmic hiring tools, systems scoring above 8/12 will exhibit documented evidence of all three drift stages at a rate exceeding 70 percent, while systems scoring below 5/12 (structured interviews with standardized rubrics, blind skill assessments) will exhibit full cascade completion at a rate below 15 percent.

**Falsified if:** In a systematic audit of 20 or more hiring tools scored on the Void Index by independent raters achieving inter-rater reliability of κ ≥ 0.70, systems above 8/12 exhibit full D1-D2-D3 cascade evidence at rates below 40 percent, *or* systems below 5/12 exhibit full cascade evidence at rates above 40 percent.

**Prediction 4: The "hidden workers" filtering effect identified by Fuller et al. (2021) will be disproportionately concentrated in high-Péclet hiring architectures and will diminish monotonically as Pe decreases.**

Fuller et al. (2021) estimated that 27 million qualified workers in the United States alone are systematically filtered out by automated screening criteria — rigid keyword matching, employment gap penalties, credential inflation, and proxy variables that correlate with protected characteristics. The Void Framework predicts that this filtering effect is not uniformly distributed across hiring systems but is a direct function of void intensity as measured by the Péclet number. Specifically, ATS platforms operating with full algorithmic ranking, no candidate feedback, and no human override (estimated Pe ≥ 5.0) will filter qualified candidates at rates exceeding 50 percent, while structured hiring processes with transparent criteria, candidate feedback mechanisms, and human review at each stage (estimated Pe ≤ 2.0) will filter qualified candidates at rates below 15 percent. The "qualified candidate" operationalization follows Fuller et al.'s methodology: candidates who meet stated job requirements but are rejected prior to human review. This prediction is testable through controlled audit studies in which matched resumes are submitted to employers using different hiring technology stacks, following the correspondence audit methodology established by Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) and extended to algorithmic contexts by Kline, Rose, and Walters (2022, Quarterly Journal of Economics).

**Falsified if:** Correspondence audit studies across at least 500 employer-application pairs reveal no statistically significant relationship (p > 0.10) between estimated Péclet number of the hiring technology stack and the callback rate differential between qualified and overqualified applicants matched on stated job criteria.

**Prediction 5: The Article 5 prohibition on emotion recognition in workplaces will produce technological displacement — migration from banned facial analysis to functionally equivalent vocal, linguistic, or behavioral biomarker assessment — rather than elimination of the underlying void architecture.**

Article 5 of the EU AI Act, now in force, prohibits emotion recognition systems in the workplace (European Parliament, 2024). HireVue's discontinuation of facial analysis in January 2021 preceded this regulation but illustrates the predicted dynamic: the company replaced visual facial scoring with audio and linguistic analysis that extracts functionally similar affective signals through a different sensory modality (HireVue, 2021). The framework predicts that prohibition of specific measurement techniques within a high-void architecture (Pe > 4) will produce modality displacement rather than void reduction, because the underlying opacity-responsiveness-coupling structure remains intact. Concretely, within 24 months of Article 5 enforcement, at least 60 percent of vendors previously offering facial emotion recognition for hiring will offer alternative assessment modalities (vocal prosody analysis, linguistic sentiment scoring, behavioral micro-gesture tracking, or physiological inference from interaction timing) that extract affective or personality signals without triggering the literal text of the emotion recognition prohibition.

**Falsified if:** Fewer than 30 percent of vendors previously offering workplace emotion recognition deploy alternative affective-signal extraction tools within 24 months of Article 5 enforcement, *and* independent technical audits confirm that successor products do not extract functionally equivalent affective or personality constructs through alternative modalities.

These five predictions collectively test the framework's core claim that void architecture — not any particular technology within it — drives harm in algorithmic hiring. Predictions 1 and 5 are testable within 12 to 24 months using publicly available compliance filings and vendor product documentation. Predictions 2 and 4 require coordinated empirical studies within 36 months. Prediction 3 requires systematic retrospective and prospective audit methodology that, while resource-intensive, employs established techniques from algorithmic auditing (Sandvig et al., 2014, International Communication Association). If the majority of these predictions fail at their specified thresholds, the Void Framework's application to algorithmic hiring requires fundamental revision.

## X. Conclusion

This paper has demonstrated that algorithmic hiring and HR technology constitutes one of the most structurally complete void architectures identified across the twenty-one domains analyzed within the Void Framework research program. The domain's composite Void Index score of 10/12, with a Péclet number of 6.1, places it firmly in Phase IV Pandemonium — a regime characterized by self-reinforcing opacity, adaptive responsiveness to observer behavior, and coupling so intense that disengagement is functionally equivalent to labor market exit. The three void conditions converge with unusual severity: proprietary algorithmic screening that no candidate can inspect (O = 3), systems that dynamically adjust to applicant optimization strategies in ways that punish the very behaviors they elicit (R = 3), and existential economic dependence that compels continued engagement regardless of perceived fairness or demonstrated harm (C = 3). The modifier score of 1 reflects the incipient but as yet incomplete regulatory infrastructure that has begun, but not yet managed, to impose meaningful constraint.

The platform-level analysis underscores the framework's discriminant validity. AI video interviewing platforms such as HireVue, which conducted over 70 million interviews including facial expression scoring prior to its January 2021 discontinuation of that modality, scored 11/12 — approaching the theoretical ceiling. Game-based psychometric tools such as Pymetrics, deployed by Unilever to screen over 250,000 candidates in a single year, scored 10/12. These systems maximize all three conditions simultaneously: candidates cannot see what behavioral features are extracted or weighted, the tools adapt their assessments based on candidate interaction patterns, and applicants must submit to them as gatekeeping mechanisms for desirable positions. By contrast, structured human interviews anchored at 4/12 and blind skill assessments at 2/12, demonstrating that transparency, invariance, and independence — the constraint-pole triad — suppress void formation even within the identical functional domain of candidate evaluation. This within-domain contrast is among the sharpest observed across the entire Void Framework series.

The drift cascade from D1 through D3 operated with particular clarity. Attribution of agentive properties to hiring algorithms — the belief that the system "knows" who is qualified — licenses boundary erosion in which candidates surrender biometric data, gameplay behavior, and conversational patterns far exceeding what any human interviewer could lawfully demand. This erosion, in turn, facilitates systematic harm: the Harvard Business School "Hidden Workers" study estimated that 27 million qualified workers were filtered out by automated screening criteria that bore no validated relationship to job performance (Fuller et al., 2021, Harvard Business School). The Amazon internal recruiting tool, abandoned in 2018 after Reuters reported systematic gender bias in its resume scoring, illustrates how D3 harm crystallizes when opacity prevents detection and coupling prevents exit.

The regulatory trajectory is consequential for this domain's void dynamics. The EU AI Act, published as Regulation 2024/1689 on August 1, 2024, classifies recruitment AI, CV screening, interview evaluation, performance monitoring, and algorithmic termination decisions as high-risk under Annex III, Section 4, with compliance obligations taking effect on August 2, 2026. Article 5, already in force, bans emotion recognition systems in workplaces — a provision that would have prohibited HireVue's pre-2021 facial analysis scoring had it applied at the time of deployment. NYC Local Law 144, effective July 5, 2023, mandates independent bias audits for automated employment decision tools, establishing the first municipal transparency requirement. The Illinois Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act requires candidate consent and disclosure for AI-analyzed video interviews. These instruments collectively address opacity and, to a limited extent, responsiveness, but none yet addresses the coupling condition — the labor market structure that compels applicant engagement regardless of algorithmic fairness.

Within the Void Framework scoring pipeline, algorithmic hiring occupies the upper bound of the distribution alongside algorithmic content recommendation and autonomous surveillance systems. The data moat in this vertical is formidable and self-reinforcing. Vendors accumulate proprietary training datasets comprising hundreds of millions of resumes, interview recordings, psychometric responses, and hiring outcomes. These datasets cannot be replicated by regulators, auditors, or competitors. The feedback loop is closed: the algorithm's own prior decisions define what counts as a "successful hire," embedding historical bias into the training signal without external correction. Raghavan et al. (2020) found that zero vendors provided meaningful algorithmic transparency to candidates — a finding that, five years later, remains substantially accurate for the majority of the market despite regulatory pressure. The data moat thus functions as a structural opacity amplifier, ensuring that even well-intentioned regulatory interventions confront an asymmetry of information that the regulated entity has every commercial incentive to maintain.

The domain's kill condition — that algorithmic hiring demonstrably produces better applicant outcomes and higher perceived fairness than structured human alternatives — remains unmet. Until that condition is satisfied, the Void Framework identifies algorithmic hiring as a system in which designed opacity, adaptive responsiveness, and compelled engagement converge to produce predictable, systematic, and disproportionately distributed harm at a scale affecting hundreds of millions of workers annually.
