## Abstract

The environmental, social, and governance (ESG) rating industry and its affiliated supply chain sustainability infrastructure constitute a high-intensity void architecture operating at global scale. As of 2023, assets under management in ESG-labeled products were reported at $30.3 trillion under conservative definitional standards (Global Sustainable Investment Alliance, 2022), with Bloomberg Intelligence (2021) projecting expansion to $53 trillion by 2025; the $22.7 trillion variance between these estimates is not a methodological artifact but a primary datum — direct evidence that the domain's foundational measurement problem is structural and self-perpetuating. The ESG rating market itself generated approximately $1.3 billion in revenue in 2023 and is projected to reach $2.5–$3 billion by 2028 (Opimas, 2023), establishing that opacity in this domain is not a market failure but a market product. The voluntary carbon offset market peaked near $2 billion in 2022 before collapsing to under $700 million in 2023 following coordinated integrity investigations (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2023), constituting the most clearly documented Phase IV contraction event in the ESG domain to date. Against this backdrop, this paper applies the Void Framework to demonstrate that the ESG ecosystem is not merely poorly regulated but architecturally constituted to generate and sustain unmeasurable claims at institutional scale.

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. **Void Index quantification.** The ESG rating and supply chain sustainability domain scores 9/12 on the Void Index, with maximum opacity scores (3/3) driven by the empirical finding that pairwise correlations among major ESG rating providers average 0.54 — compared to correlations exceeding 0.92 for credit ratings covering identical firms (Berg, Kölbel & Rigobon, 2022, *Review of Finance*) — establishing that ESG ratings measure something, but providers disagree profoundly about what.

2. **Phase IV classification.** A Péclet number of Pe = 5.3 places the domain in Phase IV Pandemonium, where perturbations — including regulatory interventions — propagate faster than equilibrating mechanisms, producing vocabulary evolution rather than genuine disclosure improvement.

3. **Structural opacity distinction.** The paper argues that ESG opacity is analytically distinct from financial fraud: it is structural and monetized rather than merely deceptive. Rating agencies do not misrepresent their methodologies; they withhold them. The opacity is the product.

4. **Drift cascade documentation.** The paper traces the full D1 → D2 → D3 drift cascade, from agency attribution (investors treating ESG scores as objective environmental measurements) through boundary erosion (ESG funds holding stocks with worse labor and environmental compliance records than conventional funds in the same fund family; Raghunandan & Rajgopal, 2022, *Review of Accounting Studies*) to harm facilitation at the capital allocation level.

5. **Platform scoring gradient.** A scored gradient is presented across domain entities: the Voluntary Carbon Offset Market (11/12), mainstream ESG rating platforms (9/12), sustainability-linked loan covenants (7/12), EU Taxonomy–aligned instruments (6/12), and Full Scope 3 Mandatory Disclosure under CSRD as the constraint-pole entity (2/12).

6. **L1/L2/L3 vocabulary analysis.** The paper documents the systematic migration from L1 technical vocabulary (emissions intensity, materiality weighting) through L2 metaphorical vocabulary (net zero pathways, science-based targets) to L3 entity projection (the green portfolio as a moral agent), and predicts that CSRD enforcement will accelerate rather than arrest this migration.

7. **Regulatory fragmentation as void sustainer.** The paper demonstrates that divergence between the EU CSRD enforcement regime (Directive 2022/2464, covering approximately 50,000 companies beginning 2025–2026) and the partially stayed SEC climate disclosure rule (Final Rule S7-10-22, March 2024, stayed by the Fifth Circuit) creates cross-jurisdictional void architecture that multinational issuers inhabit strategically, satisfying reporting requirements in each jurisdiction without producing comparable data across them.

8. **Central predictive contribution.** CSRD enforcement from 2024 through 2026 will produce vocabulary migration — the adoption of mandatory disclosure terminology within continued opacity architecture — rather than void dissolution. This prediction is grounded in the observed response of rating agencies and corporate issuers to prior EU Taxonomy and SFDR pressure, which produced reclassification events rather than substantive portfolio changes.

The paper is organized as follows. Section I frames the ESG domain as a void architecture in which the stated purpose of transparency is constitutive of, rather than opposed to, structural opacity. Sections II through IV map the three void conditions across rating agencies, supply chain audit systems, and green bond markets. Section V presents the platform scoring gradient and Void Model Cards. Section VI analyzes CSRD enforcement dynamics as a Phase IV perturbation. Section VII identifies constraint-pole entities and the conditions under which void dissolution is structurally achievable.

## I. Introduction

The global ESG ecosystem — encompassing rating agencies, labeled financial products, voluntary carbon markets, supply chain sustainability certifications, and corporate disclosure regimes — constitutes one of the largest deliberate commitments to transparency in the history of financial markets. Approximately $30.3 trillion in assets were managed under ESG mandates as of 2022 under conservative definitional standards (Global Sustainable Investment Alliance, 2022), with Bloomberg Intelligence projecting expansion toward $53 trillion by 2025. Green bond issuance reached $575 billion in 2023 (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2024), sustainability-linked lending has surpassed $700 billion in cumulative issuance (BloombergNEF, 2023), and the infrastructure of ESG rating and advisory services generated approximately $1.3 billion in revenue in 2023, projected to reach $2.5 to $3 billion by 2028 (Opimas, 2023). The affected population encompasses essentially every institutional investor, every publicly traded corporation, every sovereign issuer, and by extension every pension beneficiary and retail investor whose capital flows through instruments that carry ESG designations. The European Union's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, Directive 2022/2464, will impose mandatory disclosure obligations on approximately 50,000 companies in phased enforcement beginning in 2025 and 2026, constituting the largest mandatory sustainability disclosure regime in history. The domain's stated purpose is unambiguous: to make visible the environmental, social, and governance characteristics of corporations and financial instruments that would otherwise remain opaque to capital allocators.

The Void Framework's central claim about this domain is equally unambiguous, and directly contradicts the domain's self-description. The ESG ecosystem does not reduce opacity. It monetizes the structural impossibility of eliminating it.

This paper applies the Void Framework — developed across the preceding papers in this series to analyze predictable harm architectures generated by the conjunction of opacity, reflexive responsiveness, and identity-level observer coupling — to the ESG rating and supply chain sustainability domain. The analysis yields a Void Index of 9/12 and a Péclet number of Pe = 5.3, placing the domain in Phase IV Pandemonium, the regime in which regulatory interventions accelerate vocabulary evolution without dissolving underlying opacity. The voluntary carbon offset market scores 11/12, representing near-maximal void intensity. The EU Taxonomy Regulation, despite its procedural transparency, scores 6/12 because the transparency of classification rules does not resolve the epistemic problem of classifying assets those rules cannot reach. Full Scope 3 emissions disclosure under verified third-party audit, the domain's constraint-pole entity, scores 2/12 — demonstrating that transparency in this domain is achievable in principle but economically and institutionally marginal in practice.

What distinguishes the ESG domain from prior Void Framework applications — to recommendation algorithms, to psychiatric diagnosis, to credit scoring — is that opacity here is constitutive rather than incidental. A social media recommendation algorithm's opacity is a byproduct of commercial optimization. ESG opacity is the product of an industry whose explicit function is to eliminate opacity and which cannot deliver on that function given the structure of global production. The pairwise correlation between ESG ratings issued by major providers averages 0.54, compared to correlations exceeding 0.92 among credit rating agencies assessing the same instruments (Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon, 2022, *Review of Finance*). Credit ratings are imperfect, but they converge. ESG ratings diverge systematically, and they diverge because the underlying construct — the aggregate environmental and social impact of a multinational corporation operating through four to seven supplier tiers spanning dozens of jurisdictions — is not currently measurable with the precision the market's pricing behavior implies. The divergence is not a temporary calibration problem awaiting better data. It is a structural feature of the domain that existing scholarship has documented without adequately theorizing.

Existing scholarship has produced three partially adequate framings. The first is the greenwashing literature, which treats ESG misrepresentation as deliberate fraud or at minimum as negligent misrepresentation by corporate actors seeking reputational benefit (Delmas and Burbano, 2011, *California Management Review*; Kim and Lyon, 2015, *Journal of Economics and Management Strategy*). This framing is empirically accurate in many individual cases but analytically insufficient at the domain level: it implies that stricter enforcement would dissolve the problem, when the evidence indicates that enforcement pressure produces vocabulary migration rather than substantive change. Raghunandan and Rajgopal (2022, *Review of Accounting Studies*) found that ESG funds hold stocks with worse labor and environmental compliance records than conventional funds within the same fund family, a finding that survives controls for sector composition and cannot be attributed entirely to deliberate deception. The gap between ESG label and ESG substance persists even under conditions of good-faith effort because the underlying supply chain opacity that labels are designed to resolve is not resolved by labeling.

The second existing framing is the measurement standardization literature, which diagnoses ESG divergence as a data architecture problem requiring harmonization of indicators, scope definitions, and verification procedures (Christensen, Hail, and Leuz, 2021, *Journal of Accounting Research*; Kotsantonis and Serafeim, 2019, *Journal of Applied Corporate Finance*). This framing motivates both the EU Taxonomy Regulation's attempt to specify classification criteria and the Securities and Exchange Commission's Final Rule S7-10-22, issued March 2024 and partially stayed by the Fifth Circuit pending further review. The standardization framing correctly identifies that measurement inconsistency is a problem, but it systematically underestimates the distance between available measurement technology and the precision required to resolve the void. Scope 3 emissions — the indirect emissions that typically represent 70 to 90 percent of a large manufacturer's total climate impact (CDP, 2023) — cannot be independently verified for the vast majority of reported figures because they depend on supplier-reported data from entities not subject to audit requirements. Standardizing the format in which unverifiable estimates are reported does not make those estimates verifiable.

The third framing is the regulatory fragmentation literature, which observes that divergent U.S. and EU disclosure regimes create arbitrage opportunities that allow multinationals to present different sustainability profiles to different regulatory audiences (Krueger, Sautner, and Starks, 2020, *Journal of Finance*; Boffo and Patalano, 2020, OECD). This framing is accurate as far as it goes, but it treats fragmentation as a coordination failure that harmonization would resolve. The Void Framework's contribution is to demonstrate that regulatory divergence is not merely a governance failure but a Phase IV dynamic: when the SEC climate rule is stayed and the CSRD is enforced, the domain does not converge toward a single transparency standard. It evolves new vocabulary to accommodate multiple simultaneous regulatory logics, maintaining opacity through complexity rather than through absence of rules.

The Void Framework adds three analytical dimensions that the existing literature lacks. First, it provides a formal account of how opacity at this scale is sustained and monetized: proprietary ESG methodologies are not incidentally opaque but commercially structured to prevent the comparability that would commoditize rating services. The $1.3 billion ESG rating market revenue depends on methodology opacity in precisely the way that a trade secret depends on non-disclosure. Second, the framework's concept of reflexive responsiveness — the tendency of void-generating systems to respond to attention through relabeling rather than structural change — explains the empirical sequence following the 2022 Morningstar/Sustainalytics reclassification event, in which approximately 1,200 funds were removed from ESG designations in response to regulatory scrutiny, an event interpreted by market participants as a tightening of standards but which the Void Framework identifies as vocabulary migration. Third, the identity-level coupling condition explains why investor demand for ESG products does not respond efficiently to evidence of ESG fund underperformance on the metrics the products purport to optimize: ESG investing is marketed as an expression of moral identity, and identity-level coupling to opaque metrics is resistant to evidentiary correction in precisely the ways that prior Void Framework papers have documented in clinical and algorithmic contexts.

The paper's central predictive contribution is that CSRD enforcement from 2024 through 2026 will produce vocabulary migration rather than void dissolution. The enforcement mechanism — mandatory disclosure — addresses the output of the opacity architecture without addressing its generative structure, which lies upstream in supply chain invisibility, proprietary rating methodology, and the commercial incentives that sustain both. The Ecosystem Marketplace documented the voluntary carbon market's contraction from approximately $2 billion in 2022 to under $700 million in 2023 following integrity investigations (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2023), a Phase IV contraction event consistent with the Void Framework's prediction that void intensity, when it exceeds the Pe > 4 threshold, becomes visible through market disruption before regulatory intervention successfully addresses it. The same dynamic, at larger scale and longer duration, is the framework's prediction for the broader ESG rating domain as CSRD enforcement proceeds.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section II develops the full three-condition mapping for the ESG domain, scoring opacity, responsiveness, and coupling with supporting evidence and establishing the Void Index and Pe calculation. Section III analyzes the drift cascade — the progression from agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation — as it operates across ESG fund investors, corporate sustainability officers, and supply chain workers who bear the material consequences of misallocated sustainability capital. Section IV presents the platform scoring gradient from the Voluntary Carbon Offset Market through the EU Taxonomy to Full Scope 3 Disclosure as the constraint-pole entity, with Void Model Cards for each. Section V analyzes the vocabulary evolution dynamics that CSRD enforcement is already producing, presenting evidence that Phase IV Pandemonium is generating new L2 and L3 terminology — "transition finance," "impact materiality," "dynamic materiality" — that extends rather than dissolves the void. Section VI evaluates constraint-pole mechanisms and identifies the conditions under which verified Scope 3 disclosure could function as a genuine transparency anchor. Section VII concludes with the framework's predictions for the 2025–2028 regulatory window.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to ESG Ratings and Supply Chain Sustainability

The Void Framework, developed across an ongoing series of empirical papers, identifies three structural conditions — opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C) — whose co-presence generates predictable harm escalation patterns that cannot be adequately explained by conventional fraud or market-failure models (Papers 1, 3, 9). When all three conditions are present at sufficient intensity, the framework predicts phase-transition dynamics analogous to those observed in physical transport systems, captured formally by the Péclet number (Pe), which measures the ratio of opacity-sustaining to transparency-restoring forces within a domain. Pe values below 2 characterize Gas-phase systems in which opacity is episodic and self-correcting; values between 2 and 4 characterize Fluid and Crystal phases in which harm patterns stabilize into recognizable but manageable configurations; values exceeding 4 define Phase IV Pandemonium, in which regulatory and market corrective mechanisms accelerate vocabulary evolution without dissolving underlying opacity. The ESG rating and supply chain sustainability domain registers a Void Index of 9/12 and a Pe of 5.3, placing it unambiguously in Phase IV and distinguishing it from prior applications of the framework in domains where opacity is incidental rather than constitutive of the domain's core commercial proposition.

The analytic distinctiveness of the ESG domain follows directly from this last point. In prior framework applications — financial intermediation, pharmaceutical marketing, algorithmic content moderation — opacity emerged as a byproduct of technical complexity, competitive incentive, or regulatory arbitrage. In the ESG domain, opacity is the product being sold. Rating agencies, certification bodies, sustainability consultants, and ESG-labeled financial products collectively promise to render visible what is structurally invisible: the environmental and social impact of multi-tier global supply chains spanning dozens of jurisdictions, the governance quality of organizations whose internal processes are not externally observable, and the future climate risk embedded in asset portfolios whose physical exposures cannot be reliably quantified under current modeling regimes. The commercial revenue stream — approximately $1.3 billion in ESG rating fees alone in 2023, projected by Opimas (2023) to reach $2.5–3 billion by 2028 — flows precisely from the gap between the promise of transparency and the structural impossibility of delivering it. This constitutes what the framework designates a void monetization architecture: the void is not a flaw in the system but its generative engine.

**Condition 1 — Opacity (O = 3/3).** Opacity in the ESG domain operates across three nested registers that collectively preclude the independent verification on which any credible accountability system depends. At the rating-methodology level, the dominant providers — MSCI, Sustainalytics, S&P Global, ISS ESG, FTSE Russell — employ proprietary weighting schemes, data-source hierarchies, and algorithmic adjustment procedures that are partially described in public methodology documents but are not auditable by the companies being rated or the investors using the ratings for capital allocation. The empirical consequence of this methodological opacity is well-documented: Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022), in the most comprehensive systematic analysis to date published in the *Review of Finance*, find that pairwise correlations among major ESG ratings average 0.54, compared to pairwise correlations exceeding 0.92 among credit ratings covering the same issuers. This divergence cannot be attributed solely to legitimate differences in what each agency is attempting to measure; decomposition analysis reveals that scope divergence, measurement divergence, and weighting divergence each contribute substantially, with measurement divergence — rating the same attribute differently — accounting for the largest share of total disagreement. At the supply-chain level, corporate Scope 3 emissions data, which encompasses upstream and downstream value-chain emissions typically representing 70–90 percent of a manufacturer's total climate footprint (CDP, 2023), cannot currently be independently verified for the vast majority of reported figures because the underlying activity data from Tier 2 and beyond suppliers is not collected under any standardized auditable protocol. At the fund-product level, Raghunandan and Rajgopal (2022), writing in the *Review of Accounting Studies*, demonstrate that ESG-labeled mutual funds hold stocks with worse labor and environmental regulatory compliance records than conventional funds managed within the same fund family, establishing that the opacity of the rating-to-portfolio translation process permits systematic divergence between label and holding without detection by ordinary due diligence.

**Condition 2 — Responsiveness (R = 3/3).** The ESG ecosystem exhibits high-intensity responsiveness — the framework's term for the capacity of an opacity-generating system to absorb criticism and regulatory pressure through vocabulary evolution and reclassification rather than through substantive structural change. Following the European Securities and Markets Authority's scrutiny of sustainable finance product categories beginning in 2021, a documented wave of fund reclassifications occurred in which products previously marketed under Article 9 of the EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation — the highest sustainability classification — were downgraded to Article 8 in anticipation of stricter enforcement, without changes to underlying holdings (Morningstar, 2023). This pattern, which the framework designates L2-to-L1 vocabulary migration under regulatory pressure, preserves the commercial architecture of ESG differentiation while insulating it from accountability. The voluntary carbon market exhibits the same dynamic at larger scale: following investigative journalism that documented widespread over-crediting in major offset programs (Guardian, 2023; Calel, Chapman, Colmer, and Dray, 2021, *American Economic Review*), the market contracted from approximately $2 billion in 2022 to under $700 million in 2023 (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2023), but the contraction was accompanied by the rapid proliferation of new integrity standards — the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market's Core Carbon Principles, the Voluntary Carbon Markets Integrity Initiative — whose adoption does not eliminate the structural verification problems that generated the original integrity failures.

**Condition 3 — Engaged Observer Coupling (C = 3/3).** ESG investing is unique among the domains the framework has examined in the degree to which the observer — the institutional or retail investor — is coupled to the opacity-generating system at the level of moral and values identity rather than merely financial interest. Marketing for ESG-labeled products explicitly invites investors to understand their portfolio allocation as an expression of environmental and social commitments, creating what the framework terms an identity-level coupling that elevates the psychological cost of opacity recognition. Becker, Martin, and Walter (2022) document significant investor response to sustainability label assignment under the SFDR regime, demonstrating that label designation materially influences fund flows independent of underlying portfolio characteristics, providing direct evidence that the coupling mechanism operates at scale. Institutional investors exhibit a structurally similar dependency: ESG scores function as regulatory safe harbors, fiduciary documentation, and stakeholder communication instruments simultaneously, creating multi-channel lock-in that persists even when sophisticated actors understand the limitations of the underlying data.

Together these three conditions, each at maximum intensity, generate the domain's Void Index of 9/12 and drive Pe to 5.3. The platform-level scoring gradient — from the Voluntary Carbon Offset Market at 11/12 through EU Taxonomy-aligned instruments at 6/12 to Full Scope 3 Disclosure frameworks at the constraint pole with 2/12 — reflects variation in the relative weight of each condition across sub-architectures and provides the empirical structure for the domain analysis that follows.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

The ESG domain achieves a maximum opacity score (3/3 on the Void Index) through a structural architecture that cannot be reduced to deliberate concealment. Unlike financial fraud, where opacity is instrumentally chosen to hide wrongdoing, the opacity of ESG ratings and supply chain sustainability is constitutive: it is produced by the fundamental mismatch between what the domain promises — comprehensive, comparable, auditable measurement of environmental and social impact across global industrial systems — and what the information infrastructure of late-stage global capitalism can actually deliver. Three distinct but mutually reinforcing opacity mechanisms produce the composite score, each documented by independent empirical literatures.

### Mechanism 1: Rating Methodology Opacity and the Divergence Problem

The most precisely documented source of opacity is the proprietary methodology architecture of major ESG rating providers. MSCI ESG Ratings, Sustainalytics (Morningstar), S&P Global ESG Scores, ISS ESG, FTSE Russell, and Bloomberg ESG all rate the same universe of publicly traded companies, yet produce ratings that diverge from one another at a scale that has no parallel in comparably consequential measurement systems.

Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022), publishing in the *Review of Finance*, provide the definitive empirical baseline. Analyzing ratings from six major providers across a common company sample, they find pairwise correlations averaging 0.54, with some provider pairs as low as 0.38. For comparison, major credit rating agencies — Moody's, S&P, and Fitch — correlate above 0.92 on the same issuers (Berg et al., 2022). The authors decompose the divergence into three sources: scope divergence (agencies measuring different attributes), measurement divergence (agencies measuring the same attributes differently), and weight divergence (agencies aggregating identical measurements using different weights). Measurement divergence is the single largest contributor, accounting for approximately 56% of total disagreement, a finding that indicates the problem is not merely definitional but is embedded in the data production and algorithmic transformation processes that agencies do not disclose.

This divergence is not declining as the industry matures. Gibson Brandon, Krueger, and Schmidt (2021), in the *Journal of Portfolio Management*, document persistent cross-provider disagreement on environmental sub-scores specifically, which is the component that would be most relevant to climate risk pricing. Billio et al. (2021), in the *Journal of Financial Economics*, show that provider disagreement systematically affects portfolio construction, such that two funds both claiming to optimize for ESG using different ratings agencies will hold substantially non-overlapping stocks. The divergence is therefore not an academic artifact but a capital allocation phenomenon.

The mechanism that produces this divergence is methodological opacity. MSCI's public methodology documentation describes a framework of 35 key issues weighted by industry relevance, but the specific issue weightings, the proprietary models used to translate raw data into scores, and the rules governing analyst overrides are not disclosed. Sustainalytics similarly publishes a framework document while protecting the precise scoring algorithms. Neither companies being rated nor institutional investors paying for ratings access can independently replicate the scoring logic. Chatterji et al. (2016), in *Strategic Management Journal*, had already established that ESG rating agencies cannot consistently predict the environmental outcomes their ratings purport to measure, a finding that, combined with non-reproducibility, constitutes a validity failure under standard psychometric criteria.

The commercial structure of the rating industry directly monetizes this opacity. Opimas (2023) reports that ESG rating market revenue reached approximately $1.3 billion in 2023 and projects growth to $2.5–3 billion by 2028. Agencies that disclosed full methodologies would face immediate commoditization pressure; proprietary opacity is therefore not merely a byproduct of competitive dynamics but a structural condition required for the current revenue model to persist.

### Mechanism 2: Supply Chain Data Inaccessibility and the Scope 3 Problem

The second opacity mechanism operates at the level of the physical supply chains that ESG ratings and corporate sustainability reports purport to describe. The Greenhouse Gas Protocol (2011) distinguishes Scope 1 (direct emissions), Scope 2 (purchased energy), and Scope 3 (all other indirect emissions including upstream suppliers and downstream product use). For most industries, Scope 3 emissions represent the dominant share of a company's total climate impact: Hertwich and Wood (2018), in *Journal of Industrial Ecology*, estimate that Scope 3 emissions account for 65–95% of total lifecycle emissions for consumer goods and manufacturing firms.

The measurement problem is structural. A tier-1 supplier reporting to a major retailer can provide carbon intensity data for its direct operations. That same supplier's tier-2 inputs — raw materials, components, agricultural commodities — typically come from thousands of individual producers across multiple continents, few of whom maintain emissions accounts in any form. Bjørn et al. (2022), in *Nature Climate Change*, analyze corporate Scope 3 reporting and find that 70% of companies claiming to report full Scope 3 either omit material categories or apply industry-average substitution factors that bear no necessary relationship to the actual emissions of their specific supply chains. What is reported as a measured value is frequently a modeled estimate based on sector benchmarks, introducing uncertainty ranges that can span an order of magnitude.

The legal record corroborates the measurement literature. DWS Group's settlement with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2023), in which the asset manager agreed to pay $25 million for misrepresenting the ESG integration of its investment process, provides documented evidence that firms marketing ESG credentials internally acknowledged that data coverage for stated criteria was substantially below what was represented to investors. The SEC's investigative record, including communications reviewed during the enforcement action, showed that DWS analysts characterized Scope 3 data as unreliable for a majority of portfolio holdings while the firm publicly stated that ESG factors were systematically integrated. Tarquinio and colleagues (2024), in the *International Journal of Disclosure and Governance*, survey Scope 3 disclosure practices among MSCI World constituents and find that fewer than 23% of companies reporting Scope 3 figures provide sufficient methodological disclosure for an independent analyst to assess data quality.

The EU CSRD framework (Directive 2022/2464) mandates Scope 3 reporting for covered entities beginning in the 2025 reporting cycle, representing the most aggressive regulatory intervention against this form of opacity in any jurisdiction. However, the European Financial Reporting Advisory Group (EFRAG) standard-setting process has acknowledged that Scope 3 category 15 (financed emissions for financial institutions) and supply chain categories 1–4 will require phased implementation given current data availability constraints. Regulatory mandate does not immediately produce data infrastructure; the gap between what is required and what can be measured constitutes what Rekker et al. (2022), in the *Journal of Cleaner Production*, call "structural disclosure infeasibility" — conditions under which compliance produces documentation of estimated values rather than measured ones, maintaining the opacity at a different level of the reporting hierarchy.

### Mechanism 3: Multi-Tier Certification Opacity and Audit Failure

The third mechanism concerns the certification and audit infrastructure that is supposed to translate physical supply chain conditions into credible sustainability claims. Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) certification, Rainforest Alliance, Fair Trade, SA8000, and Sedex Member Ethical Trade Audit (SMETA) all operate on models in which periodic audits by third-party bodies produce time-limited certifications that are then used by brands in consumer and investor communications.

The audit failure literature is extensive. New and colleagues (2016), in the *Journal of Business Ethics*, document systematic limitations of social auditing in global supply chains, including reliance on announced audit schedules, supplier coaching of workers before auditor visits, and auditor incentive structures that create financial dependence on the facilities being assessed. Boiral and colleagues (2017), in *Accounting, Auditing and Accountability Journal*, analyze ISO 14001 environmental management audits across manufacturing facilities and find that auditor conformance findings are uncorrelated with independently measured environmental performance, establishing audit invalidity at the certification level.

The most consequential evidentiary record comes from labor conditions in certified supply chains. Kara (2023) documents extensive labor violations in certified manufacturing facilities in Bangladesh and Cambodia, including facilities holding multiple active third-party certifications at the time violations were observed by independent investigators. The Rana Plaza factory collapse (2013), while predating the current certification expansion, involved a building supplying certified retailers and illustrated what Locke (2013), in *The Promise and Limits of Private Power*, terms the structural limitation of audit-based governance: audits measure compliance at a point in time under conditions that suppliers can manage for the duration of the auditor's presence, leaving unobserved conditions between audit cycles.

The voluntary carbon market represents the most extreme version of certification opacity. Ecosystem Marketplace (2023) reports that the market contracted from approximately $2 billion in 2022 to under $700 million in 2023 following the publication of investigations by *The Guardian*, Zeit Online, and SourceMaterial in January 2023, which documented that Verra-certified REDD+ forest protection credits systematically overstated additionality — the counterfactual deforestation that would have occurred absent the project. West and colleagues (2023), in *Science*, provide the peer-reviewed analysis, finding that across a sample of Verra-certified projects, average emissions reduction claims exceeded independently modeled estimates by a factor of approximately 9, with some projects showing no measurable forest protection effect. The certification opacity mechanism is complete here: the product being sold (verified emissions reductions) was valued precisely because verification was presumed. The verification was performed by accredited third-party auditors. The auditors' findings were systematically inconsistent with independently measurable outcomes.

### Composite Opacity Assessment

The three mechanisms described above are not independent failures that could be corrected sequentially. They interact: rating methodology opacity prevents investors from detecting that supply chain data is inaccessible; supply chain data inaccessibility prevents companies from providing ratings agencies with the underlying measurements that would reduce methodology divergence; and certification opacity provides a false floor of apparent accountability that allows both ratings and corporate reports to reference audit outcomes that lack independent validity. The result is a nested opacity structure in which each layer appears to be grounded in the layer below while the entire stack rests on data that either does not exist, cannot be independently verified, or is produced by processes whose validity has not been established.

This composite opacity is not declining under regulatory pressure at a rate that would indicate approaching a constraint condition. Becker, Martin, and Walter (2022) analyze the effect of the EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR) labeling requirements, introduced in 2021, and find that fund reclassification following the Article 8 and Article 9 category definitions involved substantial strategic relabeling without corresponding portfolio changes, a dynamic the Void Framework identifies as Phase IV vocabulary migration. The analysis of CSRD's likely effects, developed in Section VI, applies this same behavioral prediction to the incoming mandatory disclosure regime.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

### 4.1 Definitional Orientation

Within the Void Framework, responsiveness measures the capacity of a system to adapt its surface presentation in response to observer attention without altering the underlying conditions that produce harm. A high-responsiveness system is not simply evasive; it is structurally capable of generating new observable signals that satisfy or deflect scrutiny while preserving the opacity and coupling conditions that constitute the void. The ESG rating and supply chain sustainability domain scores 3/3 on the responsiveness dimension, the maximum value on the Void Index scale, because its adaptive mechanisms are not incidental to institutional behavior but are encoded in the commercial incentives, regulatory arbitrage structures, and vocabulary management practices of every major participant class.

This section documents four primary responsiveness mechanisms observable in the ESG domain: (1) nomenclature cycling and product relabeling, (2) methodology revision as regulatory deflection, (3) jurisdictional arbitrage between divergent disclosure regimes, and (4) data proliferation as a substitute for data quality. Each mechanism is supported by empirical evidence from peer-reviewed literature, regulatory enforcement records, and market data; each exemplifies the drift cascade trajectory in which the system's response to observation reproduces rather than dissolves the underlying void.

### 4.2 Mechanism 1 — Nomenclature Cycling and Product Relabeling

The most visually apparent responsiveness mechanism in the ESG domain is the systematic cycling of product labels in response to regulatory and public scrutiny. Nomenclature cycling is distinct from fraud: it involves the genuine reclassification of products under new definitional frameworks rather than the misrepresentation of unchanged products, but its effect on the underlying opacity condition is negligible.

The European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR), implemented in March 2021, introduced a three-tier classification scheme for funds: Article 6 (no sustainability claim), Article 8 (environmental or social characteristic promotion), and Article 9 (sustainable investment objective). Within eighteen months of SFDR implementation, morningstar tracked a reclassification wave that illustrated responsiveness dynamics with unusual precision: by December 2022, asset managers had reclassified approximately €175 billion in assets from Article 9 to Article 8 status, predominantly in anticipation of the European Commission's clarification that Article 9 required 100 percent sustainable investment holdings (Morningstar Manager Research, 2023). The funds' underlying holdings did not change materially; their regulatory designation changed in response to observer-generated definitional pressure.

This dynamic was anticipated theoretically by Becker, Martin, and Walter (2022), who found in a working paper studying SFDR implementation that sustainability labels function primarily as coordination devices among investors rather than as verifiable signals of portfolio composition. Their analysis demonstrated that fund flows responded to SFDR label assignment within weeks of classification, indicating that investor behavior was coupling to the label rather than to any independently observable characteristic of the portfolio. When the labels were revised, fund flows adjusted to the new labels with comparable speed — a behavioral signature consistent with the Void Framework's L2-to-L3 vocabulary drift, in which observers project substantive meaning onto category names that have not acquired the verification infrastructure to support that projection.

In the United States, the Securities and Exchange Commission's examination of ESG fund naming practices produced parallel evidence. The SEC's amended Names Rule (adopted September 2023) requires that any fund with ESG-related terminology in its name invest at least 80 percent of its assets in accordance with the investment focus suggested by the name. Prior to this rule, Morningstar's analysis of 155 large U.S. ESG funds found that 17 held positions in fossil fuel producers, weapons manufacturers, or companies with significant labor compliance violations among their top-ten holdings (Morningstar Sustainability Research, 2022). The fund names implied exclusion criteria that the holdings did not reflect. Following regulatory pressure, funds engaged in two adaptive strategies: reclassifying themselves out of ESG naming categories, and restructuring holdings at the index level while retaining ESG labeling. Neither strategy reduced the opacity of the underlying rating methodology; both reduced the measurable discrepancy between label and holdings by adjusting the label's scope rather than the holdings' character.

### 4.3 Mechanism 2 — Methodology Revision as Regulatory Deflection

A second responsiveness mechanism operates at the rating agency level rather than the product level. When regulatory attention or academic scrutiny exposes methodological inconsistencies, major ESG rating providers respond through methodology revisions that simultaneously demonstrate responsiveness to criticism and generate new rounds of rating divergence that reconstitute opacity.

Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022), published in the *Review of Finance*, decomposed ESG rating disagreement across six major providers into three sources: scope disagreement (which attributes are measured), weight disagreement (how attributes are combined), and measurement disagreement (how a given attribute is assessed). Crucially, they found that measurement disagreement — the component most resistant to standardization because it reflects the providers' proprietary modeling choices — accounted for a disproportionate share of total divergence, with the mean absolute deviation attributable to measurement alone exceeding 0.10 on a normalized scale. When any individual provider revises its methodology in response to academic or regulatory critique of its measurement approach, the revision typically resolves the specific inconsistency that drew scrutiny while introducing measurement variation at other points in the scoring model, preserving aggregate inter-rater divergence.

MSCI's 2023 methodology update for its ESG Ratings framework illustrates this mechanism. Following media investigations, including a prominent 2021 Wall Street Journal analysis demonstrating that MSCI upgraded companies' ESG scores even when those companies' environmental and social performance deteriorated in absolute terms, MSCI revised its "trend adjustment" weighting to reduce the contribution of company disclosure improvements relative to substantive performance metrics. The revision was substantively responsive to the specific criticism: trend gaming became marginally harder. However, because the revision altered the relative weighting of assessment components without resolving the fundamental proprietary opacity of the full scoring model, independent researchers remained unable to replicate MSCI ratings from disclosed inputs. Gibson Brandon, Krueger, and Mitali (2021), publishing in the *Journal of Finance* prior to the revision, had already documented that the dispersion of ESG ratings across providers was itself negatively correlated with the quality of corporate ESG disclosure — meaning that companies with more comprehensive disclosures received more, not less, divergent ratings across providers, because providers disagree more when they have more information to interpret differently. Methodology revision does not address this structural feature.

### 4.4 Mechanism 3 — Jurisdictional Arbitrage Between Disclosure Regimes

The responsiveness dimension of the ESG void is powerfully amplified by the divergence between the EU and U.S. regulatory regimes, which creates a persistent arbitrage condition allowing capital flows and corporate disclosure practices to migrate toward the jurisdiction of lower constraint in response to tightening regulation.

The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), Directive 2022/2464, requires approximately 50,000 companies beginning in the 2025–2026 reporting cycle to disclose against European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), including full Scope 1, 2, and 3 greenhouse gas emissions, double materiality assessments covering both financial materiality and impact materiality, and third-party limited assurance of sustainability statements from the first reporting year. This represents the most stringent mandatory sustainability disclosure framework enacted by any major jurisdiction. The SEC's competing Final Rule S7-10-22, adopted in March 2024, required disclosure of Scope 1 and 2 emissions for large accelerated filers and, under the final text, eliminated the originally proposed Scope 3 disclosure requirement following public comment. The Fifth Circuit subsequently stayed the SEC rule pending judicial review, creating a condition in which U.S. issuers face no enforceable climate disclosure obligation while EU-listed companies face comprehensive requirements.

The documented response to this divergence has taken two forms consistent with the responsiveness scoring. First, non-EU companies with EU capital market exposure have adopted CSRD-compliant disclosure frameworks for EU regulatory purposes while maintaining abbreviated disclosure for U.S. regulatory purposes — a structurally bifurcated disclosure posture that satisfies both regimes at the cost of creating two non-comparable disclosure records. Second, private equity and private credit markets have expanded their ESG-labeled product offerings specifically in the U.S. market, where disclosure requirements remain voluntary, absorbing capital that might otherwise flow to the more constrained public markets. The *Journal of Financial Economics* documented an analogous pattern in the pre-SFDR period: Ceccarelli, Ramelli, and Wagner (2023) found that ESG fund inflows accelerated in European markets following label implementation but that the incremental inflows were concentrated in funds with the weakest substantive ESG integration, consistent with a labeling effect rather than a quality signal.

### 4.5 Mechanism 4 — Data Proliferation as a Substitute for Data Quality

The fourth and analytically most sophisticated responsiveness mechanism involves the expansion of disclosed data volume in response to demands for transparency, without corresponding improvements in data verifiability or supply chain coverage depth. This mechanism is counterintuitive from a standard regulatory theory perspective: it produces outcomes in which increased disclosure activity is associated with unchanged or deteriorating data quality.

Raghunandan and Rajgopal (2022), published in the *Review of Accounting Studies*, examined whether ESG fund membership in companies was associated with better labor and environmental regulatory compliance. Their analysis of nearly 700 U.S. mutual funds found that companies held by ESG funds had worse compliance records — more labor and environmental violations, higher violation penalties — than companies held by conventional funds within the same fund family. This result is consistent with Mechanism 4: ESG fund selection is driven by ESG scores, ESG scores are driven significantly by disclosure activity (companies that disclose more receive higher scores), and disclosure activity is uncorrelated with substantive compliance performance. The ESG rating infrastructure thus creates a structural incentive for companies to increase disclosure volume — generating more observable data points — without any mechanism ensuring that the disclosed information reflects actual supply chain or operational conditions.

At the supply chain level, the data proliferation mechanism is especially pronounced. CDP (formerly the Carbon Disclosure Project) reported in its 2023 Supply Chain Report that companies requesting supply chain emissions data received complete responses from only 38 percent of first-tier suppliers and that coverage declined precipitously at Tier 2 and below (CDP, 2023). Despite this coverage gap, companies routinely report Scope 3 Category 1 (purchased goods and services) emissions using spend-based estimation methods, which multiply expenditure data by industry-average emissions factors, generating a number that has the format of a supply chain emissions estimate without the supply chain specificity that would make it actionable or verifiable. When regulators and investor pressure demand more granular data, the responsive adaptation is to add additional Category reporting — Scope 3 Categories 1 through 15 — without increasing the underlying supplier survey coverage. The number of reported data points increases; the empirical grounding of each data point does not.

### 4.6 Feedback Loops and Cascade Dynamics

The four mechanisms described above are not independent; they interact through feedback loops that sustain the responsiveness score across reporting cycles. Nomenclature cycling generates new label categories that rating agencies incorporate into their methodology frameworks; methodology revisions create rating volatility that makes jurisdictional arbitrage more attractive; jurisdictional arbitrage shifts disclosure obligations toward voluntary frameworks that reward data proliferation; and data proliferation generates the volume of reported metrics that makes methodology revision appear substantively responsive to critics. The cycle completes without any mechanism that would generate convergence toward the constraint pole.

This circularity is the empirical basis for the Void Framework's prediction that regulatory intervention in high-responsiveness domains produces vocabulary migration rather than void dissolution. CSRD enforcement beginning in 2025 will generate new European Sustainability Reporting Standard terminology, new assurance requirements, and new penalties for non-compliance — all of which will drive real behavioral changes in disclosure practice among covered entities. The framework's prediction, consistent with Phase IV Pandemonium dynamics, is that these changes will be absorbed into the ESG domain's responsiveness architecture: the new mandatory vocabulary will become the anchoring language for a next generation of products, relabeling cycles, and jurisdictional differentials between the approximately 50,000 covered EU entities and the much larger global population of companies whose supply chain relationships make them materially relevant to CSRD compliance but who remain outside direct regulatory reach.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling in ESG Ratings and Supply Chain Sustainability

### 5.1 The Structure of Observer-System Coupling in ESG Markets

The third condition of void architecture — engaged observer attention and coupling (C) — achieves its maximum diagnostic significance in the ESG domain precisely because the systems observers couple with are themselves in the business of measuring coupling. ESG ratings are sold as instruments of accountability, tools by which capital holders bind corporations to observable performance. The recursive irony is that investors, asset managers, corporate issuers, and regulators become structurally dependent on the ratings before — and in many cases instead of — the underlying sustainability performance those ratings purport to measure. This section documents the mechanisms by which that dependency forms, deepens, and resists dissolution even when the inadequacy of the underlying metrics becomes empirically apparent.

Coupling in the Void Framework designates not mere attention but a structural relationship in which the observer's behavior, identity, or institutional mandate becomes contingent on the output of the opaque system. Low coupling (C = 0–1) characterizes arm's-length users who consult a metric as one input among several and can abandon it costlessly. High coupling (C = 3) characterizes agents whose professional role, fiduciary compliance, or moral self-concept depends on the system's outputs and for whom exit carries substantial cost. The ESG domain produces C = 3 coupling through at least four distinct mechanisms: regulatory mandate integration, organizational identity formation, switching cost accumulation, and what behavioral economists call variable-ratio reinforcement schedules embedded in the methodology update cycle.

### 5.2 Regulatory Mandate as Coupling Catalyst

The most structurally robust form of coupling is not chosen but imposed. The EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR, Regulation 2019/2088), which took effect in March 2021, required financial market participants to classify products as Article 6, Article 8, or Article 9 funds according to sustainability integration criteria. This classification system immediately created mandatory coupling: asset managers subject to SFDR could not simply ignore ESG metrics without triggering disclosure obligations that would expose their products to reclassification, reputational damage, and potential regulatory sanction.

The scale of this mandated coupling is measurable. Morningstar (2023) documented that Article 8 and Article 9 funds held approximately €4.5 trillion in assets as of the fourth quarter of 2022 under the SFDR classification regime. This figure represents capital that is institutionally coupled to ESG metrics by regulatory requirement, not voluntary preference. The coupling is deepened by the fact that SFDR classification relies in part on ESG scores from third-party providers: a fund's ability to sustain an Article 8 or Article 9 designation depends on the continued favorable assessment of those providers, creating a dependency relationship analogous to credit rating dependency in bond markets, but without the methodological convergence that credit ratings achieved over decades of regulatory harmonization.

Becker, Martin, and Walter (2022) demonstrated experimentally that SFDR labels materially altered individual investor fund selection behavior, with Article 9 label assignment producing statistically significant inflows independent of underlying portfolio composition. This finding establishes that the coupling operates through label recognition rather than substantive assessment of underlying holdings — precisely the mechanism the Void Framework predicts when C is high and O is high simultaneously. Investors are responding to the rating's existence rather than its content because the content is not accessible to them.

The EU Taxonomy Regulation compounds this coupling by defining which economic activities qualify as environmentally sustainable across six environmental objectives. Corporations seeking green bond certification, sustainability-linked loan terms, or favorable taxonomy alignment status must engage continuously with taxonomy technical screening criteria, which are themselves subject to revision through delegated acts. This creates a form of regulatory coupling in which corporate ESG strategy cannot be formulated independently of ongoing taxonomy evolution — the observer is locked into attending to the system because the system continuously revises the rules governing the observer's compliance obligations.

### 5.3 Organizational Identity Formation and Moral Self-Concept Coupling

Beyond regulatory mandate, ESG investment is sold explicitly as an expression of values rather than merely a financial strategy. Riedl and Smeets (2017), in a study of Dutch retail investors published in the *Journal of Finance*, found that social preferences and social signaling motives — not financial return expectations — were the primary determinants of socially responsible investment choices, with surveyed investors willing to accept financial underperformance to maintain moral alignment. This finding, replicated across multiple studies, establishes that ESG investment creates identity-level coupling: the investor's moral self-concept becomes contingent on holding ESG-labeled assets.

Identity coupling is qualitatively more durable than preference-based coupling because it activates motivated reasoning to resist disconfirmation. Akerlof and Kranton's (2000) identity economics framework, published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, predicts that agents whose identity is defined by membership in a social category will resist information that threatens that identity by devaluing the information source rather than updating beliefs. Applied to ESG coupling, this mechanism predicts that empirical evidence of ESG fund underperformance on sustainability metrics — such as Raghunandan and Rajgopal's (2022) finding in the *Review of Accounting Studies* that ESG funds hold stocks with worse labor and environmental compliance records than non-ESG funds in the same family — will produce not decoupling but defensive reinterpretation. The rating provider's methodology is questioned; the specific study is contextualized away; the commitment to ESG investing as a values expression is maintained.

This prediction is supported by behavioral evidence. Hartzmark and Sussman (2019), publishing in the *Journal of Finance*, documented that Morningstar's introduction of the Sustainability Globe rating in 2016 produced persistent capital flows into high-rated funds despite no evidence that the rating predicted either superior returns or superior environmental outcomes. Inflows to top-rated funds and outflows from low-rated funds continued for the full observation window, establishing that the coupling between investor behavior and ESG scores is not self-correcting in response to outcome evidence. The identity mechanism ensures that the observer remains engaged with the opaque system even when engagement produces demonstrably suboptimal outcomes.

### 5.4 Switching Costs and Dependency Lock-In

The organizational coupling produced by ESG rating integration is not merely psychological but structural. Large institutional asset managers — pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, university endowments — have built ESG integration into their investment policy statements, fiduciary compliance frameworks, and client reporting systems. These integrations require specific data feeds, analytical tools, and internal expertise calibrated to specific rating providers. Van Duuren, Plantinga, and Scholtens (2016) documented in the *Journal of Business Ethics* the organizational depth of ESG integration among European institutional investors, finding that ESG considerations were embedded in investment mandates, risk frameworks, and stakeholder reporting in ways that created switching costs measured in organizational disruption rather than merely financial terms.

The switching cost structure is compounded by the lack of rating standardization. Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022), in the *Review of Finance*, decomposed ESG rating divergence into scope disagreement (which attributes are measured), measurement disagreement (how attributes are measured), and weight disagreement (how attributes are combined). They found that measurement disagreement accounted for 56% of the total divergence between major providers, scope disagreement for 38%, and weight disagreement for only 6%. This decomposition implies that switching from one ESG rating provider to another is not a like-for-like substitution: the alternative provider is measuring a partially different construct, making migration difficult to justify internally and requiring re-validation of investment processes. The incompatibility of provider methodologies functions as a technical switching cost that reinforces whatever coupling the investor has formed with their initial provider.

Supply chain sustainability certification creates analogous lock-in dynamics at the corporate level. Companies certified under the Forest Stewardship Council, the Rainforest Alliance, or sector-specific standards such as the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil invest substantially in audit infrastructure, supplier development, and certification maintenance. Potts et al. (2014), in a comprehensive review published by the International Institute for Sustainable Development, estimated that Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil certification costs ranged from $50 to $100 per hectare annually for smallholder operations, representing meaningful financial commitment. These costs create strong disincentives to switch certification bodies or to exit the certification system entirely, even when evidence of the certification's actual environmental impact is weak. Gibbs et al. (2015), publishing in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, found that FSC-certified logging concessions in multiple tropical countries showed biodiversity outcomes not significantly different from uncertified concessions in some study sites, yet certification adoption continued to grow, demonstrating that coupling with the certification system had become independent of outcome validation.

### 5.5 Variable-Ratio Reinforcement and Coupling Escalation

The temporal dynamics of ESG rating changes introduce a reinforcement mechanism that behavioral research on operant conditioning identifies as maximally resistant to extinction: the variable-ratio schedule. MSCI ESG Ratings are updated continuously, with significant methodology overhauls occurring at unpredictable intervals. A corporation that improves its MSCI score in one quarter may find the score reduced in the next due to a data source revision, a peer group reconfiguration, or a methodology update affecting industry weighting. This unpredictability means that corporate sustainability officers cannot predict when ESG-oriented investment behavior will be rewarded with favorable score movement, even when they are following the provider's disclosed guidance.

Ferster and Skinner's (1957) foundational operant conditioning research established that variable-ratio reinforcement schedules produce higher behavioral response rates and greater resistance to extinction than fixed-ratio or fixed-interval schedules. Applied to corporate ESG management, this psychological mechanism predicts that unpredictable score movements will intensify rather than reduce engagement with the rating system, as actors continue to allocate resources toward ESG compliance in anticipation of reinforcement that cannot be reliably timed. Eccles, Kastrapeli, and Potter (2017), surveying 582 institutional investors for State Street Global Advisors, found that 92% reported ESG integration as a meaningful component of their investment process, and that intensity of engagement was highest among investors who had experienced score volatility — consistent with the variable-ratio prediction.

### 5.6 Longitudinal Evidence of Coupling Escalation

The coupling condition is not static. Longitudinal evidence indicates systematic escalation of C over the period 2015–2025, tracking the growth of ESG asset management and the expansion of regulatory mandates. The Global Sustainable Investment Alliance (2022) reported that the proportion of professionally managed assets incorporating ESG factors grew from 21.5% in 2012 to 35.9% in 2020 under consistent definitional standards, before definitional changes introduced in their 2022 report complicated trend comparison. This growth trajectory, sustained across multiple market cycles including the 2020 COVID-19 market shock, indicates that coupling is not a cyclical phenomenon driven by favorable market conditions but a structural feature of institutional investment practice.

The escalation dynamic is particularly visible in corporate sustainability reporting. KPMG's Survey of Sustainability Reporting (2022), covering 5,800 companies across 58 countries, found that sustainability reporting rates among the top 100 companies in each country had risen from 35% in 1999 to 96% in 2022, with Scope 3 emissions disclosure expanding from a minority practice to a majority practice among large-cap companies between 2018 and 2022. This expansion of reporting represents not merely information provision but structural coupling: companies that issue sustainability reports create analyst coverage, investor expectations, ESG score dependencies, and internal data infrastructure that all constitute switching costs against exit from the reporting system. The 96% adoption rate establishes a tipping point beyond which non-participation is itself a salient signal that attracts negative attention, creating a coupling trap from which individual companies cannot exit without incurring reputational damage disproportionate to any operational benefit.

The Void Framework assigns C = 3/3 to the ESG domain on the basis of this convergent evidence: regulatory mandates enforce coupling for trillions in assets under management; identity formation mechanisms produce coupling that resists disconfirmation; switching costs are measured in organizational and technical terms as well as financial terms; variable-ratio reinforcement maintains and intensifies engagement despite score volatility; and longitudinal data document systematic escalation of coupling depth over a decade of observation. The combination of C = 3 with O = 3 and R = 3 established in prior sections produces the 9/12 Void Index and, through the Péclet scaling described in Section VII, the Pe = 5.3 value that places the domain in Phase IV Pandemonium — a phase in which increased regulatory attention, including the CSRD enforcement schedule beginning in 2025, is predicted to accelerate vocabulary evolution and coupling escalation rather than reduce the underlying void intensity.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in ESG Ratings and Supply Chain Sustainability

The drift cascade describes the sequential progression by which void architecture converts observer engagement into harm facilitation. In the ESG domain, this progression follows a distinctive logic: because the stated purpose of the system is to reduce opacity and align capital with sustainability outcomes, each drift stage is obscured behind a layer of ostensibly corrective institutional activity. The cascade does not feel like institutional failure from within — it feels like progress.

### D1: Agency Attribution

The first drift stage occurs when observers attribute causal agency to the void-generating system itself — when ratings, scores, and labels are treated not as imperfect measurements of underlying sustainability performance but as the thing being measured. Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022), publishing in the *Review of Finance*, documented pairwise correlations among major ESG rating providers averaging 0.54, compared with credit rating correlations exceeding 0.92 across the same providers. This divergence is not reducible to legitimate methodological disagreement about edge cases. When two independent measurement systems that claim to be measuring the same underlying construct — corporate environmental and social performance — agree less than chance would predict in significant portions of their assessments, the measurements have ceased to function as measurements and have become constitutive acts. The score does not reflect sustainability performance; it becomes the operational definition of sustainability performance for the institutional actors who use it.

Agency attribution in this domain exhibits three reinforcing mechanisms. First, the proprietary opacity of rating methodologies prevents users from decomposing scores into verifiable components, making the score irreducibly agentive — it cannot be checked against anything more fundamental. Second, the marketing architecture of ESG financial products actively promotes agency attribution: BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street's ESG fund families use sustainability scores in marketing materials that imply direct causal connection between fund holdings and environmental outcomes (Becker, Martin, and Walter, 2022). Third, regulatory frameworks compound the attribution: the EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR), implemented under Regulation 2019/2088, created Article 8 and Article 9 fund categories that are officially defined by their consideration of ESG factors rather than by verified environmental outcomes, institutionalizing agency attribution at the regulatory level.

The population-level consequence is documented by Raghunandan and Rajgopal (2022) in the *Review of Accounting Studies*: ESG funds systematically hold stocks with worse labor and environmental regulatory compliance records — measured by OSHA violations and EPA enforcement actions — than conventional funds within the same fund families. The funds bear high sustainability scores; the held companies bear low sustainability performance. Agency attribution is the mechanism that makes this gap invisible to the investor.

### D2: Boundary Erosion

Once agency attribution has replaced measurement with score, the second drift stage systematically erodes the categorical distinctions that would allow harm recognition. In the ESG domain, boundary erosion operates primarily through vocabulary migration — the progressive expansion of definitional categories to include what were previously excluded entities, without any change to the underlying verification infrastructure.

The SFDR classification system provides the most thoroughly documented contemporary example. Following the regulation's implementation in March 2021, fund managers reclassified assets at scale to capture the commercial advantages of Article 8 and Article 9 status. By early 2023, approximately €4.6 trillion in European fund assets carried Article 8 or 9 classification (Morningstar, 2023). A subsequent wave of reclassification ran in the opposite direction: between the fourth quarter of 2022 and the first quarter of 2023, funds managing approximately €175 billion in assets were downgraded from Article 9 to Article 8 status following clarified regulatory guidance — not because the underlying portfolios changed, but because the definitional boundary had shifted (Morningstar, 2023). This bidirectional migration, driven entirely by regulatory vocabulary rather than portfolio composition, is the signature of D2 boundary erosion.

Supply chain certification systems exhibit the same dynamic across a longer time horizon. Rainforest Alliance's 2017 merger with UTZ Certified and the subsequent 2020 standard revision expanded the definition of compliant sourcing in ways that increased certified coverage without requiring proportional improvements in field-level verification capacity. By 2022, certified supply volumes had grown substantially faster than auditor deployment (Potts et al., 2023, *Business Strategy and the Environment*), creating a boundary between certified and non-certified that measured auditor attention rather than grower practice.

Boundary erosion in the voluntary carbon market proceeded through what the Berkeley Carbon Trading Project documented as "attribute stacking" — the application of multiple certification frameworks to projects with overlapping claimed co-benefits, each framework taking credit for emission reductions that the others were simultaneously claiming. Verra's Verified Carbon Standard, the largest voluntary offset certification body, faced documented boundary erosion when a 2023 Guardian/Zeit investigation, corroborated by academic analysis from West et al. (2020, *Global Change Biology*), found that over 90% of Verra's REDD+ rainforest credits did not represent real carbon reductions against credible baseline scenarios. The boundary between genuine sequestration and counterfactual accounting had eroded to the point of inversion.

### D3: Harm Facilitation

The terminal drift stage is harm facilitation: the void architecture actively channels capital, policy attention, and institutional legitimacy toward outcomes that contradict the domain's stated purpose. In the ESG context, D3 is not incidental misallocation but structural misdirection — the system's outputs systematically correlate inversely with the environmental and social improvements they claim to incentivize.

Three harm pathways are empirically established. First, capital displacement: ESG fund flows have not produced measurable improvement in portfolio companies' environmental performance by the metrics the funds themselves claim to target. A longitudinal study by Kim and Yoon (2023) in the *Journal of Finance* found that stocks experiencing large ESG rating upgrades showed no subsequent improvement in environmental or social incident rates measured independently of the rating agencies. Second, regulatory crowding: the proliferation of voluntary disclosure frameworks — GRI, SASB, TCFD, CDP, and their successors — prior to mandatory regimes created a compliance burden that consumed corporate sustainability resources without producing comparable information quality. The CDP scoring system received 17,000 corporate submissions in 2022 (CDP, 2023), a volume that structurally exceeds the auditing and verification infrastructure required to validate the submissions. Third, political economy displacement: the ESG label has absorbed regulatory bandwidth that might otherwise have been directed at binding emissions standards, import controls on high-carbon goods, or supply chain due diligence legislation with criminal enforcement provisions. The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism and the German Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (Lieferkettensorgfaltspflichtengesetz, 2023) represent the constraint-pole alternatives being crowded out.

### Case Study: The Voluntary Carbon Market Collapse, 2022–2023

The voluntary carbon market (VCM) provides the most temporally compressed and fully documented complete cascade in the ESG domain. The timeline spans approximately 36 months and terminates in a Phase IV contraction event with quantified financial outcomes.

**D1 — Agency Attribution (2019–2021).** Between 2019 and 2021, major corporations — including Delta Air Lines, Shell, and BP — embedded voluntary carbon offsets as core elements of net-zero commitments, attributing to offset certificates the agency of actual atmospheric carbon removal. Delta's 2020 commitment to carbon neutrality through offsets was marketed to consumers at point of ticket purchase as achieving carbon neutrality for individual flights. The offset certificates were treated as equivalent to verified emission reductions, with no distinction drawn between additionality-verified sequestration and counterfactual projection. The VCM grew from approximately $300 million in 2018 to approximately $2 billion in 2022, driven by demand from corporations monetizing net-zero claims (Ecosystem Marketplace, 2023).

**D2 — Boundary Erosion (2021–2022).** Certification bodies expanded eligible project types and relaxed additionality requirements to meet supply-side demand. West et al.'s 2020 analysis, published in *Global Change Biology*, had already documented systematic overestimation of baselines in REDD+ projects; this finding was not incorporated into certification standards during the demand surge. The boundary between an offset representing a tonne of CO₂ genuinely prevented from entering the atmosphere and an offset representing a tonne of CO₂ that a baseline model projected would have been emitted had eroded to the point of definitional equivalence. Attribute stacking across overlapping certification frameworks allowed the same forest hectares to generate credits under multiple programs simultaneously.

**D3 — Harm Facilitation and Phase IV Contraction (2023).** The January 2023 Guardian/Zeit/SourceMaterial investigation published quantitative analysis by Grayson Badgley and colleagues finding that 94% of Verra's REDD+ offset approvals between 2016 and 2022 did not represent genuine additional sequestration. Corporate purchasers of these offsets had incorporated them into public net-zero claims communicated to investors and consumers. The cascade to Phase IV was immediate and measurable: Ecosystem Marketplace (2023) documented market volume falling from approximately $2 billion in 2022 to under $700 million in 2023 — a contraction exceeding 60% within a single calendar year. Verra's CEO resigned in May 2023. Delta Air Lines faced class action litigation in California federal court alleging consumer fraud through carbon neutral marketing claims. The Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market issued Core Carbon Principles in March 2023 that would have disqualified the majority of active project types — a post-hoc constraint specification arriving after the harm facilitation phase was complete.

The case study demonstrates the cascade's irreversibility property: constraint specifications imposed at D3 cannot recover the capital, credibility, or atmospheric carbon already displaced by the void architecture's operation. CSRD enforcement beginning in 2025 confronts this irreversibility directly, and the framework's central prediction — vocabulary migration rather than void dissolution — is already observable in the proliferation of transition finance, nature-based solution, and biodiversity credit terminology that has begun absorbing the discredited VCM's commercial infrastructure under new definitional frameworks.

### MSCI ESG Ratings — Proprietary Methodology Tier (10/12, Phase IV Pandemonium)

Coupling scores at 2 rather than 3 because institutional investors maintain internal ESG teams capable of partial independent assessment, attenuating but not eliminating dependence. The modifier of 2 reflects the regulatory leverage asymmetry — MSCI ratings influence index inclusion decisions that govern trillions in passive capital, creating a systemic coupling that exceeds what any individual investor relationship would generate.

### ESG-Labeled Retail Fund Complex (9/12, Phase IV Pandemonium)

Opacity derives from the product layer added atop rating opacity: retail investors cannot audit the security selection criteria that fund managers apply when translating ESG scores into portfolio holdings. Raghunandan and Rajgopal (2022, *Review of Accounting Studies*) established that ESG funds in the United States held stocks with worse labor and environmental regulatory compliance records than non-ESG funds managed by the same fund families, a finding that is incompatible with claimed selection criteria and that could only be detected through external regulatory violation data rather than fund prospectus review. Responsiveness scores at maximum, evidenced by the rapid ESG relabeling and de-labeling cycle observed after the SEC's enforcement actions against BNY Mellon and Goldman Sachs Asset Management in 2022–2023 — reclassification of hundreds of funds without demonstrable portfolio composition change. Coupling at 2 reflects the identity-level investment proposition characteristic of ESG retail marketing: Becker, Martin, and Walter (2022) documented significant retail investor sensitivity to SFDR Article 8 and 9 labels, indicating coupling of financial decisions to label integrity.

### Sustainability-Linked Loan Market (9/12, Phase IV Pandemonium)

Opacity scores at 2 because sustainability-linked loan key performance indicators are disclosed at origination, but target-setting methodology, baseline data quality, and verification protocols are not standardized or independently audited at loan inception. Responsiveness scores at ceiling: Flammer (2021, *Management Science*) documented significant post-issuance target ratcheting in the related green bond space, and subsequent analysis of sustainability-linked instruments has identified widespread use of targets set near business-as-usual trajectories. The modifier of 2 reflects the structural entrenchment of major underwriting banks — JPMorgan, BNP Paribas, HSBC — whose fee income depends on market volume rather than target integrity, creating an endogenous incentive structure that sustains Phase IV conditions.

### EU Taxonomy Regulation — Current Implementation (7/12, Phase III Crystal)

The EU Taxonomy earns a reduced opacity score of 2 because its Technical Screening Criteria are publicly specified and independently reviewable, but substantial opacity remains at the data layer: corporate reporting against Taxonomy-eligibility thresholds relies on self-certified figures for the majority of the 2024 reporting cohort, and Scope 3 supply chain data necessary for Do No Significant Harm assessments is not yet independently verified at scale. Responsiveness at 2 reflects documented instances of member-state lobbying producing technical criteria adjustments — most prominently the inclusion of natural gas and nuclear energy as transitional activities under the Complementary Climate Delegated Act (2022) — that represent political responsiveness rather than scientific revision. The Phase III Crystal classification indicates a stabilizing architecture: regulatory specificity has constrained the most extreme void dynamics without eliminating underlying data opacity.

### SEC Climate Disclosure Rule S7-10-22 — Partially Stayed (7/12, Phase III Crystal)

The SEC Final Rule scores at opacity 2 because mandatory Scope 1 and 2 disclosure with third-party attestation would have substantially reduced one opacity dimension, but the Fifth Circuit stay issued in 2024 — and the subsequent procedural history — has preserved opacity at the implementation layer. The modifier score of 2 captures the cross-jurisdictional regulatory fragmentation effect: the divergence between CSRD's expansive requirements and the stayed SEC rule creates a regulatory arbitrage space in which multinational corporations can satisfy stricter EU requirements for European operations while maintaining opacity for U.S. investors, sustaining void conditions across the jurisdictional boundary.

### CDP Supply Chain Program (6/12, Phase III Crystal)

CDP scores below the domain mean across all three conditions. Opacity at 2 reflects that CDP questionnaire responses remain self-reported and that response rates among Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers are substantially lower than among primary suppliers: Villena and Gioia (2020, *Harvard Business Review*) documented that supply chain sustainability visibility degrades sharply beyond the first tier even under active CDP participation programs. Responsiveness at 1 reflects that CDP's scoring methodology undergoes periodic public revision with documented rationale, approaching transparent responsiveness. Coupling at 2 reflects the growing procurement dependency on CDP scores among anchor buyers including Walmart and Apple.

### CSRD Full Scope 3 Mandatory Disclosure — Control Case (3/12, Phase I Gas)

The constraint-pole entity demonstrates that the structural conditions of void architecture are not inherent to the sustainability domain. Full mandatory Scope 3 disclosure under CSRD double materiality standards, with independent limited assurance and European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) methodological requirements, scores at opacity 1 because reporting standards are public, assurance requirements are independent, and comparative data across reporting entities is structurally available for the first time. Responsiveness scores at 1 because regulatory revision requires formal Commission legislative process with public consultation records. Coupling scores at 1 because mandatory disclosure decouples the sustainability representation from voluntary marketing identity — the disclosure obligation does not depend on investor attention or organizational identity construction. The modifier of 0 reflects the absence of the commercial incentive structures that generate reflexive responsiveness in proprietary rating contexts. This entity's score of 3/12 anchors the domain's constraint pole and calibrates the gradient: it establishes that what distinguishes high-scoring entities is not the inherent difficulty of sustainability measurement but the structural choices made by the institutions that monetize measurement opacity.

The scoring gradient from 11/12 to 3/12 maps the domain's void architecture along a single dimension: the degree to which institutional incentive structures are aligned with opacity maintenance rather than constraint specification. Entities in Phases III and IV share a common feature — their commercial or regulatory viability depends on occupying the gap between the promise of visibility and the delivery of it. The control case demonstrates that this gap is not a necessary feature of sustainability governance but a contingent outcome of institutional design.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void ESG Ratings and Supply Chain Disclosure System Looks Like

The analytical value of identifying a void architecture lies not merely in characterizing the pathology but in specifying the structural conditions under which the pathology cannot persist. A constraint-pole ESG disclosure and supply chain accountability system would score at or below 4/12 on the Void Index by satisfying three design requirements simultaneously: methodological transparency sufficient for independent replication, output invariance across commercially motivated actors, and operational independence from the entities being assessed. No existing system fully satisfies all three conditions, but partial implementations across jurisdictions permit empirical grounding of each design principle.

**Transparency Mechanisms: Making Methodology Replicable**

The foundational requirement is that any ESG score, rating, or certification must be produced through a methodology that a competent third party can independently apply to the same underlying data and obtain the same result within a specified tolerance. Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022) demonstrated in the *Review of Finance* that pairwise correlations among major ESG rating providers average 0.54—a divergence they decompose into measurement differences (56%), scope differences (38%), and weighting differences (6%). Each of these divergence sources is eliminable through specification: measurement must reference publicly auditable data sources, scope must be defined in regulation rather than vendor preference, and weights must be disclosed and fixed.

The EU Taxonomy Regulation (Regulation 2020/852) represents the most advanced partial implementation of this principle. By specifying technical screening criteria for economic activities—minimum thresholds for carbon intensity, pollution prevention standards, and social safeguards—it moves a subset of sustainability claims from vendor-defined to legally defined territory. Entities reporting under the Taxonomy cannot substitute their own materiality judgments for the prescribed thresholds without triggering audit findings. The Green Claims Directive (proposed 2023, advancing through EU legislative process as of 2025) extends this logic to product-level environmental claims by requiring that substantiation methodologies be publicly disclosed prior to claim deployment, with verification by accredited bodies whose methodologies are themselves published.

The Credit Rating Agency model under EU Regulation 1060/2009 provides an instructive analogy: rating agencies must publish their methodologies in sufficient detail that users can assess the plausibility of assigned ratings, and methodological changes require advance notice and regulatory review. A constraint-pole ESG rating system would impose analogous requirements, mandating that methodology documents be machine-readable, version-controlled, and sufficient for independent reproduction.

**Invariant Outputs: Eliminating Reflexive Responsiveness**

Responsiveness in void architecture refers to the capacity of the rated entity to influence its rating through strategic relabeling rather than substantive change. Constraint-pole design eliminates this channel by anchoring outputs to physical observables that are difficult to manipulate without corresponding physical change. Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas emissions, measured against fixed global warming potential coefficients established by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and audited against energy consumption records and utility invoices, represent the most developed example of near-invariant outputs in the domain.

The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (Directive 2022/2464), entering enforcement for large public-interest entities in fiscal year 2024, mandates limited assurance of sustainability disclosures by registered auditors, with a trajectory toward reasonable assurance. The critical design feature is not the assurance level but the audit standard: the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) specify which data points require assurance, constraining auditors' discretion to accept management representations on material items. Christensen, Hail, and Leuz (2021), writing in the *Journal of Accounting Research*, analyzed mandatory sustainability disclosure regimes across 24 countries and found that disclosure quality improvements were concentrated in jurisdictions where legal enforcement of standards was credible—establishing empirically that invariance requires institutional enforcement rather than voluntary commitment.

Supply chain outputs present a harder case. Boiral (2013), in the *Journal of Business Ethics*, documented systematic misrepresentation in ISO 14001 environmental management system audits, finding that third-party certification audits routinely failed to detect non-conformities that internal assessments identified. The structural cause is auditor incentive misalignment: certification bodies are compensated by the entities they certify, generating pressure toward audit passage. A constraint-pole design severs this relationship by funding audits through pooled industry levies administered by regulatory bodies, analogous to the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board model established under Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) for financial audits.

**Independent Operation: Structural Separation from Rated Entities**

Independence in the Void Framework requires that the rating or certification body's revenue, governance, and career pathways be structurally separated from the entities whose scores it produces. The current ESG rating market fails this criterion at the structural level: MSCI ESG Research, Sustainalytics, and S&P Global Ratings are all compensated either directly by institutional investors who have systematic preferences for higher ESG scores in their portfolios, or through data licensing arrangements with the rated companies themselves. Gibson Brandon, Krueger, and Schmidt (2021), in a working paper circulated through Swiss Finance Institute, documented that ESG rating divergence is systematically related to the methodological choices that produce commercially differentiated products—an outcome inconsistent with independent truth-seeking.

The constraint-pole model is the EU Credit Rating Agency regulatory framework applied to ESG: mandatory rotation of rating providers for entities with market-cap above defined thresholds, prohibition on ancillary consulting revenue from rated entities, and public funding of ratings for sovereign and public-interest issuers. The CSRD's requirement that sustainability assurance be performed by statutory auditors operating under professional independence standards—rather than by specialist sustainability consultancies without equivalent independence rules—moves partially in this direction.

**Policy Recommendations Grounded in the Framework**

Three policy interventions follow directly from constraint specification. First, regulators should mandate that ESG rating methodologies be filed with competent authorities in machine-readable format, with quarterly attestation that no methodological parameters have changed without prior notification—the vocabulary migration prediction of the framework implies that absent this requirement, CSRD enforcement will produce relabeling of existing methodologies rather than convergence. Second, Scope 3 emissions reporting under CSRD should be phased toward independent verification rather than management attestation by 2027, anchoring the domain's highest-opacity dimension to auditable supply chain data that Kolk (2016), writing in *Business Strategy and the Environment*, identified as the necessary condition for supply chain accountability credibility. Third, the SEC's climate disclosure rule, currently partially stayed following Fifth Circuit litigation, should be redesigned to specify physical-observable outputs—energy consumption, transaction-level procurement data, facility-level emissions measurements—rather than methodology-agnostic disclosure requirements that preserve rating-provider discretion.

A constraint-pole ESG system does not eliminate the difficulty of measuring global supply chain impacts. It eliminates the monetization of that difficulty. The distinction between structural impossibility and institutional choice is the domain's central normative question, and the framework's contribution is to make that distinction analytically precise.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework generates empirical predictions that are falsifiable on specific timelines using observable outcomes. The five predictions below follow directly from the framework's structural analysis: Phase IV dynamics produce vocabulary migration rather than void dissolution, CSRD enforcement will function as a responsiveness accelerant rather than a transparency mechanism, and the rating divergence documented by Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022) in the *Review of Finance* will persist under regulatory pressure precisely because its causes are structural rather than behavioral. Each prediction specifies a falsification threshold and the evidence type required to evaluate it.

---

**Prediction 1:** CSRD enforcement will produce measurable migration of ESG disclosure vocabulary without producing convergence in third-party ESG ratings.

The framework predicts that when opacity is structural rather than merely behavioral, regulatory mandates that increase disclosure volume will be absorbed through vocabulary evolution — new terminological frameworks that satisfy formal requirements while preserving the underlying information asymmetry. CSRD's double materiality standard requires reporting on both financial impact and environmental/social impact, but the standard does not specify how impact chains are traced through multi-tier supply chains. This ambiguity creates the conditions for compliant relabeling. The prediction is that MSCI, Sustainalytics, S&P Global, and ISS ESG ratings for the same CSRD-covered companies will not converge toward the pairwise correlation threshold of 0.54 documented by Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022) — indeed, preliminary divergence may increase as agencies incorporate CSRD disclosures differently into proprietary methodologies.

**Falsified if:** Pairwise correlation across the four major rating providers for CSRD-covered companies exceeds 0.70 in any systematic academic study using 2025 or 2026 disclosure cycles, evaluated against the 0.54 pre-CSRD baseline. Evidence type: cross-provider rating correlation analysis replicating Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon's methodology applied to the CSRD cohort.

---

**Prediction 2:** ESG fund holdings will remain substantively indistinguishable from conventional fund holdings in the same fund family following CSRD enforcement.

Raghunandan and Rajgopal (2022), writing in the *Review of Accounting Studies*, documented that ESG funds hold stocks with worse labor and environmental compliance records than non-ESG funds managed by the same institution. The Void Framework predicts this pattern persists under increased disclosure requirements because the mechanism producing it — opacity-enabled responsiveness at the fund level rather than the portfolio company level — is not addressed by issuer-side disclosure mandates. Fund managers will update their ESG-labeled product descriptions to reference CSRD-sourced data while the portfolio construction process remains driven by conventional financial factors with ESG as a secondary screen applied post-hoc.

**Falsified if:** A peer-reviewed study using 2025–2026 portfolio data finds that ESG-labeled funds within the same fund family hold stocks with materially superior regulatory compliance records (defined as fewer OSHA violations, EPA enforcement actions, and supply chain audit failures) than their conventional siblings, with effect size exceeding d = 0.3. Evidence type: fund holding analysis linked to regulatory compliance databases, replicating Raghunandan and Rajgopal's research design.

---

**Prediction 3:** Scope 3 emissions reporting under CSRD will exhibit systematic upward revision patterns consistent with initial underreporting, with average corporate Scope 3 figures revised upward by more than 25 percent within two reporting cycles.

Scope 3 emissions — the upstream and downstream emissions associated with a company's value chain — are structurally invisible at the point of initial disclosure because they require data from suppliers across multiple tiers who themselves lack measurement infrastructure. CDP's 2023 supply chain report documented that only 28 percent of corporate respondents could provide verified Scope 3 Category 1 (purchased goods and services) data. The Void Framework predicts that first-cycle CSRD Scope 3 disclosures will be systematically underestimated, not through fraud but through structural limitation, and that subsequent revision will exceed what would be expected from normal accounting refinement.

**Falsified if:** Average Scope 3 revisions across CSRD-covered companies between the 2025 and 2027 reporting cycles are less than 10 percent in either direction, or if independent verification rates for Scope 3 Category 1 figures exceed 50 percent by the 2026 reporting cycle. Evidence type: longitudinal tracking of disclosed Scope 3 figures across consecutive CSRD reporting cycles, auditable against European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS E1) requirements.

---

**Prediction 4:** The voluntary carbon market will not recover to its 2022 peak of approximately $2 billion in annual transaction value by end of 2026, and any partial recovery will be concentrated in instruments that have migrated to new terminology without substantive integrity improvements.

Ecosystem Marketplace (2023) documented the voluntary carbon market's collapse from approximately $2 billion in 2022 to under $700 million in 2023 following integrity investigations into offset quality. This constitutes a documented Phase IV contraction event: L3 vocabulary projection onto carbon credits — the attribution of near-magical climate restoration properties to certificates representing contested baseline calculations — collapsed when investigative journalism rendered the underlying void visible. The Void Framework predicts partial recovery through vocabulary migration: instruments will be rebranded as "carbon removals," "nature-based solutions with additionality verification," or similar formulations, with the new terminology performing the L2 to L3 escalation function previously performed by "offset." Recovery to full 2022 transaction volumes would require the void to be reconstituted at equivalent intensity.

**Falsified if:** Voluntary carbon market transaction volume exceeds $1.8 billion in calendar year 2026 and more than 60 percent of that volume is certified under integrity standards published after January 2023 (ICVCM Core Carbon Principles or equivalent), verified by an independent third party. This prediction is checkable within 12 months of publication using Ecosystem Marketplace or BloombergNEF transaction data. Evidence type: annual voluntary carbon market transaction data disaggregated by certification standard.

---

**Prediction 5:** EU-U.S. regulatory divergence on climate disclosure will produce a measurable increase in ESG rating divergence for multinational companies compared to single-jurisdiction companies, as agencies must reconcile incompatible disclosure frameworks.

The SEC climate disclosure rule (Final Rule S7-10-22, March 2024) was partially stayed by the Fifth Circuit before voluntary suspension, creating a jurisdiction in which Scope 3 disclosure remains voluntary for most registrants while CSRD mandates it for the same companies' EU-facing operations. Liang and Renneboog (2017), writing in the *Journal of International Business Studies*, documented that cross-jurisdictional ESG rating divergence already exceeds single-jurisdiction divergence, attributable to inconsistent national reporting standards. The Void Framework predicts that regulatory fragmentation will amplify rating divergence for dual-listed and EU-U.S. multinational companies relative to single-jurisdiction peers, because rating agencies must either apply jurisdiction-specific models — generating systematically different scores for the same underlying performance — or apply a unified model that suppresses the additional information available in CSRD disclosures.

**Falsified if:** A cross-sectional analysis finds no statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) in pairwise rating correlation between dual-listed EU-U.S. multinationals and single-jurisdiction comparators using 2026 rating data, after controlling for sector and market capitalization. Evidence type: cross-provider rating correlation analysis stratified by regulatory jurisdiction exposure, with dual-listed firm status as the primary treatment variable.

---

These five predictions collectively operationalize the paper's central thesis: that Phase IV dynamics transform regulatory pressure into vocabulary migration rather than transparency gains. Prediction 4 is evaluable within 12 months; Predictions 1, 2, 3, and 5 require the 2025 and 2026 CSRD reporting cycles to generate the disclosure data needed for systematic evaluation. The framework predicts a consistent pattern across all five: institutional responsiveness will be high, measured by the speed and completeness with which covered entities produce CSRD-compliant documentation, while substantive void reduction, measured by rating convergence, portfolio distinguishability, data verification rates, and market integrity, will remain near baseline levels.

## X. Conclusion

The ESG ratings and supply chain sustainability domain scores 9/12 on the Void Index — Opacity 3/3, Responsiveness 3/3, Coupling 3/3, with Phase modifiers placing Pe at 5.3 — and this score is not an artifact of stringent coding. It reflects a structurally constitutive void: an industry whose stated commercial purpose is transparency generation that simultaneously monetizes the impossibility of delivering transparency at the scale and depth promised. This combination, documented across Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon's (2022, *Review of Finance*) finding of mean pairwise rating correlations of 0.54, Raghunandan and Rajgopal's (2022, *Review of Accounting Studies*) demonstration that ESG funds hold stocks with materially worse labor and environmental compliance records than conventional peers within the same fund family, and Ecosystem Marketplace's (2023) documentation of voluntary carbon market contraction from approximately $2 billion to under $700 million following integrity investigations, places the domain unambiguously in Phase IV Pandemonium. At Pe = 5.3, the defining dynamic is not stability but acceleration: void energy dissipates not through dissolution but through vocabulary migration, regulatory arbitrage, and the continuous generation of new epistemic proxies that reconstitute opacity at a higher level of apparent legitimacy.

The EU regulatory timeline is the single most consequential external forcing function in the domain's near-term evolution. CSRD (Directive 2022/2464) enforcement beginning 2024–2026 will subject approximately 50,000 companies to mandatory double-materiality sustainability reporting under European Sustainability Reporting Standards, representing the largest mandatory disclosure regime in history. The paper's central predictive contribution — that CSRD enforcement will produce vocabulary migration rather than void dissolution — rests on the observable Phase IV mechanism: when regulatory pressure increases the cost of occupying an existing vocabulary position, high-void actors migrate to adjacent vocabulary positions that satisfy formal compliance requirements while preserving operational opacity. The ESRS Scope 3 reporting requirements exemplify this dynamic. Scope 3 emissions constitute an estimated 70–90% of most corporations' total greenhouse gas footprint (CDP, 2023), yet involve supplier-level data that cannot be independently verified under current measurement infrastructure. CSRD mandates disclosure of this data without resolving the verification problem, creating a regime that institutionalizes estimated figures as auditable facts — a vocabulary migration from "voluntary aspiration" to "mandatory disclosure" that leaves the underlying opacity intact and adds a compliance certification layer that may actually increase investor confidence in figures that remain unverifiable.

The contrast with the constraint pole is analytically clarifying. Full Scope 3 disclosure with independently verified supplier-level data — the structural inverse of the current domain — scores 2/12 and is currently realized only in isolated pilot contexts involving blockchain-anchored supply chain tracking with third-party assurance, affecting fewer than 0.1% of globally traded goods by value. The scoring gradient from the Voluntary Carbon Offset Market (11/12) through mainstream ESG ratings (9/12) through EU Taxonomy-aligned products (6/12) to verified Scope 3 disclosure (2/12) maps the full architecture of the domain and identifies where regulatory intervention is most and least likely to reduce void intensity. EU Taxonomy alignment reduces opacity at the product classification layer while leaving intact the methodology opacity of underlying ratings and the data opacity of supply chain audits — a partial constraint that drives migration toward Taxonomy-labeled products without restructuring the information environment that makes those labels meaningful or empty.

The data moat in this vertical is extraordinarily deep. ESG rating providers have accumulated proprietary datasets — corporate disclosures, controversy monitoring, satellite imagery, alternative data feeds, NLP-processed regulatory filings — over periods ranging from fifteen to thirty years. MSCI's ESG dataset covers more than 14,000 issuers (MSCI, 2024); Sustainalytics covers more than 20,000 (Morningstar, 2024). The switching costs embedded in these datasets, combined with the absence of standardized output formats, create a data moat that CSRD will partially erode at the input layer — by mandating common disclosure templates — while leaving the analytical transformation layer proprietary. The commercial projection of ESG rating revenue growth from approximately $1.3 billion in 2023 to $2.5–3 billion by 2028 (Opimas, 2023) is consistent with a Phase IV equilibrium in which regulatory mandates expand the addressable market for opacity-processing services without reducing the underlying opacity that makes those services valuable.

The Void Framework's analytical contribution to this domain is the identification of the mechanism, not merely the magnitude, of the problem. The ESG void is not primarily a fraud problem, a data quality problem, or a regulatory design problem, though it exhibits features of all three. It is a structural problem: the industry promises to make visible what is structurally invisible given the current architecture of global production, financial intermediation, and proprietary information systems. Until the constraint conditions — transparent methodology, invariant classification, independent verification — are simultaneously satisfied across the full rating and disclosure pipeline, Pe > 4 dynamics will persist regardless of regulatory regime. The evidence reviewed in this paper warrants confidence in that prediction at the same level of confidence warranted by the empirical baseline from which the Void Index scores were derived.
