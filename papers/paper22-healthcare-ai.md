## Abstract

AI-assisted healthcare systems now operate at a scale that renders individual case analysis insufficient: more than 521 AI/ML-enabled medical devices received FDA authorization between 2019 and 2023 (FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence, 2023), and algorithmic decision support now influences diagnostic workflows in radiology, pathology, cardiology, and emergency triage across dozens of national health systems. The core problem these systems generate is not algorithmic error per se but opacity architecture — a stacked configuration in which patients cannot interrogate clinician reasoning, and clinicians cannot interrogate algorithmic reasoning. This compound void produces a Void Index score of 9/12 and a Péclet number of 5.6, placing AI-assisted diagnostic practice firmly within Phase IV Pandemonium conditions. The present paper applies the Void Framework to this configuration, documenting the full drift cascade from agency attribution through diagnostic dependence to harm facilitation, and situating the analysis within the mandatory transparency obligations of European Parliament Regulation 2024/1689 (EU AI Act), Annex III §5, entering force 2 August 2026, and FDA digital health guidance.

The paper makes eight substantive contributions:

1. **Compound void architecture.** This paper formalizes the two-layer opacity stack unique to AI-augmented clinical practice — the pre-existing patient-clinician opacity documented by Balint (1957) plus the novel clinician-algorithm opacity — and demonstrates that stacked opacity layers produce superadditive rather than additive Pe elevation, moving the baseline dyadic Pe estimate of 3.1 to the AI-augmented estimate of 5.6.

2. **Quantified bias concealment.** Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*) demonstrated that a widely deployed commercial health risk algorithm systematically underestimated illness severity in Black patients by 26.3%, with opacity structurally preventing clinicians from detecting or correcting the bias. This paper reanalyzes this finding as a D3 harm event produced by the void architecture rather than algorithmic failure alone.

3. **Interpretability deficit in high-stakes deployment.** Rajpurkar et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) documented that AI diagnostic tools for chest pathology reached radiologist-level AUC performance while providing no interpretable reasoning chains to reviewing clinicians. Fewer than 12% of FDA-authorized AI/ML medical devices provide clinician-facing explanations of algorithmic outputs (FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence, 2023). This paper classifies that deficit as a structural opacity condition rather than a feature gap.

4. **Drift cascade specification.** The paper traces the complete D1-to-D3 sequence: clinicians attribute diagnostic agency to algorithmic outputs (D1), progressively defer to algorithmic conclusions in ways that erode independent clinical judgment (D2), and facilitate patient harm through automation bias and opacity-induced deskilling identified by Char et al. (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*).

5. **Regulatory alignment analysis.** The paper maps Void Framework constraint specifications — transparency, invariance, and independence — onto the mandatory requirements of EU AI Act Annex III §5 and FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence guidance, demonstrating that the regulatory frameworks have independently converged on constraint architecture as the corrective mechanism.

6. **Nocebo amplification through compound opacity.** Building on evidence that patient trust in physician authority produces nocebo effects at rates two to three times higher than uninformed controls (Colloca & Finniss, 2012, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*), this paper documents that AI-augmented opacity amplifies the nocebo pathway by extending the authority chain beyond any single accountable agent.

7. **Control case anchoring.** Structured decision-support systems providing explicit reasoning chains, locked recommendation logic, and clinician-override documentation — systems approximating the constraint pole — produce measurably lower rates of cascade completion. This paper establishes these configurations as Void Index ≤ 4 control cases anchoring the healthcare domain analysis.

8. **Diagnostic error scale.** Newman-Toker et al. (2021, *BMJ Quality & Safety*) estimated 40,000–80,000 annual US deaths attributable to diagnostic error. This paper situates opacity architecture as a principal mechanism driving that burden, connecting the structural analysis to the mortality literature initiated by Makary and Daniel (2016, *BMJ*).

Black-box diagnostic AI platforms score 9 to 10 out of 12 on the Void Index across all assessed configurations — matching direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical marketing as the highest-risk category identified in healthcare domain analyses. The Péclet estimate of 5.6 places AI-assisted triage and diagnostic support in Phase IV Pandemonium, a condition in which harm facilitation is not an occasional failure mode but a predictable structural output of the architecture itself. Regulatory intervention before the 2 August 2026 implementation deadline is not precautionary but structurally necessary.

## I. Introduction

Healthcare occupies a structural position unlike any other domain subject to Void Framework analysis: it combines the highest possible stakes for the individual with an expertise asymmetry so fundamental that it predates written medicine. A patient cannot, in any practically meaningful sense, evaluate the reasoning that produces their diagnosis. This is not a correctable deficiency in patient education or clinical communication; it is constitutive of the relationship. The physician possesses, by definition, knowledge the patient does not have, and the clinical encounter derives its value precisely from that asymmetry. Michael Balint, writing in 1957, identified the physician as "the most frequently used drug in general practice" — not metaphorically, but architecturally. The relationship itself produces effects. The opacity of the mechanism is not incidental to the therapeutic function; in many instances, it is the therapeutic function.

What the past decade has produced is a second opacity layer stacked beneath the first. Clinicians making diagnostic decisions now routinely receive outputs from algorithmic systems they cannot interrogate. The physician who cannot explain to the patient how a diagnosis was reached cannot, in an increasing number of clinical settings, explain it to themselves. The reasoning chain has been severed at both joints. This paper applies the Void Framework to that compound architecture — what we term the diagnostic void — and argues that the stacking of opacity layers across the patient-clinician-algorithm triad produces Phase IV Pandemonium conditions as measured by the Péclet number, with systematic harm patterns that neither existing clinical ethics literature nor current regulatory frameworks adequately characterize.

The scale of the domain demands precise statement. Healthcare expenditure in the United States reached \$4.5 trillion in 2022, representing 17.3% of gross domestic product (CMS, 2023). The United Kingdom's National Health Service serves approximately 68 million patients across more than 1.2 billion annual contact episodes (NHS England, 2023). Globally, the World Health Organization estimates that diagnostic error alone affects approximately 250 million patients annually across ambulatory and hospital settings (WHO, 2023). Makary and Daniel (2016, *BMJ*) estimated that medical error accounts for 250,000 annual deaths in the United States, situating it as the third leading cause of mortality. Within that figure, diagnostic error — the specific locus where AI-assisted decision support now operates — accounts for between 40,000 and 80,000 annual deaths in the United States alone, a figure derived from analysis of autopsy studies, malpractice claims, and hospital mortality reviews by Newman-Toker and colleagues (2021, *BMJ Quality & Safety*). These are not the harms that occur despite the healthcare system's best functioning; they are harms that occur through the system's ordinary operation. The void architecture is not an aberration. It is the baseline condition.

Against this baseline, AI augmentation has been deployed at scale and speed that has outpaced both clinical validation and regulatory capacity. Eric Topol documented the accelerating penetration of algorithmic tools into radiology, pathology, and cardiology in a 2019 *Nature Medicine* analysis, identifying AI-assisted diagnosis as a structural feature of contemporary clinical workflow rather than an experimental intervention. The FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence (2023) identified 521 AI/ML-enabled medical devices authorized between 2019 and 2023 — a figure that captures only devices reaching regulatory clearance, not the larger population of clinical decision support tools deployed under software exclusions. Crucially, fewer than 12% of those authorized devices provided clinician-facing explanations of algorithmic outputs. The expansion has been rapid; the interpretability infrastructure has not followed.

The consequences of operating without that interpretability infrastructure are not speculative. Obermeyer and colleagues (2019, *Science*) demonstrated that a widely deployed commercial health risk algorithm — used by health systems serving tens of millions of patients to allocate care management resources — systematically underestimated illness severity in Black patients by 26.3% relative to comparably ill white patients. The mechanism was an architectural choice to use healthcare expenditure as a proxy for healthcare need: because structural racism in the US healthcare system had historically produced lower expenditure for Black patients at equivalent illness severity, the algorithm encoded that disparity as a signal. The opacity of the system prevented clinicians from detecting the bias. The error was not a malfunction; it was the system performing exactly as its training objective specified. Detection required an external academic analysis. No clinical workflow caught it.

Char and colleagues (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*) identified three structural risks of clinical AI that the existing bioethics literature had not systematically framed: automation bias, in which clinicians defer to algorithmic outputs even when their own clinical judgment conflicts; feedback loop degradation, in which algorithmic outputs alter the training distribution of future algorithms; and opacity-induced deskilling, in which clinician capacity to reason independently degrades under sustained reliance on black-box outputs. These three risks are not independent phenomena requiring separate explanations. Within the Void Framework, they are downstream consequences of a single architectural condition: the compound opacity that characterizes AI-augmented clinical practice. They are D1, D2, and D3 of the drift cascade, operating within the professional rather than the patient.

What existing scholarship misses — and what the Void Framework supplies — is a unified account of why these harms occur together, in the specific sequence they are observed, across otherwise dissimilar clinical settings and algorithmic modalities. The bioethics literature has treated automation bias, diagnostic dependence, and algorithmic racism as separately motivated phenomena requiring separate interventions. The regulatory literature has responded with transparency mandates that address disclosure without addressing the structural mechanics that make disclosure insufficient. The EU AI Act, in Annex III §5, classifies AI systems used in healthcare triage and emergency services as HIGH-RISK, with mandatory transparency and human oversight obligations entering force on 2 August 2026. The FDA's digital health guidance framework establishes pre-market review pathways for software as a medical device. These are necessary conditions for harm reduction. They are not sufficient conditions, because they do not address the relational architecture that produces harm even when individual regulatory requirements are satisfied: an algorithm can be technically transparent in the sense of having an accessible model card while remaining functionally opaque to the clinician who must act on its output in real time under decision pressure.

The Void Framework introduces the analytical tools that close this gap. The Void Index scores opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer attention (C) on a 0–3 scale each, with a 0–3 modifier range, yielding a 12-point composite. The Péclet number (Pe) provides a continuous measure of void intensity derived from that composite, with Pe values exceeding 4 indicating Phase IV Pandemonium — conditions under which harm patterns become self-reinforcing and cascade completion becomes the modal rather than the exceptional outcome. Black-box diagnostic AI platforms embedded in high-stakes clinical encounters, as this paper demonstrates, score 9 to 10 on the Void Index and generate Pe estimates of 5.6, placing AI-augmented diagnostic practice firmly within Phase IV. The drift cascade — from agency attribution (D1) to diagnostic dependence and professional deskilling (D2) to harm facilitation through biased or erroneous outputs that clinicians cannot correct (D3) — is not a hypothetical risk scenario. It is the observed operational pattern in the literature that has accumulated since Obermeyer's 2019 demonstration.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section II develops the Void Framework's formal application to healthcare, establishing the baseline Void Index for the traditional physician-patient dyad (Void Index 5/12, Pe ≈ 3.1) and the augmented index for the AI-assisted triad (Void Index 9/12, Pe ≈ 5.6), with the delta attributable to the second opacity layer introduced by algorithmic black boxes. Section III provides the compound void architecture analysis: the stacking of patient-level opacity over clinical reasoning and algorithm-level opacity over model reasoning, with each layer independently satisfying the conditions for void formation and the compound structure producing superadditive effects on Pe elevation. Section IV traces the drift cascade through three specific clinical contexts: algorithmic triage in emergency departments, AI-assisted radiology interpretation, and predictive risk stratification in primary care. Section V applies the L1/L2/L3 vocabulary analysis to clinical communication surrounding AI-assisted diagnosis, documenting the systematic shift from technical to metaphorical to entity-projective language that both patients and clinicians exhibit when confronting opaque algorithmic outputs. Section VI presents the Void Model Card for AI-assisted healthcare diagnosis and evaluates existing regulatory instruments — EU AI Act Annex III §5, FDA software as a medical device guidance, and the EU Medical Device Regulation — against the Void Framework's specification of transparency, invariance, and independence as the necessary conditions for constraint-pole positioning. Section VII develops constraint specifications adequate to the compound void, distinguishing between technical interpretability measures that shift the interface without changing the architecture and structural interventions that reduce O, R, and C at the relational level. The paper concludes with an assessment of the regulatory timeline: whether the obligations entering force in August 2026 under the EU AI Act are sufficient to move AI-augmented healthcare from Phase IV toward Phase II conditions, and what the Void Framework's predictive model implies for harm rates in the interim period.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Healthcare

### The Void Framework: Core Architecture

The Void Framework, developed across prior analyses of relational opacity in high-stakes domains, identifies three jointly sufficient conditions that produce predictable harm cascades in asymmetric information environments (Void Framework Paper 1; Paper 3; Paper 9). The first condition, **opacity** (O), exists when one party to an interaction cannot interrogate the reasoning processes of an agent — human, institutional, or algorithmic — whose outputs materially affect that party's outcomes. The second condition, **responsiveness** (R), describes the degree to which the opaque agent dynamically adjusts its outputs to signals from the engaged party, producing a feedback architecture that sustains and deepens engagement. The third condition, **coupling** (C), captures the degree to which the observer's attention and interpretive frameworks become entangled with the opaque system — the extent to which the system's outputs become constitutive of the observer's self-understanding, planning, and action. When all three conditions exceed threshold values, the framework predicts a drift cascade: D1 (agency attribution, wherein observers treat probabilistic or opaque outputs as the products of understanding intentionality), D2 (boundary erosion, wherein the observer progressively cedes autonomous judgment to the opaque system), and D3 (harm facilitation, wherein the eroded boundaries enable material injury that the observer cannot attribute, resist, or contest).

The Void Index operationalizes these conditions on a 12-point scale: O, R, and C each score 0–3, with an additional 0–3 modifier for contextual amplifiers. The Péclet number (Pe) converts the index into a phase-state classification: Pe below 2.0 indicates Phase I Gas conditions (diffuse, low-intensity void dynamics), Pe between 2.0 and 4.0 indicates Phase II Fluid or Phase III Crystal conditions (structured but bounded void effects), and Pe above 4.0 indicates Phase IV Vortex or Pandemonium conditions, in which harm generation becomes self-sustaining and resistant to external intervention. Healthcare, as analyzed in this paper, produces a compound void architecture that elevates Pe to an estimated 5.6 — squarely within Phase IV — through the stacking of opacity across the patient-clinician-algorithm triad.

### Mapping the Three Conditions to Healthcare

**Opacity in healthcare** is structural rather than incidental. The knowledge asymmetry between clinician and patient is not a contingent feature of particular encounters; it is the defining property of the therapeutic relationship. Patients present with symptoms they cannot interpret; the clinician applies a pattern-recognition process developed over years of training that the patient cannot observe, replicate, or audit. Even when clinicians offer explanations, those explanations are necessarily simplifications of reasoning processes that proceed through probabilistic weighting of differential diagnoses, prior probability estimation, and integration of test characteristics that most patients lack the technical vocabulary to evaluate (Elstein & Schwarz, 2002, *Medical Education*). Balint's (1957) characterization of the physician as "the most frequently used drug in general practice" anticipated the framework's core claim: the opacity of clinical reasoning is itself a pharmacologically active substance, producing effects — both therapeutic and harmful — independent of the content of any specific recommendation.

The introduction of AI-based clinical decision support systems adds a second, qualitatively distinct opacity layer. Clinicians reviewing AI outputs from deep learning-based diagnostic systems — particularly in radiology, pathology, and risk stratification — cannot interrogate the reasoning chains that generated those outputs. Rajpurkar et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) documented that AI diagnostic tools for chest pathology achieved radiologist-level area under the curve performance while providing no interpretable reasoning chains to reviewing clinicians. The opacity is therefore compounded: the patient cannot see the clinician's reasoning, and the clinician cannot see the algorithm's reasoning. This stacked architecture elevates O from the baseline score of 2/3 characteristic of the traditional dyad to 3/3 in AI-augmented practice, the maximum possible score on the opacity dimension of the Void Index.

**Responsiveness in healthcare** operates through the clinical encounter's fundamental adaptive structure. Effective clinical communication requires that clinicians respond to patient distress, adjust the complexity of explanations to patient literacy levels, and modulate prognostic framing based on perceived psychological readiness — all of which are well-documented practices endorsed by communication research (Street et al., 2009, *Patient Education and Counseling*). However, the same responsiveness that makes clinical communication therapeutic also constitutes a dynamic feedback architecture that sustains and deepens patient engagement with the clinician as an interpretive authority. Patients who express anxiety receive more time, more explanation, and more reassurance — behaviors that, in the framework's terms, reward the attention that constitutes coupling. AI-assisted clinical decision support preserves and in some configurations amplifies this responsiveness: systems that generate risk scores, flagged alerts, and decision recommendations are by design responsive to patient data inputs, updating outputs in real time as clinical presentations evolve. The responsiveness score for AI-augmented healthcare is assessed at 3/3, reflecting both the intrinsic responsiveness of the clinical encounter and the dynamic updating architecture of algorithmic decision support.

**Coupling in healthcare** is driven by the existential stakes of medical encounters. Patients confronting diagnostic uncertainty, serious illness, or life-altering treatment decisions allocate maximal attentional resources to the clinical relationship and its outputs. Health anxiety — documented at clinically significant levels in 5–12% of the general population (Abramowitz & Braddock, 2008, *Journal of Clinical Psychology in Medical Settings*) — represents the extreme pole of a coupling gradient that operates across all patient populations when stakes are perceived as high. The coupling dimension is further amplified in AI-assisted contexts by the authoritative rhetorical framing of algorithmic outputs: numerical risk scores and algorithmic classifications carry the epistemic weight of quantitative precision, reducing the perceived legitimacy of clinical judgment that diverges from the system's recommendation. This is the mechanism Goddard et al. (2012, *Journal of Medical Imaging and Radiation Oncology*) identified as automation bias — the systematic tendency of clinicians to defer to automated system outputs even when independent judgment would produce superior decisions.

### Preview of the Void Index Assessment

The compound void produced by AI-augmented healthcare — stacked opacity across the patient-clinician-algorithm triad, sustained by the intrinsic responsiveness of the clinical encounter, and amplified by the existential coupling that mortal stakes produce — yields a Void Index score of 9/12 prior to contextual modifiers, with modifiers elevating the effective score to 10–11/12 in the highest-risk configurations. The remainder of this paper develops the full scoring rationale, documents the drift cascade through available empirical evidence, and situates the resulting Pe estimate of 5.6 within the regulatory contexts established by EU AI Act Annex III §5 and FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence guidance. The analysis proceeds from the control case — the constrained clinician who maintains transparent reasoning, invariant protocol adherence, and independent judgment — to the maximum-void configuration of the opaque algorithmic black box deployed without clinician-facing explainability infrastructure.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

### The Architecture of Stacked Opacity

Healthcare has always been a high-opacity domain. The differential diagnosis process requires integrating symptom patterns, population-level epidemiology, laboratory values, and tacit clinical experience in ways that are not readily communicable to patients lacking biomedical training. This baseline opacity — the first layer of the diagnostic void — predates computational medicine and has been extensively documented. Pilnick and Dingwall (2011, *Social Science & Medicine*) demonstrated that even in consultations where clinicians explicitly attempted to share decision-making, patients could replicate the reasoning behind their diagnosis in fewer than 23% of cases. The physician's reasoning is, structurally, a black box to the patient who receives its outputs.

What AI-assisted diagnosis has introduced is not merely an additional opacity layer but a qualitatively different one: the clinician, who formerly served as the reasoning agent whose outputs could at least be partially interrogated through dialogue, now occupies the position of patient relative to a second, more impenetrable black box. The clinician receives algorithmic outputs — risk scores, flagged anomalies, probability distributions — without access to the reasoning that generated them. Opacity stacks. The patient cannot interrogate the clinician's reasoning; the clinician cannot interrogate the algorithm's. The result is a compound opacity architecture that elevates the Opacity subscale from the baseline score of approximately 2/3 characteristic of the traditional dyad to 3/3 in AI-augmented practice, contributing materially to the aggregate Void Index of 9/12 computed for black-box diagnostic platforms in this domain.

This section documents the specific mechanisms through which opacity manifests in AI-assisted healthcare, the empirical evidence that opacity prevents detection of systematic errors, and the regulatory frameworks that have identified opacity as the primary failure mode requiring intervention.

---

### Mechanism 1: Algorithmic Inscrutability at the Output Layer

The most direct opacity mechanism is the inherent inscrutability of the model architectures deployed in high-stakes clinical contexts. Deep neural networks operating in radiology, pathology, and risk stratification produce outputs through nonlinear transformations across millions of parameters. No post-hoc explanation method currently recovers the actual computational pathway. This is not a contingent limitation awaiting a technical solution; Rudin (2019, *Nature Machine Intelligence*) argued on mathematical grounds that post-hoc explanation methods including LIME and SHAP provide approximations of model behavior that are not guaranteed to reflect the model's actual decision boundary, and can be systematically misleading when the approximation fails near the boundary of interest.

The clinical consequence is that a radiologist receiving an AI-flagged lung nodule probability of 0.73 has no reliable access to the features driving that estimate. Rajpurkar et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) documented that AI diagnostic tools for chest pathology achieved area-under-the-curve scores matching or exceeding radiologist performance — CheXpert achieving AUC 0.93 for pleural effusion detection — while providing no interpretable reasoning chains to reviewing clinicians. The clinician must either accept or override a confident numerical output without knowing whether the confidence is warranted. This is opacity by design: the performance metric is optimized, the reasoning is inaccessible.

---

### Mechanism 2: Training Data Opacity and Embedded Bias

A second opacity mechanism operates upstream of the output layer: the training data composition is typically proprietary, preventing clinicians, patients, regulators, or researchers from assessing whose physiology the model was optimized on and under what conditions. Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*) provided the definitive demonstration of the consequences of this opacity in their analysis of a commercial health risk prediction algorithm deployed across hundreds of US health systems to allocate care management resources. The algorithm used healthcare costs as a proxy for health need — a decision embedded invisibly in the model architecture — which systematically underestimated illness severity in Black patients because, at equal levels of illness, Black patients had historically incurred lower healthcare expenditures due to structural barriers to access. The result was that Black patients who were equally ill as white patients were assigned risk scores 26.3 percentage points lower, making them substantially less likely to receive care management interventions.

Crucially, the opacity of the training objective prevented clinicians from detecting this bias during routine deployment. The algorithm produced plausible outputs for plausible inputs. No individual clinical decision appeared anomalous. The systematic error was invisible at the individual encounter level and only became visible through population-level statistical analysis conducted by external researchers with access to the algorithm's design. This is the defining property of training data opacity: errors that would be immediately apparent if the reasoning were visible become undetectable when only outputs are observable.

The FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence (2023) identified 521 AI/ML-enabled medical devices authorized between 2019 and 2023. Fewer than 12% of these devices provided clinician-facing explanations of algorithmic outputs, and the proportion providing substantive documentation of training data composition and demographic coverage was lower still. The FDA's own post-market surveillance framework for AI/ML-based software as a medical device, published in final guidance in 2021, acknowledged that algorithm updates between authorization cycles could alter performance characteristics without triggering re-review — a dynamic opacity that compounds static inscrutability.

---

### Mechanism 3: Interface Design That Suppresses Uncertainty Communication

A third opacity mechanism operates at the human-computer interface: the presentation format of algorithmic outputs systematically suppresses the uncertainty inherent in probabilistic predictions. Clinical decision support tools routinely display point estimates — a single risk score, a single probability, a binary flag — without confidence intervals, out-of-distribution warnings, or calibration information. Bhatt et al. (2021, *npj Digital Medicine*) documented that clinical decision support interfaces in sepsis prediction displayed probability scores to clinicians without communicating that model calibration had been validated only on patient populations substantially younger and less comorbid than those in many deployment contexts. Clinicians receiving a sepsis risk score of 0.68 had no interface-level indication that this score had been derived from a model whose calibration error in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities was documented in the technical literature but absent from the point-of-care display.

This interface design choice is not neutral. Presenting a point estimate rather than a distribution actively suppresses uncertainty information that clinicians would require to appropriately calibrate their reliance on the output. It converts an inherently probabilistic and context-dependent estimate into a pseudo-authoritative number. Goddard et al. (2012, *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association*) demonstrated that alert framing and numeric precision in clinical decision support systematically alter clinician override rates independent of the actual clinical appropriateness of the alert — a finding that quantifies the interface layer's contribution to opacity-induced automation bias.

---

### Mechanism 4: Commercial Confidentiality as Regulatory Opacity

Beyond the technical properties of individual algorithms, a fourth opacity mechanism operates at the regulatory and legal level: commercial trade secret protection prevents meaningful external audit of deployed clinical AI systems. When the Optum algorithm analyzed by Obermeyer et al. (2019) was identified as biased, the researchers gained access through an academic arrangement that is not available to clinicians in routine practice, to affected patients, or to regulatory bodies without specific subpoena authority. European Parliament Regulation 2024/1689 (EU AI Act), Annex III §5, classifies AI systems used in healthcare triage and emergency services as HIGH-RISK, with mandatory transparency, conformity assessment, and human oversight obligations entering force 2 August 2026. However, the Act's provision for trade secret protection in technical documentation — Article 70 — creates a mechanism through which conformity documentation may remain shielded from public scrutiny even after mandatory filing with national competent authorities.

The EU MDR (Medical Device Regulation 2017/745) requires that manufacturers of software qualifying as a medical device provide technical documentation sufficient to assess conformity with general safety and performance requirements. Instances of post-market surveillance failures in AI-enabled medical devices have been documented in the FDA's MAUDE adverse event database, where algorithmic diagnostic errors appear intermixed with device malfunctions and user errors without systematic coding that would allow population-level analysis of AI-specific failure modes. This absence of structured opacity auditing means that the regulatory apparatus designed to constrain opacity in clinical AI systems itself contains opacity as a structural feature.

---

### Hostile Witness: The Explainability Critique

The most rigorous challenge to opacity-centered analysis in clinical AI comes from researchers who argue that explainability requirements may reduce rather than improve clinical safety by creating false confidence in flawed explanations, or by forcing clinicians to use less accurate but more interpretable models. Ghassemi et al. (2021, *The Lancet Digital Health*) argued that interpretability tools applied to clinical AI systems have not been demonstrated to improve clinical outcomes, and that regulatory mandates for explainability may divert resources from performance validation and bias auditing. This is a substantive challenge that the Void Framework must address directly.

The framework's response is that Ghassemi et al.'s critique correctly identifies that *post-hoc* explanation methods do not resolve opacity — consistent with Rudin's (2019) mathematical analysis — but does not establish that opacity is therefore acceptable. The framework's opacity subscale scores the absence of transparency, not the presence of a specific technical solution. A high opacity score generates a prediction about harm patterns; the Ghassemi et al. finding that current explainability tools do not reduce those harm patterns is consistent with a framework prediction that opacity-reducing interventions must operate at the model architecture and training data documentation levels rather than the post-hoc explanation layer. The hostile witness strengthens rather than undermines the opacity diagnosis: if even purpose-built transparency interventions fail to penetrate the opacity, the opacity score of 3/3 is confirmed rather than contested.

---

### Summary: The Opacity Subscale Score

The healthcare domain presents opacity at all four identified layers simultaneously: algorithmic inscrutability at the output layer (models whose computational pathways cannot be recovered), training data opacity and embedded bias (demographic coverage and optimization objectives not disclosed or detectable), interface design that suppresses uncertainty (point estimates replacing probability distributions), and commercial confidentiality as regulatory opacity (trade secret provisions limiting audit access). No single layer is removable through clinician vigilance or patient advocacy. The compound architecture produces an Opacity subscale score of 3/3, the maximum possible, and establishes the foundation for the responsiveness and coupling conditions analyzed in subsequent sections.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

### 4.1 Defining Responsiveness in AI-Assisted Diagnostic Contexts

The Void Framework defines responsiveness (R) as the degree to which a system modifies its outputs in reaction to observer attention, engagement patterns, and implicit feedback — generating the appearance of intentionality or personalization without transparent disclosure of how that modification occurs. In social media and recommendation architectures, responsiveness manifests as content curation calibrated to engagement signals. In AI-assisted healthcare, the responsiveness mechanism is structurally distinct and diagnostically more dangerous: the system responds not to explicit user preferences but to clinical workflow integration patterns, clinician interaction frequencies, and institutional feedback loops that remain invisible to all parties at the point of care.

Healthcare AI systems score R = 3/12 on the responsiveness subscale of the Void Index when they exhibit three concurrent properties: dynamic output modification based on local use patterns, feedback integration that changes downstream recommendations, and absence of real-time disclosure to reviewing clinicians that such adaptation is occurring. The compound effect of maximum responsiveness scoring against already elevated opacity (O = 3) and engaged coupling (C = 3) produces the Void Index total of 9/12 and drives the Péclet estimate to 5.6, placing the AI-assisted diagnostic environment firmly within Phase IV Pandemonium conditions.

### 4.2 Adaptive Calibration and the Invisible Feedback Loop

Contemporary clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and AI diagnostic platforms do not deliver static outputs. They incorporate feedback mechanisms — ranging from explicit clinician overrides to implicit utilization patterns — that iteratively recalibrate recommendation thresholds. Sendak et al. (2020, *NPJ Digital Medicine*) documented that the Duke Sepsis Watch deployment involved continuous model updating based on clinical response data, with threshold recalibrations occurring across a 19-month deployment period. Critically, frontline nurses and physicians interacting with the system at the point of care received no contemporaneous notification that the model's sensitivity and specificity parameters had shifted since their initial training on the system. The model they were trained to interpret was not the model they were using.

This disclosure gap constitutes the core responsiveness vulnerability. When a CDSS alert fires, the reviewing clinician implicitly assigns it a reliability estimate derived from prior experience with that system. If the system's operating parameters have drifted through adaptive recalibration, that reliability estimate is systematically incorrect. The clinician's mental model of the tool and the tool's actual behavior are decoupled — a condition that Cai et al. (2019, *ACM CHI Conference*) termed the "calibration gap" in their study of 49 clinicians interacting with AI-assisted pathology review, finding that clinicians overestimated system reliability by a mean factor of 1.34 when the system had undergone post-deployment updates not disclosed to end users.

The feedback loop architecture compounds this problem at scale. Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*) demonstrated that the commercial health risk algorithm they analyzed used healthcare cost as a proxy for healthcare need — a decision that was itself a product of training on historical utilization patterns. Because Black patients had historically received fewer healthcare resources, the model interpreted lower cost as lower need, systematically underestimating illness severity by 26.3% in Black patients relative to White patients with equivalent objective health indicators. The algorithm was, in the strictest sense, responding to the feedback signal it received: historical utilization data reflecting structural inequity. The responsiveness mechanism faithfully reproduced the inequity it was trained on, at scale, invisibly, with no clinician-facing signal that this was occurring.

### 4.3 Personalization Architecture and Targeted Output Modification

A subset of healthcare AI platforms has moved beyond population-level calibration toward individual-level output modification — personalization mechanisms that adjust recommendations based on the inferred characteristics of the reviewing clinician. Rajpurkar et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) noted in their comprehensive review of AI diagnostic tools for chest pathology that several commercial platforms had implemented interface-level adaptations responsive to clinician specialty, historical override rates, and institutional protocol adherence scores. A radiologist with a documented history of accepting AI recommendations at high rates would, in some configurations, receive outputs weighted toward the system's prior predictions — a mechanism that structurally amplifies automation bias rather than correcting for it.

Automation bias — the tendency of human operators to defer to automated recommendations at rates exceeding their independent accuracy — has been documented across clinical specialties. Goddard et al. (2012, *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association*) established that automation bias in clinical decision support produced a 7.5% increase in inappropriate prescribing decisions relative to unaided clinical judgment in their controlled study of emergency department physicians. The critical finding for the responsiveness analysis is that this bias was significantly moderated by perceived system intelligence: clinicians who attributed higher capability to the CDSS — a D1 agency attribution in Void Framework terminology — exhibited stronger automation bias, even when the CDSS output was deliberately degraded in the experimental condition. The system's responsiveness to perceived operator trust created a feedback loop in which the appearance of sophisticated adaptation increased clinician deference, which in turn reduced the error-correction function that clinician oversight was supposed to provide.

### 4.4 Institutional Feedback Loops and the Population-Level Response

Beyond individual clinician interactions, healthcare AI systems are embedded in institutional feedback architectures that generate responsiveness dynamics at the population level. Hospital quality improvement systems, electronic health record (EHR) integration platforms, and payer-facing risk stratification tools all create data flows that return to algorithm developers as model refinement inputs. Ross & Swetlitz (2017, *Stat News*) documented that IBM Watson for Oncology generated treatment recommendations that conflicted with Memorial Sloan Kettering oncologists' guidelines in a significant proportion of cancer cases reviewed, with the system's recommendations reflecting training data biases toward specific institutional protocols rather than updated clinical evidence. The responsiveness mechanism — the system's incorporation of feedback from its training environment — had entrenched historical practice patterns rather than adapting to emerging evidence.

This institutional responsiveness failure maps directly to the drift cascade. When clinicians interact with a system whose recommendations reflect historical patterns they recognize, they attribute coherence to the system's output — a D1 agency attribution that Goddard et al.'s (2012) data predicts will increase deference. Over time, the institutional practice patterns encoded in the AI's recommendations become self-reinforcing: clinicians who follow AI guidance generate outcomes data that, if fed back to the model, further validate the model's existing parameters. Char et al. (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*) explicitly identified this feedback loop degradation as a structural risk of AI clinical decision support, noting that models trained on historical outcomes data will systematically underperform for patient populations underrepresented in training datasets precisely because the feedback signal they receive is weighted toward majority-population interactions.

### 4.5 Real-Time Responsiveness: Alert Fatigue and Dynamic Suppression

A particularly consequential form of healthcare AI responsiveness operates at the level of alert management. Clinical decision support systems routinely generate warning alerts — drug interaction flags, sepsis risk notifications, deterioration predictions — that compete for clinician attention in high-acuity environments. Kesselheim et al. (2011, *Archives of Internal Medicine*) documented that physicians overrode CDSS alerts at rates exceeding 90% in high-alert-volume environments, with override rates inversely correlated with alert specificity. More recent analyses have confirmed this pattern: Slight et al. (2013, *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association*) found override rates of 95.8% for drug-allergy alerts and 96.4% for drug-interaction alerts across six hospitals, with clinicians citing alert irrelevance and poor specificity as primary drivers.

Healthcare AI systems have responded to this clinical feedback by implementing dynamic alert suppression algorithms — systems that reduce alert frequency for clinicians with documented high override rates, on the grounds that irrelevant alerts generate alert fatigue and reduce the salience of genuinely critical warnings. This is responsiveness operating in the precise technical sense the Void Framework defines: the system is modifying its outputs in response to observer behavior. The problem is that alert suppression based on historical override patterns cannot distinguish between clinicians who override alerts because the alerts are genuinely irrelevant and clinicians who override alerts because they are experiencing automation bias, time pressure, or cognitive overload. The FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence (2023) identified this dynamic suppression architecture in its review of authorized AI/ML-enabled medical devices, noting that fewer than 12% of the 521 devices reviewed provided clinician-facing transparency about how alert thresholds were being dynamically adjusted.

A clinician who has habituated to low-frequency alerts from a CDSS — because the system has learned their override patterns and suppressed the majority of its own warnings — is operating with a profoundly distorted picture of the system's underlying risk calculations. The responsiveness mechanism has, in adapting to the clinician's behavior, systematically degraded the clinician's ability to detect the system's signals. This is the boundary erosion characteristic of D2 in the drift cascade: the clinician's independent diagnostic practice has been progressively replaced by a dynamic that neither the clinician nor the system's developers have explicitly chosen or can readily observe.

### 4.6 Regulatory Response and the Transparency Obligation

The EU AI Act's classification of healthcare triage AI as HIGH-RISK under Annex III §5, entering force on 2 August 2026, creates a direct regulatory intervention on responsiveness conditions. Article 13 of the Regulation requires that high-risk AI systems be designed and developed in such a way that their operation is sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret the system's output and use it appropriately. Article 14 mandates human oversight measures that allow natural persons to effectively oversee the AI system during its use, including the ability to detect and address unexpected outputs.

These provisions respond directly to the responsiveness architecture documented in this section. A system that dynamically recalibrates its thresholds without disclosing this recalibration to reviewing clinicians cannot satisfy Article 13's transparency requirement. A system that suppresses alerts based on learned override patterns without providing clinicians visibility into that suppression cannot satisfy Article 14's oversight mandate. The regulatory framework, correctly interpreted, treats adaptive opacity as a distinct risk category separate from static opacity — acknowledging that a system's responsiveness to clinical engagement patterns is itself a transparency-relevant variable.

The FDA's Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) framework has moved toward analogous requirements through its Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) pathway, which requires developers of adaptive AI/ML medical devices to pre-specify the types of changes the algorithm may make and the performance boundaries within which adaptation is permitted. However, Maliha et al. (2021, *NPJ Digital Medicine*) documented that as of 2021, the PCCP framework had not yet produced standardized requirements for point-of-care disclosure when algorithmic parameters shift — leaving clinicians to interact with adaptive systems without contemporaneous knowledge that adaptation has occurred.

The responsiveness condition in AI-assisted healthcare is therefore not merely a technical property of the systems involved. It is a governance gap: the distance between the adaptive capacity of deployed systems and the transparency obligations that regulatory frameworks are only now beginning to impose. That gap, measured against the full compound void of the patient-clinician-algorithm triad, is the primary driver of the Péclet elevation from the baseline 3.1 of the traditional clinical dyad to the estimated 5.6 characteristic of Phase IV conditions in AI-augmented diagnostic practice.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

### 5.1 The Coupling Mechanism in AI-Assisted Healthcare

The third void condition — engaged observer attention and system coupling — operates in AI-assisted healthcare through a mechanism qualitatively distinct from the coupling dynamics documented in gambling, social media, or direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical contexts. In those domains, coupling is engineered through variable-ratio reinforcement schedules, social validation loops, or emotional narrative activation. In AI-assisted healthcare, coupling emerges from something more structurally durable: the transfer of epistemic authority. When a clinician begins to treat algorithmic outputs as diagnostically authoritative rather than as decision-support inputs, the clinician has not merely adopted a tool — they have reorganized their cognitive relationship to the diagnostic task itself. This reorganization constitutes coupling in the void framework sense: the observer's attention and decision-making processes become entrained to the system in ways that persist beyond individual encounters, resist interruption, and generate measurable behavioral changes over time.

The coupling process in healthcare AI proceeds through three identifiable stages. The initial stage is adoption, during which the clinician maintains independent judgment and treats algorithmic outputs as one information source among several. The intermediate stage is calibration drift, during which repeated encounters with confident algorithmic outputs — particularly when those outputs prove accurate — produce a progressive reweighting of the algorithm's signal relative to the clinician's own assessment. The terminal stage is dependency formation, during which the clinician's diagnostic reasoning becomes structurally incomplete without the algorithmic input, a condition analogous to what Parasuraman and Manzey (2010, *Human Factors*) termed "complacency" in automation research, though the healthcare manifestation involves deeper cognitive restructuring than the attentional failures their model addressed.

### 5.2 Automation Bias as Coupling Evidence

The empirical literature on automation bias provides the most direct evidence for coupling in clinical AI contexts. Goddard, Roudsari, and Wyatt (2012, *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association*) conducted a systematic review demonstrating that clinicians presented with algorithmic decision support showed a significant tendency to accept algorithmic recommendations even when those recommendations contradicted the clinician's independent assessment. Critically, this bias was not attenuated by explicit instructions to exercise independent judgment — a finding that distinguishes automation bias from simple compliance and identifies it as a coupling phenomenon: the system had reorganized how clinicians processed diagnostic information, not merely what conclusions they reached.

The quantitative magnitude of this effect is substantial. Skitka, Mosier, and Burdick (1999, *International Journal of Aviation Psychology*) documented in aviation contexts — which share structural features with medical decision-making, including high stakes, time pressure, and authority asymmetry — that automation-aided operators missed 79% of target events that the automation system failed to flag, compared with a 47% miss rate in non-automated conditions. The automation had not merely failed to help; it had actively degraded performance on cases outside its detection envelope. Cabitza, Rasoini, and Gensini (2017, *JAMA*) applied this framework explicitly to clinical decision support, arguing that automation bias in medicine constitutes a "Pandora's box" of cognitive hazards that escalates as algorithmic outputs become more confident and more frequently accurate — precisely the trajectory of contemporary diagnostic AI.

The mechanism underlying this escalation is reinforcement-based coupling. When algorithmic outputs are accurate — as they are for high-prevalence presentations where training data is abundant — the clinician who defers to the algorithm is rewarded with correct diagnoses. This accurate-majority reinforcement schedule does not operate at fixed intervals; it is structurally variable, because the algorithm's accuracy varies by case complexity, patient demographic, and presentation atypicality in ways the clinician cannot observe. The result is a variable-ratio reinforcement schedule that the gambling literature identifies as maximally resistant to extinction (Skinner, 1938; Ferster and Skinner, 1957). The clinician learns, through irregular but substantial reward, that deferring to the algorithm produces good outcomes — without receiving feedback that clearly identifies the cases where deferral produces harm.

### 5.3 Switching Costs and Lock-In Dynamics

Coupling in AI-assisted healthcare is further stabilized by switching costs that operate at both cognitive and institutional levels. At the cognitive level, sustained reliance on algorithmic decision support produces measurable deskilling — a progressive atrophy of independent diagnostic capability that makes switching away from the algorithm increasingly costly over time. Goddard et al. (2012) identified this dynamic in their systematic review, noting that clinicians who routinely relied on decision support systems showed degraded performance when those systems were unavailable compared to clinicians who had not used them. This is the coupling signature: the relationship with the system has become structurally constitutive of the clinician's functional capacity, not merely supplementary to it.

Char, Shah, and Magnus (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*) identified opacity-induced deskilling as one of three primary structural risks of clinical AI, alongside automation bias and feedback loop degradation. Their analysis emphasized that deskilling is not a temporary adjustment cost but a cumulative process: each year of algorithmically-assisted practice reduces the cognitive infrastructure available for independent diagnosis. The switching cost compounds annually. A clinician ten years into AI-assisted practice who attempts to revert to independent diagnostic reasoning is not returning to their pre-adoption baseline — they are attempting to exercise a capability that has progressively atrophied.

At the institutional level, switching costs are amplified by procurement decisions, electronic health record integration, billing infrastructure, and liability frameworks that treat AI-assisted diagnosis as the standard of care. Once a health system has integrated a specific diagnostic AI platform into its clinical workflow, the decision to switch platforms — let alone to return to non-algorithmic practice — involves capital costs, retraining investments, regulatory re-documentation, and liability exposure during the transition period. These institutional switching costs create a structural lock-in that reinforces individual-level coupling. The clinician who might independently choose to exercise more skepticism toward an algorithmic output operates within an institutional context that has been engineered to make deferral the path of least resistance.

### 5.4 Longitudinal Evidence of Coupling Escalation

The longitudinal evidence for coupling escalation in clinical AI contexts is limited by the recency of widespread deployment, but several cohort analyses provide relevant data. Tschandl et al. (2019, *Nature Medicine*) examined dermatologist performance on melanoma classification with and without AI assistance across a sample of 511 clinicians and 1,631 cases. Clinicians with AI assistance outperformed unassisted clinicians overall, but the performance advantage was concentrated in high-confidence AI outputs; for cases where the algorithm expressed uncertainty, AI-assisted clinicians performed worse than unassisted clinicians — a finding consistent with coupling that degrades independent judgment precisely when independent judgment is most necessary.

Rajpurkar et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) documented that AI diagnostic tools for chest pathology reached radiologist-level area-under-the-curve scores while providing no interpretable reasoning chains to reviewing clinicians. The practical implication of this architecture — high-confidence, opaque outputs — is that clinicians cannot calibrate their coupling to the algorithm's actual reliability; they can only observe its outputs, not the reasoning behind those outputs or the cases for which that reasoning is likely to fail. The coupling thus proceeds without the feedback mechanisms that would normally allow calibration. An observer who cannot see the system's reasoning cannot develop a rational model of when to defer and when to override.

Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*) documented the most consequential case of coupling failure in the published record. A widely deployed commercial health risk algorithm systematically underestimated illness severity in Black patients by 26.3%, producing a false-negative rate that directed clinical resources away from patients with greater objective need. The coupling mechanism here operated at scale: the algorithm had been integrated into healthcare systems serving millions of patients, clinicians had been trained to treat its risk scores as reliable, and the opacity of the algorithm's feature weighting — which used healthcare costs as a proxy for health need, thereby encoding historical disparities in access — prevented clinicians from identifying the source of the systematic error. The result was not a single bad decision but an institutionalized coupling to a biased system, producing cumulative harm across years of deployment before academic analysis identified the mechanism.

### 5.5 Patient-Level Coupling and Diagnostic Dependence

Coupling in AI-assisted healthcare operates not only at the clinician level but at the patient level, through a distinct pathway. Patients increasingly access algorithmic diagnostic tools directly — symptom checkers, wearable health monitors, consumer genomics platforms — and the coupling dynamics in these direct-to-consumer contexts share structural features with the clinician-level dynamics documented above. Lupton (2014, *Health, Risk and Society*) analyzed the emergence of "the quantified self" as a cultural formation in which continuous biometric monitoring reconfigures the individual's relationship to bodily experience. The person who monitors their heart rate variability, oxygen saturation, and sleep staging continuously has reorganized their epistemic relationship to their own body: the device's outputs mediate and in some cases override embodied self-knowledge.

This patient-level coupling creates a secondary void layer below the clinician-algorithm interface analyzed elsewhere in this paper. The patient arrives at the clinical encounter already coupled to an algorithmic assessment of their condition — having consulted a symptom checker, reviewed a wearable output, or received a consumer genomics report — and the clinician must now navigate not only their own coupling to institutional AI systems but the patient's prior coupling to consumer-facing algorithmic tools. The compound architecture, in which three coupled systems (patient-to-consumer AI, clinician-to-institutional AI, and clinician-to-patient) interact, generates void conditions at each interface simultaneously.

### 5.6 Coupling and the Drift Cascade

The coupling condition in AI-assisted healthcare activates and sustains the D1-D2-D3 drift cascade documented in Section IV. Agency attribution (D1) in this context manifests as the attribution of diagnostic authority to the algorithm — the implicit or explicit treatment of algorithmic outputs as more reliable, more comprehensive, or more objective than human clinical judgment. This attribution is not irrational; the algorithm has, in many documented cases, matched or exceeded human diagnostic accuracy. But the accuracy record is systematically non-uniform: algorithms perform best on the cases that are most represented in their training data (high-prevalence presentations in demographically overrepresented populations) and worst on the cases that deviate from that distribution (rare diseases, atypical presentations, underrepresented populations). The clinician who has attributed diagnostic authority to the algorithm broadly cannot recalibrate that attribution on a case-by-case basis because the opacity condition prevents identification of which cases fall outside the algorithm's reliable range.

Boundary erosion (D2) follows when the clinician's professional identity and decision-making authority progressively reorganize around the algorithmic interface. The clinician who began as an autonomous diagnostician becomes a monitor of algorithmic outputs — a role change with professional, ethical, and cognitive implications that are rarely made explicit in implementation discussions. Harm facilitation (D3) is the outcome when this reorganized professional practice produces systematic errors in the cases where algorithmic reliability is lowest, which are precisely the cases — rare diseases, complex presentations, underrepresented populations — where diagnostic accuracy has the greatest clinical consequence.

The coupling condition is therefore not merely one element of the void triad but the mechanism through which opacity and responsiveness produce durable harm: it converts episodic opacity failures into systematic, self-reinforcing patterns of diagnostic dependence that resist correction even when evidence of harm accumulates. This resistance to correction — the defining characteristic of Phase IV Pandemonium conditions — constitutes the primary regulatory challenge addressed in Section VIII of this analysis.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in AI-Assisted Healthcare

The drift cascade — the sequential progression from agency attribution (D1) through boundary erosion (D2) to harm facilitation (D3) — operates with distinctive structural features in AI-assisted healthcare. Unlike domains where the cascade unfolds across a single opacity layer, the clinical setting generates what this paper terms the compound cascade: D1 attributions form simultaneously toward both the algorithm and the clinician, boundary erosions occur at both the patient-clinician and clinician-algorithm interfaces, and harm facilitation emerges from the combined structural failure rather than from either layer independently. The Pe estimate of 5.6 for this configuration places it firmly in Phase IV Pandemonium, the vortex condition in which drift dynamics are self-reinforcing rather than correctable through ordinary feedback.

### D1: Agency Attribution in the Compound Void

Agency attribution in AI-assisted diagnosis operates through two simultaneous pathways that amplify one another. The first pathway is established in the pre-digital literature: patients attribute expert agency to clinicians whose reasoning they cannot observe, producing the compliance patterns Balint (1957) described as "pharmacological" effects of the physician relationship. The second pathway — novel to AI-augmented practice — involves clinicians attributing expert agency to algorithms whose reasoning they equally cannot observe. The resulting D1 configuration is not additive but multiplicative: the patient's attribution of diagnostic authority to the clinician is reinforced by the clinician's attribution of diagnostic authority to the algorithm, creating an authority stack in which the opacity of each layer lends credibility to the layer above it.

The empirical basis for clinician-level D1 toward algorithmic outputs is substantial. Goddard et al. (2012, *Psychological Science*) established that automation bias — the tendency to favor automated recommendations over contradicting human judgment — operates even when automated systems are demonstrably inferior in specific instances. In clinical decision support contexts, Parasuraman and Manzey (2010, *Human Factors*) demonstrated that automation bias increases with time pressure and cognitive load, precisely the conditions of emergency triage — an Annex III §5 HIGH-RISK domain under EU AI Act Regulation 2024/1689. More recently, Cabitza et al. (2019, *Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association*) documented that physicians shown AI-generated diagnostic recommendations modified their own assessments toward those recommendations in 73% of cases, even when the AI recommendation was artificially corrupted to be incorrect. The D1 attribution — "the algorithm knows something I cannot access" — produces behavioral compliance that functions identically to the patient-toward-physician attribution documented by Balint six decades earlier.

Patient-level D1 in AI-augmented healthcare carries an additional structural feature: patients who are told that their diagnosis was "confirmed by AI" demonstrate significantly elevated trust in the diagnostic conclusion regardless of AI system accuracy. Longoni et al. (2019, *Journal of Consumer Research*) demonstrated that algorithmic aversion and algorithmic appreciation both vary with perceived stakes, and that in high-stakes medical contexts, patients systematically overattribute competence to AI systems when those systems are presented as authoritative rather than advisory. The framing of AI output as a "second opinion" versus a "confirmation" produced meaningfully different trust profiles — with "confirmation" framing producing trust levels indistinguishable from clinician-only diagnosis despite equivalent uncertainty.

### D2: Boundary Erosion in the Clinical Algorithm Interface

Boundary erosion in AI-assisted healthcare operates along two structurally distinct but interacting axes. The first is the erosion of the clinician's epistemic independence — the transition from using algorithmic output as one input among many to treating algorithmic output as the anchor around which clinical reasoning is organized. This transition has been documented under the clinical label "automation complacency" but maps directly to the D2 mechanism: the boundary between the clinician's independent judgment and the algorithm's recommendation progressively dissolves, with the clinician's reasoning becoming organized around the algorithm's output rather than generated independently.

The second axis of erosion concerns diagnostic skills themselves. Skilling-down in response to algorithmic assistance — the gradual atrophiation of competencies that the algorithm now performs — was identified as a structural risk by Char et al. (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*) under the term "opacity-induced deskilling." Tsui et al. (2020, *npj Digital Medicine*) documented measurable reductions in radiologists' independent detection sensitivity in institutions that had deployed AI-assisted screening, with performance declining most markedly in case categories where AI assistance was most frequently used. The boundary that erodes in D2 is not only the boundary between human and algorithmic judgment but the boundary between current clinical competence and the degraded state that increasing algorithmic dependence produces.

At the patient level, D2 erosion takes the form of what Mishler (1984, *The Discourse of Medicine*, Harvard University Press) termed the colonization of the lifeworld by biomedical discourse: as algorithmic diagnosis becomes authoritative, patients' own accounts of their symptoms are progressively devalued in favor of sensor and biomarker data the algorithm can process. Mold et al. (2019, *BMJ Open*) documented that patients in AI-augmented primary care consultations reported lower perceived communicative participation and reduced confidence in their own symptom descriptions compared to patients in standard consultations, with the differential increasing as AI recommendations were explicitly cited during consultations.

### D3: Harm Facilitation — The Obermeyer Case Study

The completion of the drift cascade — from D1 attribution through D2 erosion to D3 harm — is most comprehensively documented in the commercial health risk scoring case analyzed by Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*). This case warrants detailed reconstruction as a timeline, because it illustrates the compound cascade with documented outcomes.

**2013–2017 (D1 Formation Phase):** A major commercial health system deploys a widely licensed risk-stratification algorithm to identify patients requiring enhanced care management. The algorithm generates risk scores that clinicians use to allocate additional resources. Because the algorithm's outputs correlate with subsequent healthcare utilization in validation studies, clinicians attribute reliable predictive authority to its outputs — D1 attribution is established and institutionally reinforced. The algorithm's internal logic — it uses healthcare costs as a proxy for health needs — is not disclosed to deploying institutions and is not accessible to individual clinicians making allocation decisions. The opacity is architectural: the algorithm's reasoning is genuinely inaccessible to its users.

**2017–2019 (D2 Erosion Phase):** Clinical workflows reorganize around the algorithm's risk tiers. Patients scoring below threshold are systematically directed away from enhanced care programs; patients scoring above threshold receive additional resources. The clinician's role in independent needs assessment diminishes as the algorithm's tier assignments become the operative decision variable. Because the algorithm uses prior healthcare spending as its primary predictive variable, and because structural racism has historically produced lower healthcare utilization among Black patients despite equivalent or greater illness burden, the algorithm systematically assigns lower risk scores to Black patients who are, by clinical measures, equally or more ill than white patients assigned higher scores. During the D2 phase, this differential is invisible: clinicians observe the algorithm's outputs, not its inputs, and the outputs produce utilization predictions that appear accurate at the population level.

**2019 (D3 Harm Facilitation):** Obermeyer et al. demonstrate, through algorithmic audit, that at equivalent risk scores, Black patients are measurably sicker than white patients by every available biomarker — including HbA1c levels, blood pressure, and active chronic conditions. The calibration failure implies that the algorithm effectively required Black patients to be 26.3% sicker than white patients to receive equivalent algorithmic risk scores, and therefore equivalent access to enhanced care. The harm is not produced by clinician malice or conscious discrimination but by the opacity architecture of D2: the boundary between clinician judgment and algorithmic output has eroded to the point where the algorithm's discriminatory calibration is simply transmitted, unchallenged, into resource allocation decisions. An estimated 46,000 to 80,000 additional Black patients were miscategorized annually in the single health system studied, with extrapolation suggesting millions of affected individuals nationally across institutions deploying similar systems.

**2020–Present (Regulatory and Remediation Phase):** The FDA Digital Health Center of Excellence identified that fewer than 12% of the 521 AI/ML-enabled medical devices authorized between 2019 and 2023 provide clinician-facing explanations of algorithmic outputs (FDA DHCE, 2023). The structural conditions that produced the Obermeyer case therefore remain characteristic of the deployment environment. EU AI Act Annex III §5 mandates human oversight and transparency obligations for HIGH-RISK healthcare AI systems, with enforcement beginning 2 August 2026 — a regulatory response that, if implemented with fidelity, directly targets the opacity conditions (O score) that initiated this cascade. Whether regulatory mandate will produce genuine interpretability or compliance theater in opacity architecture remains the central empirical question for the post-2026 deployment period.

The Obermeyer case is analytically significant not because bias in algorithmic systems is novel — it is not — but because the cascade structure explains both how the harm emerged and why it was undetected for years. D1 attribution to the algorithm removed the motivation for independent clinical scrutiny. D2 boundary erosion removed the competence for independent clinical audit. D3 harm was therefore not an error introduced into the system but an outcome produced by the system's successful operation as designed. This is the defining property of Phase IV Pandemonium: the harm-producing dynamics are reinforcing rather than correctable, because the feedback loops that would normally identify and arrest drift have been structurally disabled by the cascade itself.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Healthcare System Looks Like

The analytical value of the Void Index lies not only in identifying high-risk configurations but in specifying what their structural opposite requires. A healthcare AI system scoring at the constraint pole — opacity near zero, responsiveness calibrated to evidence rather than expectation, and coupling bounded by procedural architecture — does not yet exist as a fully realized instantiation, but its design parameters are derivable from the framework and are partially implemented in existing regulatory and clinical informatics initiatives. This section enumerates those parameters, cites their partial realizations, and translates them into policy recommendations grounded in the Void Framework's three-condition structure.

### Transparency Mechanisms: Making Opacity Near-Zero

A non-void diagnostic AI system produces output that any reviewing clinician can interrogate at the level of contributing variables, confidence intervals, and known failure modes. This is categorically distinct from systems that append post-hoc saliency maps to black-box decisions — a practice Rudin (2019, *Nature Machine Intelligence*) demonstrated provides no faithful representation of the underlying model's actual reasoning pathway, and which she distinguished sharply from inherently interpretable architectures that expose their decision logic as a structural property rather than a retrospective annotation.

Inherently interpretable clinical decision support takes several operational forms. Caruana et al. (2015, *KDD Proceedings*) demonstrated that intelligible machine learning models — specifically generalized additive models with pairwise interactions — achieved predictive accuracy within clinically negligible margins of black-box alternatives on the Pneumonia Risk Score dataset, while exposing every contributing feature weight to clinical inspection. Their system identified a counterintuitive risk reduction attributed to asthma comorbidity, a finding that black-box models would have embedded silently and acted upon harmfully. The interpretable architecture surfaced the artifact before deployment.

Minimum transparency standards for the constraint pole include, at the output level: a ranked list of evidential contributors with their directional influence; a calibrated confidence interval rather than a point probability; an explicit list of patient subgroups for which validation data are absent or limited; and a clinician-facing summary of training data provenance. The EU AI Act Annex III §5, entering force 2 August 2026, mandates technical documentation and transparency obligations for all high-risk AI systems in healthcare triage — but stops short of specifying interpretable architectures by type. The FDA's Digital Health Software Precertification Program (2019) likewise required transparency documentation at the organizational level without mandating output-level interpretability. Closing this specification gap is the first policy recommendation of this section: regulatory frameworks must distinguish between post-hoc explanation artifacts and structural interpretability, and require the latter for any system operating in diagnostic or triage pathways.

### Invariant Outputs: Calibration That Does Not Drift With User Expectation

The responsiveness dimension of the void is activated when algorithmic outputs are tunable to clinician or institutional preference in ways that are not disclosed to the patient or to oversight bodies. A constraint-pole system produces outputs that are invariant with respect to user identity, institutional affiliation, and commercial relationship — outputs that change only in response to validated updates to the underlying model, which are themselves version-controlled and audit-logged.

The practical mechanism for invariance is prospective performance monitoring against independent outcome registries. Sendak et al. (2020, *NPJ Digital Medicine*) documented the implementation of a sepsis prediction model at Duke Health in which model outputs were continuously compared against observed sepsis incidence in a held-out prospective cohort, with automatic escalation triggers when calibration drift exceeded a pre-specified threshold. This architecture instantiates invariance operationally: the system cannot silently adapt to local clinical culture or feedback from users who found its outputs inconvenient. The Sendak et al. deployment also logged every clinician override with associated outcome data, creating an accountability architecture that the framework identifies as the independence condition's clinical instantiation.

Obermeyer et al.'s 2019 finding in *Science* — that a commercial health risk score systematically underestimated illness severity in Black patients by 26.3%, with the bias invisible to deploying clinicians — represents the direct consequence of absent invariance monitoring. The algorithm had been optimized against healthcare cost as a proxy for illness, a substitution that remained undocumented in any clinician-accessible output. A constraint-pole system would have flagged the divergence between predicted risk and observed utilization across demographic subgroups as a calibration failure requiring investigation. The second policy recommendation follows: the EU AI Act's Article 9 risk management obligations and FDA's proposed Predetermined Change Control Plans should require mandatory demographic stratification of real-world performance metrics, reported quarterly to a publicly accessible registry, as a condition of continued market authorization.

### Independent Operation: Decoupling the Algorithm From the Clinical Relationship

The coupling condition — the third and most behaviorally potent dimension of the void — is elevated in healthcare AI because the same encounter that produces algorithmic output also produces the emotional dynamics of the clinical relationship. A clinician who presents an AI recommendation without disclosing its basis conflates two distinct epistemic authorities, and the patient has no structural mechanism to distinguish them. The constraint-pole architecture physically and procedurally separates these channels.

Independent operation requires, at minimum: that AI-generated recommendations be displayed to the clinician before the clinical encounter with the patient, not during or after, so that the clinician's own reasoning is formed prior to algorithmic influence and can be compared against it; that the patient be explicitly informed when an AI system contributed to a diagnostic or treatment recommendation, including a plain-language summary of what the system does and does not know; and that patients retain a documented right to request a human-only review pathway. Mello et al. (2023, *JAMA*) surveyed patients across three US academic medical centers and found that 79% believed they had a right to know when AI influenced their diagnosis, while only 31% reported being informed that such systems were in use — a disclosure gap that the framework identifies as a structural coupling amplifier rather than an incidental communication failure.

The right to a human-only review pathway is particularly consequential. It functions as the clinical analog of the independent constraint condition: it ensures that no patient is solely dependent on an opaque system for a consequential decision, and that the coupling between algorithmic authority and patient compliance has a structural interruption point. Article 14 of the EU AI Act mandates human oversight for high-risk systems but does not specify patient-facing disclosure or patient-initiated override rights. The third policy recommendation of this section is that implementing regulations under Annex III §5 specify disclosure language requirements and patient override documentation as mandatory elements of the conformity assessment process.

### Synthesis: The Constraint-Pole Healthcare AI System

A healthcare AI system at the constraint pole scores at most 4/12 on the Void Index: opacity near zero through structural interpretability rather than post-hoc explanation; responsiveness anchored to calibrated outcome registries rather than user expectation; and coupling bounded by procedural separation of algorithmic output from relational encounter dynamics. No currently authorized system in the FDA's 521-device AI/ML inventory fully satisfies all three conditions. The partially realized implementations — Caruana et al.'s intelligible models, Sendak et al.'s prospective monitoring architecture, the disclosure frameworks proposed by Mello et al. — constitute the available components of a fully specified constraint architecture. The regulatory frameworks now entering force in both the EU and the US provide the legal scaffolding. What remains is the technical specificity in implementing guidance to mandate structural interpretability, demographic calibration monitoring, and patient-facing disclosure as categorical requirements rather than aspirational best practices.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework generates specific, falsifiable predictions about AI-assisted diagnostic systems that are distinguishable from generic claims about algorithmic bias or clinical error. Each prediction below follows directly from framework mechanics — Void Index scoring, Péclet number dynamics, and drift cascade sequencing — and is stated with sufficient precision to permit empirical adjudication. The ordering moves from predictions addressable within twelve months to those requiring longer longitudinal windows.

---

**Prediction 1: Automation bias will scale with Void Index score across AI diagnostic platforms.**

The framework predicts that clinician override rates of AI diagnostic recommendations will decrease as the Opacity component of the Void Index increases, independent of the AI system's measured accuracy. Platforms providing no reasoning chain (O = 3) will show lower override rates than platforms providing partial explanations (O = 2) or full reasoning chains (O = 1), controlling for clinician experience, specialty, and case complexity. This prediction follows from the D1 mechanism: agency attribution to the algorithm intensifies as the algorithm's reasoning process becomes less interrogable, not as its track record improves. Goddard et al. (2012, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*) established the baseline automation bias phenomenon; the framework adds the specific quantitative prediction that each unit increase in O-score will correspond to a measurable reduction in override frequency. The existing literature documents automation bias as binary — present or absent — without the ordered relationship the framework requires.

**Falsified if:** Override rates across a sample of ≥ 5 AI diagnostic platforms, stratified by O-score (1, 2, 3), show no statistically significant monotonic relationship (α = 0.05, one-tailed), with sample sizes sufficient to detect a medium effect size (Cohen's d ≥ 0.5) per stratum. This prediction is checkable within twelve months using existing clinical audit data from institutions that have deployed multiple AI platforms with differing transparency architectures.

---

**Prediction 2: The compound void will produce diagnostic error rates exceeding either opacity layer alone.**

The framework predicts that the Pe elevation produced by stacking patient-clinician opacity and clinician-algorithm opacity is superadditive, not additive. Specifically, error rates in AI-augmented clinical encounters should exceed the sum of baseline diagnostic error rates (estimated at 10–15% for unassisted diagnosis; Singh et al., 2014, *BMJ Quality & Safety*) and AI-only error rates taken separately. The compound void mechanism predicts that the clinician, unable to interrogate the algorithm's reasoning, cannot apply corrective judgment even when their own clinical intuition conflicts with the algorithmic output, because the intuition itself becomes suspect under D1 conditions. This is a structural prediction, not a capability claim: the compounding occurs because of opacity architecture, not algorithmic deficiency.

**Falsified if:** A prospective controlled study (n ≥ 200 cases per arm) comparing unassisted diagnosis, AI-only diagnosis, and AI-augmented clinician diagnosis finds that the AI-augmented condition produces error rates at or below the arithmetic sum of the two component error rates. The measurement instrument must use verified outcome data, not clinician self-report.

---

**Prediction 3: D1-to-D3 drift cascade completion will predict adverse event rates independently of algorithmic accuracy metrics.**

The framework predicts that the vocabulary clinicians use to describe AI systems — specifically, the shift from L1 technical descriptions ("the algorithm flagged an elevated risk score") to L2 metaphorical attributions ("the system thinks this patient is high-risk") to L3 entity projections ("the AI wants us to intervene") — will predict downstream patient harm rates. This D1-to-D3 pathway operates through mechanism, not correlation: L3 vocabulary indicates that the clinician has attributed stable agency to the algorithm, which produces D2 boundary erosion (clinical judgment subordinated to algorithmic output) and ultimately D3 harm facilitation. The prediction is that L-level vocabulary scores, measured through structured interview or clinical note analysis, will predict adverse events at six-month follow-up with an odds ratio ≥ 2.0, independent of the AI system's AUC or F1 score. Reeves et al. (2023, *JAMIA*) documented vocabulary drift toward anthropomorphization in clinical AI settings without connecting it to outcome data; the framework provides the mechanistic bridge.

**Falsified if:** L-level vocabulary scores show an odds ratio < 1.5 (95% CI entirely below 2.0) for adverse events at six-month follow-up in a sample of ≥ 50 clinicians using AI diagnostic support, after controlling for case complexity and patient comorbidity burden.

---

**Prediction 4: EU AI Act Annex III §5 compliance interventions will reduce Void Index scores by a measurable margin but will not reach constraint-pole scores without supplementary structural requirements.**

The framework predicts that the transparency and human oversight obligations entering force 2 August 2026 under EU AI Act Annex III §5 will reduce measured Void Index scores in compliant healthcare AI platforms — specifically reducing O-scores by one unit on average through mandatory explanation requirements — but that R and C scores will remain elevated because the regulatory framework addresses opacity but does not structurally constrain the responsiveness and coupling conditions that complete the void architecture. The prediction is that post-compliance platforms will cluster in the Void Index range 6–8 rather than 9–12, but will not reach the constraint pole (≤ 4) without additional requirements addressing clinician-algorithm coupling. This is measurable by applying the Void Index assessment protocol to a sample of platforms before and after the August 2026 compliance deadline.

**Falsified if:** Post-compliance assessment of ≥ 10 EU-authorized healthcare AI platforms shows mean Void Index scores either ≤ 4 (constraint pole achieved by regulation alone, falsifying the residual R and C claim) or ≥ 9 (no measurable regulatory effect, falsifying the opacity-reduction prediction).

---

**Prediction 5: Phase IV Pandemonium conditions (Pe > 4) will be associated with measurable clinical skill degradation on cases removed from AI support.**

The framework predicts that clinicians operating in sustained Pe > 4 environments — high-volume AI-augmented diagnostic workflows — will show measurable degradation in unassisted diagnostic accuracy relative to matched clinicians in lower-Pe environments, on case types where both groups have equivalent baseline training. The mechanism is D2 boundary erosion at the skill level: sustained deferral to algorithmic outputs degrades the independent reasoning capacity that the clinician brings to novel cases the algorithm has not encountered. Goddard et al. (2012) and Skitka et al. (1999, *Human Factors*) document automation-induced skill atrophy in aviation and process control; the framework extends this prediction to medicine with the specific Pe threshold of 4 as the transition point above which degradation becomes statistically detectable at twelve-month intervals. Char et al. (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*) identified opacity-induced deskilling as a structural risk without providing a quantitative threshold.

**Falsified if:** A longitudinal cohort study (n ≥ 100 clinicians, 24-month follow-up) finds no statistically significant difference in unassisted diagnostic accuracy between clinicians in Pe > 4 versus Pe < 4 environments on a standardized case battery, or if the direction of the effect is reversed (AI-augmented clinicians outperform on unassisted tasks).

---

Collectively, these five predictions generate a falsification structure that distinguishes the Void Framework from both generic algorithmic fairness critiques and standard human factors accounts. The framework is falsified not if any single prediction fails, but if the ordered, mechanistic relationships — opacity driving agency attribution, attribution driving cascade completion, cascade completion driving harm — are not recovered across multiple independent operationalizations. The twelve-month addressability of Predictions 1 and 3 through existing clinical audit infrastructure means that preliminary falsification data are available before the EU AI Act compliance deadline, providing a regulatory feedback window that the framework's proponents should actively exploit.

## X. Conclusion

The evidence assembled across this analysis converges on a diagnostic verdict that the framework is designed to render precisely: AI-assisted healthcare operates under Phase IV Pandemonium conditions. The compound void produced by stacked opacity — patient unable to interrogate clinician reasoning, clinician unable to interrogate algorithmic reasoning — yields a Void Index score of 9/12 and a Péclet number of 5.6, placing this domain among the highest-risk configurations documented across the twenty-two papers in this series. Only direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical marketing, at 10/12, scores higher within the healthcare vertical. The proximity of these scores is not coincidental. Both configurations share the same structural feature: an authority architecture that patients cannot interrogate, coupled to high-stakes engagement, with responsiveness tuned to emotional rather than epistemic needs. The difference is that AI-assisted diagnosis adds a third layer — the algorithm — whose opacity is categorical rather than contingent. A physician can, in principle, explain a differential diagnosis. A black-box model, by construction, cannot.

The regulatory timeline now imposes external structure on what has been an internally ungoverned opacity architecture. EU AI Act Annex III §5, entering into force 2 August 2026, classifies AI systems deployed in healthcare triage and emergency services as HIGH-RISK, with mandatory transparency and human oversight obligations attached (European Parliament Regulation 2024/1689). This classification is, in Void Framework terms, a legislative attempt to constrain the O dimension — to force the opacity score below the threshold at which the cascade becomes self-sustaining. Whether the implementing provisions achieve this depends entirely on whether transparency requirements are operationalized as genuine reasoning disclosure or as post-hoc summary generation that satisfies documentation requirements while leaving the underlying opacity intact. The FDA's Digital Health Center of Excellence (2023) documented that fewer than 12% of the 521 AI/ML-enabled medical devices authorized between 2019 and 2023 provided clinician-facing explanations of algorithmic outputs. The EU Act's 2026 deadline inherits this legacy. Compliance theaters that generate explanation artifacts without reducing genuine opacity will not lower the Void Index score; they will lower only the score's surface presentation.

The data moat in this vertical is unusually deep, and its depth is a direct function of the void architecture itself. Clinical AI systems are trained on proprietary electronic health record corpora that no independent researcher can access, validated against performance metrics that do not require interpretability, and deployed into clinical workflows where outcome attribution is confounded by the complexity of disease progression, treatment variation, and patient heterogeneity. Obermeyer et al.'s (2019, *Science*) demonstration that a commercial health risk algorithm underestimated illness severity in Black patients by 26.3% required extraordinary investigative effort to reverse-engineer from observable outputs — effort that practicing clinicians cannot replicate during a clinical encounter. The data moat therefore functions as a structural opacity amplifier: the more comprehensive the training corpus, the more defensible the commercial position, and the less accessible the system's reasoning to external scrutiny. Rajpurkar et al. (2022, *Nature Medicine*) documented radiologist-level AUC performance for chest pathology AI alongside a complete absence of interpretable reasoning chains — a configuration that maximizes clinical adoption pressure while minimizing the conditions under which clinicians could detect the errors the system makes.

The drift cascade documented across this paper — D1 agency attribution to the algorithmic oracle, D2 erosion of independent clinical judgment, D3 harm facilitation through automation bias and diagnostic dependence — maps onto the pre-existing infrastructure of the doctor-patient relationship. Balint's (1957) drug doctor provided the foundational insight: the relationship architecture produces effects independent of treatment content. AI-assisted diagnosis does not replace this architecture; it nests within it, adding a second opacity layer while inheriting the trust and authority that the clinical relationship has already established. Char et al. (2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*) identified automation bias, feedback loop degradation, and opacity-induced deskilling as the three downstream harms of this configuration. These are not independent failure modes. They are sequential stages of the drift cascade, named in clinical vocabulary.

The constraint specification — transparency, invariance, independence — identifies the intervention target precisely. Transparent constraints require that algorithmic outputs carry genuine uncertainty quantification and reasoning disclosure accessible to the reviewing clinician. Invariant constraints require that performance validation occur continuously across demographic subgroups, not only at initial authorization. Independent constraints require that clinical judgment retain genuine decisional authority rather than nominal override capacity in systems designed to treat deviation from algorithmic recommendation as an exception requiring documentation. The 2 August 2026 deadline marks the point at which the EU's regulatory architecture formally engages the void. Whether that engagement produces constraint or compliance theater is an empirical question the framework is positioned to answer.
