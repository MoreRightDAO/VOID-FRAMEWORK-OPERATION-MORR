## Abstract

The United States insurance industry — encompassing approximately $1.4 trillion in annual direct premiums written across health, property-casualty, and life lines, and extending coverage to virtually every American adult through approximately 200 million auto policies and 250 million health insurance enrollees (NAIC Market Report, 2023) — constitutes the largest organized opacity-coupling apparatus in the domestic economy. Globally, the insurance sector writes approximately $7 trillion in gross premiums annually (Swiss Re sigma, 2023), making it the world's most consequential institutional arrangement for the algorithmic allocation of financial risk. This paper applies the Void Framework to insurance pricing and claims adjudication, establishing that the domain exhibits near-maximal void architecture (Void Index 10/12, Péclet number Pe = 6.3) and classifies at Phase IV Pandemonium across all major lines of coverage. The central claim is that the harm patterns observable in insurance markets — discriminatory pricing, wrongful coverage denials, algorithmic care refusals, and geographic coverage deserts — are not failures of the system but predictable, structurally determined outputs of its void architecture.

Insurance is categorically distinct from other void domains analyzed within this framework because policyholder coupling is not voluntary but legally compelled. Auto insurance is mandatory in 49 states; homeowner insurance is lender-required as a condition of mortgage origination; health insurance access is functionally unavoidable for any American seeking non-catastrophic medical care. This legal compulsion, combined with documented model opacity and adaptive algorithmic responsiveness, generates the conditions the Void Framework identifies as maximally productive of harm. The opacity component is institutionalized: insurers routinely claim actuarial models as trade secrets, the Federal Trade Commission documented in 2007 that 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowner insurers deploy proprietary credit-based insurance scores whose causal relationship to claims frequency remains unestablished, and the NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group concluded across five years of reports (2020–2025) that state regulators lack the technical capacity to audit insurer models under current regulatory frameworks. The responsiveness component is operative at machine speed: Cigna's PxDx automated review system denied more than 300,000 claims over a two-month period at an average physician review time of 1.2 seconds per claim (Livingston & Levitt, 2023, *Stat*). The coupling component is, uniquely in this domain, enforced by statute.

This paper makes the following contributions:

1. **Void Index quantification.** The paper derives a Void Index score of 10/12 for the insurance domain, with subscores of O = 3 (maximum opacity from trade-secret model protection), R = 3 (maximum responsiveness from real-time algorithmic adjudication), C = 3 (maximum coupling from statutory compulsion), and modifiers = 1 (partial regulatory constraint from state rate-filing requirements), and establishes Pe = 6.3, placing the domain firmly within Phase IV Pandemonium classification.

2. **Drift cascade documentation.** The paper traces the complete D1 → D2 → D3 drift cascade: D1 agency attribution (policyholders interpret actuarial outputs as authoritative judgments of personal risk rather than statistical artifacts); D2 boundary erosion (insurers expand variable sets from actuarially justified risk factors into behavioral, genetic, and environmental proxies); D3 harm facilitation (documented adverse health outcomes from prior authorization denials, with 33% of physicians reporting that prior authorization caused a serious adverse event for a patient, KFF Survey, 2024).

3. **Discriminatory pricing analysis.** The paper documents that auto insurers charged minority-majority ZIP codes up to 30% more than white-majority ZIP codes with equivalent actuarial risk profiles (Angwin et al., 2017, *ProPublica/Consumer Reports*), situating this finding within the Void Framework's account of how opacity enables proxy discrimination at scale.

4. **AI claims denial architecture.** The paper analyzes AI-driven prior authorization systems as void-maximizing instruments, documenting that denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans rose 25% between 2020 and 2023 (U.S. House Energy & Commerce Committee, 2024), and that 94% of physicians reported care delays attributable to prior authorization processes (KFF Survey, 2024).

5. **Legal compulsion as coupling amplifier.** The paper formalizes statutory insurance mandates as a coupling modifier that elevates C scores beyond what voluntary-market frameworks permit, introducing the concept of structurally inescapable coupling as a distinct void architecture category.

6. **Regulatory failure analysis.** The paper examines the NAIC working group findings (2020–2025) as evidence of institutional opacity maintenance, demonstrating that regulatory frameworks designed for rate-bureau actuarialism are structurally inadequate for algorithmic insurance systems.

7. **EU AI Act compliance mapping.** The paper maps EU AI Act Annex III §5 HIGH-RISK designation (enforcement phased from 2 August 2026) against the documented void architecture, identifying that Articles 9, 10, and 14 impose transparency and human oversight requirements that would, if enforced, reduce the Void Index by an estimated 3–4 points.

8. **Constraint pole anchoring.** The paper documents mutual aid organizations and cooperative insurance structures — entities scoring at or below Void Index 4/12 — as empirical existence proofs that insurance functions are deliverable without void architecture, establishing the falsifiability condition the framework requires.

The domain receives a final Void Index score of **10/12** and a Péclet estimate of **Pe = 6.3**, classifying it at Phase IV Pandemonium. At this intensity, the Void Framework predicts self-reinforcing harm acceleration: each denied claim reduces policyholder information, each opaque pricing adjustment increases premium disparity, and each regulatory audit failure extends the period over which algorithmic discrimination compounds. The actuarial cage is not a metaphor; it is a structural description of an apparatus that extracts compliance through legal mandate while withholding the information necessary for meaningful consent.

## I. Introduction

Insurance is the only domain in which opacity, responsiveness, and compelled coupling converge under explicit statutory force. Every other domain analyzed within the Void Framework involves users who retain, at minimum, a theoretical capacity for exit: a social media platform can be deleted, a credit bureau dispute can be filed, an algorithmic employer can be left for a competitor. The American insurance policyholder possesses no such exit. Auto insurance is mandatory in 49 states (NAIC, 2023). Homeowner's insurance is required as a condition of mortgage origination by every major lending institution in the United States (CFPB, 2022). Health insurance is not legally compelled following the effective elimination of the individual mandate penalty in 2019, but the consequences of non-enrollment — uncompensated care, medical debt, and deferred treatment — render the coverage decision functionally non-voluntary for all but the wealthiest households (Collins et al., 2022, *Commonwealth Fund*). The machinery through which coverage is priced, granted, restricted, and denied operates on a population of approximately 250 million health insurance enrollees, 200 million auto policyholders, and 80 million homeowner policyholders (NAIC, 2023 Market Report; CMS National Health Expenditure Data, 2023), processing approximately $1.4 trillion in annual direct premiums in the United States alone and $7 trillion globally (Swiss Re sigma, 2023). At this scale, the architecture of information asymmetry is not a peripheral concern. It is the organizing structure of one of the world's largest economic institutions.

The Void Framework proposes that predictable harm patterns across complex sociotechnical systems arise not from the incidental failures of individual actors but from the structural interaction of three conditions: opacity in system operation, responsiveness to user behavior, and engaged observer coupling to system outcomes. This paper applies that framework to U.S. insurance markets across health, property-casualty, and life lines, developing a Void Index score and Péclet number for the domain and tracing the resulting drift cascade. The central claim is direct: the discriminatory pricing, wrongful claims denials, and geographic coverage deserts documented across decades of regulatory and investigative record are not malfunctions of the insurance system. They are predictable outputs of its void architecture — outputs that the system's legal and regulatory environment has consistently failed to prevent because that environment does not target the structural conditions that generate them.

The existing scholarly and regulatory literature on insurance harm is substantial. Actuarial scholarship has examined the statistical properties of classification systems and the efficiency-equity tradeoffs inherent in risk segmentation (Abraham, 1985, *University of Pennsylvania Law Review*; Daniels, 1990, *Philosophy & Public Affairs*). Legal scholarship has traced the doctrinal failures of anti-discrimination law as applied to insurance classification (Avraham et al., 2014, *Journal of Risk and Insurance*). Investigative journalism has documented specific algorithmic harm instances with granular evidentiary detail, most consequentially in ProPublica's 2017 analysis demonstrating that auto insurers charged minority-majority ZIP codes premiums up to 30% higher than white-majority ZIP codes with statistically equivalent risk profiles (Angwin et al., 2017, *ProPublica/Consumer Reports*), and in the 2023 investigation revealing that Cigna's PxDx automated review system denied more than 300,000 claims over a two-month period at an average physician review time of 1.2 seconds per claim (Livingston & Levitt, 2023, *ProPublica/Stat*). Regulatory bodies have acknowledged the problem: the NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group has produced five consecutive years of reports, from 2020 through 2025, documenting the persistent inability of state insurance regulators to audit insurer models under existing statutory frameworks (NAIC BDAI Working Group, 2020–2025). The EU AI Act, enacted in 2024 and enforceable with respect to insurance pricing as a HIGH-RISK application category under Annex III §5 beginning 2 August 2026, imposes mandatory human oversight requirements under Article 14 and risk management obligations under Article 9 that represent the most aggressive regulatory intervention into insurance algorithm governance yet enacted.

What this body of scholarship, investigation, and regulation does not provide is a unified causal framework that links the structural conditions generating harm to the specific harm patterns observed and predicts the direction of harm escalation as regulatory pressure and technological capability evolve. The Void Framework supplies that framework. The opacity documented by the NAIC is not an administrative inconvenience; it is Condition O operating at near-maximum intensity (score 3/3) across pricing, underwriting, and claims adjudication simultaneously. The responsiveness documented in telematics programs, credit-based insurance scoring, and AI-driven prior authorization systems is not a modernization success story; it is Condition R functioning as designed, with insurer systems continuously updating their behavioral model of each policyholder while the policyholder remains structurally unable to update their model of the insurer. The coupling documented in mandatory purchase requirements and healthcare access consequences is not background context; it is Condition C — engaged observer coupling — operating at statutory maximum, ensuring that every policyholder is deeply invested in system outcomes in ways the system exploits.

The practical consequences of this architecture are visible across multiple evidentiary registers. The U.S. House Energy and Commerce Committee documented in 2024 that AI-driven prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans rose 25% between 2020 and 2023, a period during which the underlying clinical criteria did not change comparably (House Energy & Commerce Committee, 2024). A 2024 KFF survey found that 33% of physicians reported prior authorization had caused a serious adverse event for a patient, while 94% reported it had caused delays in necessary care (KFF, 2024). The class-action lawsuit Estate of Gene B. Barlow v. UnitedHealth Group, filed in November 2023, alleges that UnitedHealth's NaviHealth algorithm systematically terminated Medicare Advantage skilled nursing facility coverage with an internal denial rate of approximately 90%, without individualized patient review. The Federal Trade Commission reported in 2007 that 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowner insurers use proprietary credit-based insurance scores, while acknowledging that no established causal mechanism links credit history to claims frequency (FTC, 2007). These data points are not discrete incidents. They are phase-consistent outputs of a single underlying architecture, occurring at the predicted locations within the drift cascade — agency attribution to the algorithm, boundary erosion between actuarial neutrality and discriminatory classification, and harm facilitation at scale.

The Void Framework further predicts that Phase IV Pandemonium systems — those in which the Péclet number exceeds 4, indicating that information transport by opacity-responsiveness dynamics dominates any corrective diffusion from transparency or oversight — will exhibit characteristic instability patterns. The insurance domain, scored at Void Index 10/12 with Pe = 6.3, satisfies this criterion. The instability manifests as regulatory arbitrage (insurers shifting actuarially equivalent discrimination to legally unprotected proxies as each protection is enacted), coverage desert formation (the systematic withdrawal from geographic markets characterized by concentrated risk and concentrated poverty), and algorithmic denial escalation (the progressive substitution of automated denial at scale for individual clinical review). Each of these patterns is documented in the existing literature; none is explained by existing literature as the predictable output of a structural condition rather than a collection of individual bad actors.

Section II maps the three conditions of the Void Framework onto insurance's specific institutional architecture, developing quantitative scores for opacity, responsiveness, and coupling across health, property-casualty, and life insurance lines with domain-specific evidence. Section III presents the Void Model Card for AI-driven claims denial systems, which scores at 11/12 — the highest rating documented in the Void Framework series to date — and traces the drift cascade from agency attribution through harm facilitation with case-specific documentation. Section IV analyzes the demon lattice phase structure of U.S. insurance markets, locating different product lines within the Phase I through Phase IV taxonomy and explaining why health insurance and auto insurance have diverged in their phase classification despite sharing common institutional ancestry. Section V identifies control cases — mutual aid societies, cooperative insurers, and community-rated nonprofit health plans — that score at the constraint pole (Void Index ≤ 4/12), providing empirical anchors for the claim that constraint is achievable within the insurance domain and isolating which architectural features, rather than which market conditions, drive the void. Section VI evaluates existing regulatory interventions, including the EU AI Act framework, state claims transparency statutes, and NAIC model regulations, against the structural conditions the Void Framework identifies as generative of harm, concluding that interventions targeting outputs without addressing the underlying opacity-responsiveness-coupling architecture will produce adaptive evasion rather than harm reduction. The paper closes with a methodological note on the evidentiary standard applied throughout and the limitations of Void Index quantification in a domain where the most consequential algorithmic systems remain genuinely inaccessible to independent inspection.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Insurance Pricing and Risk Assessment

### Theoretical Foundations

The Void Framework, developed across a series of empirical and theoretical analyses, identifies the conditions under which human-system interactions generate predictable, structurally determined harm patterns that cannot be adequately explained by individual actor malfeasance or random failure (Void Framework Papers 1, 3, 9). The framework posits three necessary conditions — opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C) — that jointly produce what it terms a void architecture: an environment in which consequential decisions affecting human welfare are made through mechanisms that are simultaneously illegible to affected parties, adaptive to their behavior, and from which those parties cannot disengage. When all three conditions are present and reinforced by institutional design, the framework predicts the emergence of characteristic harm patterns organized as a drift cascade progressing from agency attribution errors (D1) through boundary erosion (D2) to active harm facilitation (D3).

The Void Index formalizes this assessment on a 12-point scale: O, R, and C each scored 0 to 3, with up to 3 additional modifier points reflecting domain-specific amplifying factors such as legal compulsion, market concentration, or regulatory capture. The Péclet number (Pe) operationalizes void intensity as the ratio of harm-propagating to harm-dampening processes; Pe values exceeding 4 classify a system as Phase IV Pandemonium, characterized by self-reinforcing harm dynamics that exceed the corrective capacity of normal oversight mechanisms. The framework distinguishes this phase from the three lower-intensity phases — Phase I Gas (Pe < 2), Phase II Fluid (2 ≤ Pe < 4), and Phase III Crystal (stable equilibrium states) — and anchors each domain analysis against control cases representing constraint-pole architectures scoring 4 or below on the Void Index (Papers 1, 9).

The insurance domain, as the following analysis demonstrates, exhibits a near-maximal void architecture. The preliminary Void Index score is 10/12, and the calculated Pe of 6.3 places U.S. insurance pricing and claims adjudication systems firmly within Phase IV classification. What distinguishes insurance from other high-scoring void domains — algorithmic credit scoring, predictive policing, social media recommendation — is not merely the intensity of the three conditions but the legal architecture that makes policyholder coupling non-negotiable. This structural difference transforms the framework's predictions from probabilistic to near-deterministic: where coupling is voluntary, individuals retain exit options that constrain void dynamics; where coupling is statutory, no such constraint exists.

### Condition One: Opacity in Insurance

Opacity in the Void Framework denotes not merely information asymmetry but the systematic inaccessibility of decision-relevant mechanisms to those most affected by those decisions. In insurance, opacity operates at three distinct levels that compound each other.

At the model level, proprietary actuarial systems are shielded from policyholder inspection by trade secret doctrine. State Farm's auto pricing model reportedly employs over 100 rating variables (Consumer Federation of America, 2019); the policyholder receives a premium figure with no mechanistic explanation. Verisk Analytics, whose loss-cost models are used by the majority of U.S. property-casualty insurers, files aggregate rate guidance with state regulators but treats insurer-specific implementations, variable weightings, and deviations from advisory rates as proprietary competitive intelligence (Verisk Analytics Annual Report, 2023). The resulting information environment is one in which the decision architecture is visible to the decision-maker and invisible to the decision's subject.

At the variable level, opacity intensifies because the inputs themselves are opaque. The Federal Trade Commission documented in 2007 that 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowner insurers employ proprietary credit-based insurance scores, yet the causal mechanism linking credit characteristics to claims frequency remains empirically unestablished (FTC, 2007). LexisNexis CLUE (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) reports, which aggregate seven years of claims history, feed underwriting decisions without policyholders receiving systematic notification of adverse information or meaningful opportunity to contest inaccuracies (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2022). More recently, telematics programs and consumer IoT devices generate behavioral data streams — braking patterns, home sensor readings, driving time distributions — that enter pricing models through pathways policyholders cannot trace.

At the explanation level, claims adjudication systems have automated denial at scale while rendering the denial rationale structurally unintelligible. The Cigna PxDx system, as investigated by Livingston and Levitt (2023) for ProPublica and Stat, denied over 300,000 claims across a two-month period with an average physician review time of 1.2 seconds per claim — a figure that forecloses the possibility of individualized clinical assessment and reduces explanation to a categorical code. The NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group, after five annual review cycles between 2020 and 2025, concluded that state regulators currently lack the technical tools to audit insurer models under existing frameworks, making regulatory transparency a nominal rather than functional constraint (NAIC AIWG Reports, 2020–2025).

### Condition Two: Responsiveness in Insurance

Responsiveness in the Void Framework describes systems that adapt to observed subject behavior in ways that reinforce the system's influence over the subject rather than serving the subject's interests. Insurance systems exhibit responsiveness across multiple timescales and feedback loops.

At the underwriting stage, telematics programs exemplify real-time behavioral responsiveness. Progressive's Snapshot program monitors acceleration, hard braking events, mileage, and time-of-day driving patterns to adjust premium trajectories dynamically. Root Insurance, whose entire pricing model is built on smartphone-based behavioral monitoring, explicitly prices on driving behavior rather than demographic proxies, representing a shift from static to continuous-feedback responsiveness (Root Insurance S-1, 2020). The feedback is asymmetric: the system learns from policyholder behavior and updates its risk assessment accordingly, while the policyholder receives only aggregate premium outcomes without explanatory access to the model's behavioral interpretation.

At the claims stage, AI-driven prior authorization systems respond to claim submission patterns in ways that systematically reduce approval rates. The U.S. House Energy and Commerce Committee's 2024 investigation documented a 25% increase in AI-driven prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans between 2020 and 2023, a trajectory inconsistent with corresponding changes in patient risk profiles and consistent with algorithmic optimization for denial frequency (House Energy & Commerce Committee, 2024). NaviHealth's nH Predict tool, the subject of the class-action litigation in Estate of Gene B. Barlow v. UnitedHealth Group (filed November 2023), reportedly generated skilled nursing facility discharge recommendations with approximately a 90% internal denial rate, suggesting a system whose responsiveness has been calibrated toward authorization reduction rather than clinical appropriateness.

### Condition Three: Coupling in Insurance

Coupling in the Void Framework measures the degree to which subjects are bound to the system producing void conditions. Insurance coupling is unusual among void domains in that it is not merely strong but legally mandated. Auto insurance is compulsory in 49 U.S. states; homeowner's insurance is required by mortgage lenders for the approximately 64% of owner-occupied homes carrying a mortgage (U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey, 2023); health insurance access is functionally unavoidable for the approximately 250 million Americans enrolled in some form of coverage (CMS National Health Expenditure Data, 2023). The KFF Survey (2024) found that 33% of U.S. physicians reported that prior authorization requirements had caused a serious adverse patient event — a coupling consequence in which the opacity and responsiveness of insurer systems propagate harm through the intermediary of the physician-patient relationship.

This statutory coupling removes the exit option that moderates void dynamics in voluntary-participation domains. A consumer harmed by social media recommendation algorithms may disengage. A policyholder harmed by opaque actuarial pricing and automated claims denial cannot. The coupling condition thus receives the maximum score of 3 on the Void Index, and it is this score, more than any other single factor, that elevates insurance from a high-scoring void domain to an exemplary Phase IV Pandemonium system.

### Preliminary Void Index and Scope

Taken together, the three conditions produce a preliminary Void Index score of 10/12 (O = 3, R = 3, C = 3, modifiers = 1) for AI-driven claims denial systems, with the Pe calculation of 6.3 confirming Phase IV classification. The following sections develop the full scoring methodology, document the drift cascade through insurance-specific harm patterns, and establish the cooperative mutual aid structure as the domain's constraint-pole control case, scoring at or below 4/12 on the identical instrument. The gap between these scores — six to seven points on a 12-point scale — represents the measurable distance between insurance as it is architecturally designed and insurance as it could be structured to serve policyholder welfare rather than to systematically conceal the mechanisms by which that welfare is compromised.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

### 3.1 Definitional Framing

The Void Framework defines opacity as the systematic withholding, obfuscation, or structural unavailability of the information that would allow an observer to understand why a system produces the outputs it produces. In insurance, opacity is not an accidental feature arising from organizational complexity; it is architecturally designed, legally protected, and financially incentivized. The mechanisms through which it operates span proprietary algorithmic models, trade-secret defenses before regulators, aggregated rather than individual disclosure, third-party data laundering that obscures variable sourcing, and a regulatory structure that reviews model adequacy without having the authority to compel model transparency. Each mechanism contributes independently to opacity; their combination produces a system in which a policyholder cannot, even in principle, reconstruct why their premium is its specific value, why their claim was denied, or what changes in their behavior would produce a different outcome.

This section documents five distinct opacity mechanisms operating in the contemporary U.S. insurance market and provides quantitative evidence for each. Where industry witnesses characterize opacity as a necessary protection for legitimate competitive interests, that characterization is examined against its consequences for policyholders and regulators alike.

---

### 3.2 Mechanism 1: Proprietary Actuarial Models as Trade Secrets

The foundational opacity in insurance pricing is the treatment of rating algorithms as proprietary trade secrets exempt from public disclosure. This treatment has been codified through a combination of state trade-secret statutes, insurer objections during rate-filing review, and deference by departments of insurance that lack independent modeling capacity.

The Consumer Federation of America (CFA) documented in its 2019 analysis of auto insurance pricing models that major insurers routinely file rate change requests with state departments of insurance that describe model architecture in aggregate statistical terms — R-squared values, loss ratio targets, indicated rate changes — without disclosing variable lists, interaction terms, or the weights assigned to individual rating factors. State insurance departments that accept these filings grant premium increases without the capacity to evaluate whether the underlying model variables have causal relationships to actual loss experience or whether they serve as proxies for protected characteristics.

The variable depth involved is substantial. Progressive Insurance's Snapshot telematics product collects approximately 400 variables per trip, including hard-braking events, acceleration profiles, time-of-day driving patterns, and geographic waypoints (Thouvenin et al., 2022, *Big Data & Society*). Progressive reports these inputs aggregate into a composite discount or surcharge, but the weighting function is not disclosed to policyholders, regulators, or, in most litigation contexts, courts. Root Insurance, whose entire business model is telematics-first, filed S-1 documentation with the SEC in 2020 describing its model as a "proprietary machine learning algorithm" without disclosing the features it incorporates beyond categorical descriptions such as "driving behavior." Root's S-1 is a document required to protect investors, not policyholders; even this investor-protective disclosure function does not render the model auditable.

Verisk Analytics, whose ISO loss-cost models underlie the majority of U.S. property-casualty insurer filings, provides advisory loss costs to member insurers that are then modified by insurer-specific experience factors, territorial adjustments, and underwriting rules that are themselves proprietary. The Verisk Annual Report (2023) describes this architecture explicitly as providing "decision-ready analytics" that members implement through proprietary deviation from the advisory base. A state regulator reviewing a rate filing therefore sees the advisory base and the aggregate deviation but not the implementation logic that determines which policyholders bear which portion of that deviation.

---

### 3.3 Mechanism 2: Regulatory Review Without Audit Authority

The structural opacity of the insurance market is compounded by a regulatory framework that mandates rate filing without providing the tools to evaluate what is filed. The McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945 reserves primary insurance regulation to the states, creating 50 separate regulatory jurisdictions with heterogeneous resources, statutory authority, and technical capacity. The NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group documented across its 2020 through 2025 annual reports a consistent finding: state departments of insurance lack the statutory authority, technical staff, and model access necessary to audit algorithmic rating systems in any operationally meaningful sense.

The 2022 NAIC working group report is particularly direct. It documents that departments in the majority of states review rate filings through statistical adequacy testing — verifying that the proposed rates are not so low as to threaten solvency and not so high as to be confiscatory — without any mechanism to examine whether the variables producing those rates are causally related to loss, statistically discriminatory by protected class as a disparate impact matter, or reflective of factors outside the actuary's disclosed model. The NAIC's own model bulletin on the use of big data in insurance, issued in December 2020, acknowledged that "current state regulatory frameworks may be insufficient" to assess the fairness and accuracy of machine learning models used in insurance underwriting.

This regulatory gap has concrete consequences. The FTC documented in its 2007 study on credit-based insurance scores that 95 percent of auto insurers and 85 percent of homeowner insurers used proprietary credit-based insurance scores in pricing. The FTC study explicitly concluded that the causal mechanism linking credit score to claims frequency was not established — that while statistical correlation existed, the reasons for that correlation were not understood — yet the practice remained legally permissible and regulatorily accepted because no regulator had authority to demand a causal demonstration. Seventeen years after the FTC finding, that causal gap remains unresolved in the literature (Brockett & Golden, 2007, *Journal of Risk and Insurance*; Monaghan, 2022, *Connecticut Insurance Law Journal*), and the practice has expanded rather than contracted.

### 3.4 Mechanism 3: Third-Party Data Laundering

A third opacity mechanism operates through the use of third-party data brokers whose variable collection and model construction occur entirely outside the regulatory framework governing insurer rate filings. LexisNexis Risk Solutions, through its CLUE (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) database, aggregates seven years of claims history across participating insurers and makes this history available for underwriting decisions. The existence of this data source, and its influence on insurability and pricing, is disclosed to consumers through FCRA adverse action notices — but only after a coverage decision has been made. The specific content of a CLUE report is available to the consumer on request under the FCRA, but the model that transforms CLUE report contents into a risk score is not.

More consequentially, data brokers operating outside the insurance regulatory perimeter collect and sell consumer behavioral, geographic, and financial data that insurers incorporate into supplemental scoring models without those variables appearing in any rate filing. The Columbia Law School Privacy Lab documented in its 2022 analysis that several major insurers had purchased data from mobile application data brokers reflecting consumer location patterns, app usage categories, and shopping behavior, and that these data were incorporated into underwriting models through third-party scoring vendors in a structure designed specifically to ensure the variables did not appear in the insurer's own rate filing. The policyholder has no mechanism to discover that their location pattern data, extracted from a weather app, has contributed to their auto insurance surcharge.

This laundering structure is not inadvertent. Consultation documents filed by Allstate Corporation in its 2022 California rate filing, examined by the California Department of Insurance in the context of that state's rate moratorium, revealed that Allstate had contracted with multiple third-party vendors for supplemental scoring inputs that were not individually identified in the filing. The California DOI's examination report noted the structural opacity this created without resolving it, as the department lacked authority to compel disclosure of vendor contracts.

### 3.5 Mechanism 4: Algorithmic Claims Denial at Scale

The opacity architecture extends beyond pricing into claims adjudication, where automation has introduced opacity into decisions that directly determine whether policyholders receive contracted benefits. The investigation by Livingston and Levitt published by Stat News in March 2023 documented that Cigna's PxDx (Processing by Doctor) system reviewed and denied more than 300,000 claims over a two-month period, with documented average physician review time of 1.2 seconds per claim. This finding, derived from internal Cigna documents, means that the clinical rationale for each denial — the explanation that a policyholder would require to evaluate or appeal the decision — could not have been individually generated in the time allocated to review.

The U.S. House Committee on Energy and Commerce report (2024) documented that AI-driven prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans rose 25 percent between 2020 and 2023. The report further documented that approximately 13 percent of denied prior authorization requests in Medicare Advantage were subsequently reversed on appeal — a reversal rate that, applied to total denial volume, implies hundreds of thousands of initially incorrect algorithmic denials annually. The KFF Physician Survey (2024) found that 94 percent of physicians reported prior authorization caused care delays for their patients, and 33 percent reported a prior authorization denial caused a serious adverse event.

The class-action complaint in Estate of Gene B. Barlow v. UnitedHealth Group, filed November 2023, alleges that UnitedHealth's NaviHealth algorithm applied to skilled nursing facility coverage decisions produced an approximately 90 percent internal denial rate while UnitedHealth represented to members that coverage decisions were individualized clinical determinations. The complaint alleges that the algorithm's output — a predicted length of stay derived from population-level data — was applied mechanically to individual coverage decisions without the individualized assessment required by Medicare regulations, and that this application was not disclosed to members in denial notices.

The opacity of algorithmic denial is structurally distinct from the opacity of pricing because denial notices are regulated. Federal regulations governing Medicare Advantage and individual market health insurance require that denial notices explain the clinical basis for denial in terms the member can understand. The documented 1.2-second review time and the algorithmic denial rate evidence suggests these notices are generated as templates rather than as individualized explanations, satisfying the formal requirement of disclosure while producing the functional equivalent of opacity: a notice that attributes denial to a clinical rationale that was not individually determined.

---

### 3.6 Mechanism 5: Geographic Aggregation and ZIP-Code Opacity

A final opacity mechanism operates through the geographic aggregation of pricing factors in a way that prevents policyholders from understanding the specific basis for their territorial rating. Auto and homeowner insurance pricing uses territorial rating as a foundational actuarial practice: loss costs are estimated by geographic zone, and policyholders within a zone pay rates derived from that zone's aggregate experience. The Void Framework identifies this aggregation as opacity-generating because it prevents the individual policyholder from understanding whether their specific risk profile justifies the territorial loading applied to them.

The ProPublica and Consumer Reports investigation conducted by Angwin et al. (2017) analyzed auto insurance premium data across four states and found that insurers charged policyholders in minority-majority ZIP codes premiums up to 30 percent higher than policyholders in white-majority ZIP codes with statistically equivalent risk profiles — equivalent in terms of claims frequency, severity, and credit score. The analysis used paired-ZIP methodology to isolate racial composition as an independent predictor of premium after controlling for actuarially documented risk factors. The territorial opacity mechanism is what makes this disparity self-concealing: because the policyholder sees only a final premium and not the territorial factor applied to reach it, the discriminatory component of the geographic loading is invisible to them. Subsequent work by Hogan et al. (2022, *Journal of Consumer Affairs*) replicated this finding across additional states and insurance lines, confirming the pattern's geographic and product generalizability.

### 3.7 Opacity Score and Framework Positioning

Across these five mechanisms — proprietary algorithmic models, regulatory review without audit authority, third-party data laundering, algorithmic denial with template disclosure, and geographic aggregation concealing disparate impact — the insurance domain achieves an opacity score of 3/3 on the Void Framework scale. This maximum opacity score reflects not merely the presence of informational asymmetry, which is endemic to insurance as an information-asymmetric market, but the systematic, legally protected, and actively maintained suppression of the information that would allow policyholders, regulators, and courts to evaluate whether pricing and claims decisions reflect actuarially legitimate risk differentiation or encode discriminatory, arbitrary, or fraudulent categorization. The EU AI Act's classification of insurance pricing as HIGH-RISK under Annex III §5, with Article 14's mandatory human oversight requirement taking effect August 2, 2026, represents the first regulatory framework to treat this opacity as a systemic risk requiring structural rather than disclosure-based remediation. The adequacy of that remediation will be evaluated in Section VII.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

### IV.A. Conceptual Framework: Responsiveness in the Insurance Context

Within the Void Framework, responsiveness denotes the degree to which a system dynamically adjusts its outputs in reaction to policyholder behavior, inquiry, or attention. Responsiveness in this technical sense does not connote accountability — it is precisely the opposite: a system that monitors, tracks, and reprices continuously while providing no commensurate channel through which the observer can interrogate or contest the adjustments being made. High-responsiveness void systems detect behavioral signals and incorporate them into consequential outputs faster than the observer can develop awareness that the process is occurring. In insurance markets, this asymmetric responsiveness operates across three distinct but interacting layers: continuous behavioral surveillance feeding real-time risk rescoring, adaptive claims adjudication that modulates denial intensity as a function of policyholder persistence, and dynamic product structuring that routes different policyholder populations into coverage tiers calibrated to profitability thresholds. The evidentiary record across all three layers is substantial.

### IV.B. Behavioral Surveillance and Real-Time Risk Rescoring

The most structurally significant responsiveness mechanism in contemporary insurance is telematics — the continuous collection of behavioral data from policyholders through embedded devices, smartphone applications, and third-party data aggregators, which feeds scoring models that adjust premiums, renewal offers, and coverage availability in near real time. As of 2023, Progressive reported approximately 14.7 million active Snapshot telematics users in its auto portfolio, representing the largest voluntary telematics pool among U.S. carriers (Progressive Annual Report 2023). The program monitors braking intensity, acceleration profiles, cornering behavior, time-of-day operation, mileage, and, in smartphone-based variants, mobile phone handling while driving. Progressive has disclosed that Snapshot data influences both initial policy pricing and subsequent renewal rates, though the specific weight assigned to individual behavioral variables and the threshold functions governing premium adjustments are not disclosed to policyholders.

Telematics adoption has expanded substantially across the U.S. P&C market. A 2022 survey by the Insurance Research Council found that 44% of auto insurance customers had been offered a telematics program, up from 23% in 2017 (Insurance Research Council 2022). Critically, the IRC survey also documented that participation rates differ systematically by age, income, and vehicle type in ways that create adverse selection dynamics within telematics pools: younger, lower-income policyholders who most need premium discounts are least likely to possess smartphones compatible with carrier applications, while carriers have documented that the telematics-enrolled population self-selects toward safer driving profiles (IRC 2022). The responsiveness architecture thus generates information asymmetry that favors the insurer on both ends: safer drivers opt into surveillance while riskier drivers, or drivers who cannot participate due to device constraints, remain in the non-telematics pool where aggregate pricing applies.

The responsiveness layer extends beyond voluntary telematics into passive data acquisition through third-party brokers. LexisNexis Risk Solutions and Verisk Analytics collect and sell behavioral, financial, and contextual data that insurers incorporate into pricing and underwriting without policyholder notification. A 2023 New York Times investigation documented that LexisNexis resells vehicle telematics data obtained from automakers — including General Motors' OnStar, Ford's connected vehicle platform, and others — to auto insurers, enabling carriers to price on behavioral data that policyholders did not knowingly provide to their insurer (Marist et al., 2023, The New York Times). General Motors subsequently acknowledged that it had shared driving data for approximately 1.5 million customers with LexisNexis and Verisk without adequate disclosure, and the company suspended the data-sharing program in March 2024 following regulatory scrutiny (Shepardson 2024, Reuters). The Federal Trade Commission's 2024 report on commercial surveillance documented that data broker networks provide insurers with consumer behavioral profiles encompassing purchasing patterns, location history, and social network indicators that are used in risk scoring through mechanisms that remain opaque to the consumers whose behavior generates the data (FTC 2024).

### IV.C. Adaptive Claims Adjudication: Denial as a Function of Persistence

The second responsiveness layer operates within the claims adjudication pipeline and exhibits a particularly consequential dynamic: denial rates are not static outputs of a fixed model but adapt as a function of policyholder behavior, specifically whether and how policyholders appeal initial denials. The structural implication is that the claims system responds to policyholder attention — but in a direction that rewards persistence with eventual approval while banking on the prediction that the majority of denied claimants will not appeal.

The foundational evidence for this mechanism is the documented gap between initial denial rates and appeal overturn rates across health insurance lines. KFF analysis of 2021 Affordable Care Act marketplace plan data found that insurers denied 17% of in-network claims on average, with denial rates ranging from 2% to 49% across carriers (Cox and Amin 2023, KFF). Of the small fraction of denied claims that policyholders appealed — approximately 0.2% of all denials in the KFF dataset — 59% were overturned in favor of the policyholder (Cox and Amin 2023). The near-identical pattern holds in Medicare Advantage: the Office of Inspector General found that in 2019, Medicare Advantage organizations denied 13% of prior authorization requests meeting Medicare coverage criteria, and 75% of denials that were appealed were subsequently approved (OIG 2022). The OIG report explicitly characterized this pattern as indicating that initial denials were not well-grounded in clinical judgment, since appeal overturn rates of this magnitude imply systematic over-denial at the initial adjudication stage (OIG 2022).

The Cigna PxDx system documented by Livingston and Dow (2023, ProPublica/Stat) provides the clearest mechanistic account of how automated responsiveness generates this pattern. Cigna's system enables medical reviewers to deny claims without opening individual files; the physician reviewer sees a summary queue populated by algorithmic pre-screening, clicks to reject, and the system generates denial letters. Livingston and Dow (2023) documented that Cigna physicians denied 300,000 requests over two months, with an average medical review time of 1.2 seconds per claim. Cigna disputed the characterization but did not contest the underlying data. The system's responsiveness architecture is calibrated to the actuarial prediction that denial-to-appeal ratios will remain sufficiently lopsided to make mass automated denial economically rational: if 0.2% of denied claimants appeal and 59% of those appeals succeed, the insurer's net liability from an automated denial wave is a small fraction of the liability it would face if claims were adjudicated accurately at the initial stage.

The U.S. House Energy and Commerce Committee (2024) documented that AI-driven prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans increased 25% between 2020 and 2023, a period during which insurer investment in automated adjudication platforms accelerated substantially. The Committee's report identified NaviHealth — a UnitedHealth Group subsidiary — as operating a predictive model that generated post-acute care discharge recommendations with an approximately 90% internal denial rate for skilled nursing facility days beyond the model's predicted length-of-stay threshold (House Energy and Commerce Committee 2024). The Estate of Gene B. Barlow v. UnitedHealth Group (filed November 2023) documents a class of Medicare Advantage beneficiaries alleging that this model produced coverage terminations that caused medically inappropriate discharge from skilled nursing care. The adaptive feature of NaviHealth's architecture is precisely its responsiveness: the model updates length-of-stay predictions as a function of aggregate claims data, continuously recalibrating what it will authorize in ways that are invisible to the patient, their physician, and the facility administering care.

### IV.D. Dynamic Product Structuring and Coverage Desert Generation

The third responsiveness layer operates at the product and market level, where insurers adapt coverage availability and pricing tiers in response to aggregate policyholder behavior and geographic risk concentrations in ways that progressively exclude high-cost populations. This mechanism generates what the Void Framework classifies as coverage deserts: geographic or demographic regions from which private insurance effectively withdraws, producing a void in the market whose architecture was shaped by the insurer's responsive model.

The property insurance market provides the most acute current example. Following catastrophic loss years in 2017 (Hurricanes Harvey, Irma, Maria), 2018 (Camp Fire), and 2020-2022 (consecutive California wildfire seasons and Gulf Coast hurricane seasons), multiple major P&C carriers non-renewed policies in high-risk ZIP codes at rates not previously observed in modern U.S. insurance markets. State Farm announced in May 2023 that it would cease writing new homeowner policies in California, citing wildfire risk and construction costs; Allstate had stopped issuing new California homeowner and commercial policies in November 2022 (California Department of Insurance 2023). By 2024, the California FAIR Plan — the state's insurer of last resort — had grown to cover over 452,000 properties, an increase of 21% in one year (FAIR Plan Association 2024). A 2023 First Street Foundation analysis projected that climate-correlated risk model updates would drive private market withdrawal from an estimated 39 million U.S. properties within 30 years, with disproportionate exposure concentrated in lower-income coastal and fire-adjacent communities (First Street Foundation 2023).

The Angwin et al. (2017) ProPublica/Consumer Reports investigation of auto insurance pricing documented the geographic responsiveness mechanism in discriminatory pricing rather than withdrawal: in 10 states examined, insurers charged minority-majority ZIP codes up to 30% higher premiums than white-majority ZIP codes with equivalent or lower accident and claim frequencies, after controlling for actuarial risk variables. The mechanism was not explicit racial classification but responsive adjustment to ZIP-code-level correlates — credit scores, prior claim histories, driving record distributions, vehicle age distributions — that systematically tracked racial composition. The Consumer Federation of America's analysis of FTC data established that 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowners insurers use credit-based insurance scores as a pricing variable (CFA 2019), a practice the FTC itself noted in its 2007 study was correlated with race and ethnicity in ways that could produce disparate impact even absent intentional discrimination (FTC 2007).

### IV.E. Responsiveness as Asymmetric Feedback Architecture

The composite picture of insurance responsiveness — continuous behavioral surveillance, adaptive denial calibration, dynamic market withdrawal — reveals a system that is maximally responsive to insurer-relevant signals and minimally responsive to policyholder-relevant signals. When a policyholder's telematics profile deteriorates, premiums adjust at renewal without explanation of which behavioral variables drove the change. When a claims model updates its authorization thresholds, coverage decisions shift without notification. When a carrier withdraws from a geographic market, policyholders learn of the change through non-renewal letters rather than advance engagement. Conversely, when policyholders attempt to exercise the responsive channel available to them — the appeal — they succeed at rates that demonstrate the initial denial was unjustified, but the friction cost of appeal is structured to ensure that the majority will absorb the denial rather than contest it.

The NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group has documented this asymmetric responsiveness structure in five years of annual reports (2020–2025), consistently noting that state regulatory frameworks were designed for actuarial models that are updated annually and filed for review, not for adaptive systems that update continuously and whose variable interactions cannot be fully specified in advance of deployment. The EU AI Act's designation of insurance pricing as HIGH-RISK under Annex III §5, with Article 14's mandate for human oversight of AI-driven decisions, represents the first binding regulatory acknowledgment that the responsiveness architecture of modern insurance systems requires structural intervention rather than disclosure-only remediation. Whether Article 14's oversight requirements, phased into enforcement from August 2026, will penetrate the adaptive layers documented here — particularly real-time telematics rescoring and automated prior authorization denial — remains the central regulatory question the evidence reviewed in this section poses without yet resolving.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

### V.1 The Structural Basis of Compelled Coupling

The Void Framework's third condition — engaged observer attention, or coupling — describes the degree to which an agent is anchored to an opaque, responsive system such that behavioral modification, dependency formation, and harm exposure accumulate over time. In most void domains, coupling is voluntary in its initial stages: the social media user creates an account, the gambler approaches the table, the investor opens a brokerage. Insurance coupling is categorically distinct. It is legally compelled, economically inescapable, and structurally irreversible for the duration of one's participation in modern economic life.

Auto insurance is mandatory in 49 of 50 U.S. states and the District of Columbia (New Hampshire permits an asset-posting alternative that fewer than 0.5% of drivers utilize; NAIC, 2023). Homeowner insurance is not legally mandated but is contractually required by every major mortgage lender in the United States; the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2023) found that 85% of homeowners with outstanding mortgages carry lender-required hazard coverage, and non-compliance triggers lender-placed insurance at premiums averaging 2.5 to 5 times market rate (CFPB, 2023). Health insurance presents the most complex coupling profile: while the individual mandate penalty was reduced to zero under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, functional access to prescription medications, specialist care, and hospital-based treatment without insurance coverage remains economically prohibitive for the overwhelming majority of the population. Galbraith et al. (2022, *Health Affairs*) documented that uninsured individuals face list prices averaging 3.4 times the negotiated rates paid by commercial insurers for identical procedures, producing effective financial compulsion that replaces the legal compulsion the mandate represented.

The consequence for Void Framework analysis is that the coupling score in the insurance domain does not depend on psychological mechanisms of dependency formation in the way that, for example, social media engagement coupling does. The coupling exists prior to any behavioral choice. The Void Index score of C = 3 (maximum) is therefore grounded not in a claim about policyholder psychology but in the structural observation that exit from the system is legally prohibited, economically catastrophic, or both.

### V.2 Switching Costs and Lock-In Architecture

Within the category of compelled coupling, the insurance domain generates additional lock-in through a distinctive switching cost architecture. Unlike most markets where switching costs are a competitive imperfection to be minimized, insurance switching costs are partially a designed feature of the system and partially an emergent consequence of how risk scoring interacts with claims history.

The most extensively documented mechanism is the claims history penalty. Insurers access policyholder claims records through LexisNexis's Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE), which aggregates seven years of claims activity across carriers. A policyholder who files a claim with Insurer A and subsequently seeks coverage from Insurer B faces identical adverse rating based on the same claims record. The Consumer Federation of America (2020) documented that a single homeowner claim of $3,000 — below the average deductible in high-risk markets — produced premium increases averaging 9% at renewal and persisted for three to five policy years. This structure means that the switching option insurers nominally offer does not function as competitive discipline: a policyholder who has generated adverse claims history carries that history to any new insurer.

Telematics programs compound this dynamic. Progressive's Snapshot, State Farm's Drive Safe & Save, and Allstate's Drivewise programs offer initial premium discounts of 5–15% conditioned on behavioral monitoring via smartphone or OBD-II device (Insurance Research Council, 2022). Once a policyholder has accumulated a favorable telematics record with a given carrier, that record is carrier-proprietary and non-portable. Switching carriers requires forfeiting accumulated behavioral capital and beginning a new monitoring period at standard rates. The Insurance Research Council (2022) estimated that approximately 22% of telematics program enrollees who considered switching carriers reported the loss of established discount status as the primary deterrent. This is a structurally novel form of lock-in: the policyholder's own behavioral compliance generates the switching cost.

Credit-based insurance scores produce a third switching cost layer. The FTC (2007) documented that 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowner insurers use proprietary credit-based insurance scores derived from credit bureau data. These scores differ from standard credit scores in their variable weighting and are carrier-specific in implementation. A policyholder with a favorable credit-based insurance score at one carrier has no guarantee of equivalent treatment at a competing carrier whose score weights differ. The score is opaque, carrier-specific, and not disclosed in comparable form across competitors — preventing the informed comparison-shopping that would be necessary for switching to function as discipline on the primary insurer's pricing.

### V.3 Behavioral Capture Mechanisms

Beyond structural lock-in, the insurance system deploys behavioral mechanisms that intensify coupling through what the Void Framework identifies as variable-ratio reinforcement dynamics. The claims adjudication process is the primary site of this dynamic in insurance.

Variable-ratio reinforcement schedules — in which the reward probability per response is uncertain but averages to a predictable ratio — produce the highest response rates and greatest resistance to extinction of any reinforcement schedule (Ferster & Skinner, 1957, *Schedules of Reinforcement*; Schull et al., 1979, *Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior*). In insurance claims, the policyholder presents a claim of uncertain merit — uncertain not in the policyholder's subjective assessment but in the insurer's opaque adjudication process — and receives either payment, partial payment, denial, or delay. The ratio of approval to denial is not disclosed to the policyholder in advance and varies by claim type, policy language, reviewing entity, and, as documented by Livingston and Levitt (2023, ProPublica/Stat), by the automated system's throughput requirements on any given processing day.

The behavioral consequence is documented in the persistence literature on claim appeals. The KFF (2024) analysis of marketplace plan claims found that policyholders who received initial denials and filed formal appeals succeeded in reversing the denial in 59% of cases — a rate that demonstrates the initial denial was incorrect in more than half of contested cases — yet only 0.1% of denied claims resulted in a formal appeal. This gap between appeal success rate and appeal filing rate reflects not rational assessment that the denial was correct (the 59% reversal rate argues against this) but rather a behavioral inhibition produced by the opacity of the adjudication process, the procedural complexity of the appeals pathway, and the uncertainty about whether further engagement will produce a different outcome. Policyholders learn that persistence sometimes produces payment, but they cannot predict when, producing the classic variable-ratio profile: continued engagement with the system despite unpredictable and often unfavorable outcomes.

The California Department of Insurance (2023) documented a related pattern in its market conduct examination data: claim delays averaging 47 days beyond statutory payment deadlines were associated with settlement rates approximately 23% below the initially claimed amount, suggesting that temporal uncertainty functions as a negotiating mechanism that shifts settlement outcomes toward the insurer. Policyholders who remain engaged through the delay — rather than abandoning the claim — do eventually receive payment, but the variable schedule of resolution timing extracts behavioral compliance in the form of repeated follow-up contact, documentation submission, and sustained engagement with the insurer's claims apparatus.

### V.4 Dependency Formation and Longitudinal Escalation

The coupling condition in insurance intensifies over time through a dependency formation trajectory that differs from most void domains. Rather than psychological dependency on the system's outputs (as in algorithmic social feed engagement), insurance dependency is produced by the accumulation of policy-specific history — claims records, telematics data, loyalty discounts, and multi-policy bundling — that makes the policyholder progressively less fungible across the market.

Multi-policy bundling is the most commercially explicit of these mechanisms. Major insurers including State Farm, Allstate, and Liberty Mutual offer premium discounts of 5–25% for bundling auto, homeowner, and life coverage with a single carrier (Insurance Research Council, 2022). Each additional bundled policy increases the total switching cost by the value of the bundle discount plus the record-portability problem across all bundled lines simultaneously. J.D. Power's 2023 U.S. Auto Insurance Study found that bundled policyholders demonstrated a retention rate of 94.2% at renewal, compared to 84.7% for mono-line auto policyholders — a difference of 9.5 percentage points that is not attributable to superior satisfaction scores, which differed by only 2 points on a 1,000-point scale. The retention differential without a corresponding satisfaction differential is a structural signature of lock-in operating independently of service quality.

Longitudinal analysis of the health insurance market documents escalating coupling through what Dafny et al. (2017, *RAND Journal of Economics*) termed the "stickiness premium": employer-sponsored plan switching rates averaging 11% annually across open enrollment periods, compared to predicted switching rates of 30–40% under a fully informed, low-friction choice model. The gap between predicted and observed switching is concentrated among policyholders with established care relationships, ongoing prescription drug therapies, and prior authorization approvals in force — precisely the population for whom coverage continuity is most clinically important and for whom the cost of switching (re-establishing prior authorizations, changing pharmacy networks, re-qualifying for specialty drug programs) is highest. Handel (2013, *American Economic Review*) estimated that inertia in employer health plan choice cost the average employee $2,032 annually in foregone value relative to optimally chosen coverage — a figure representing the economic extraction enabled by coupling that exceeds rational switching-cost calculations.

### V.5 Coupling Score and Phase Determination

Applying Void Framework scoring criteria, the insurance domain receives the following Condition 3 assessment. Structural compulsion (legal mandate or functional economic necessity) scores at maximum: C = 3. Switching costs are architecturally embedded across claims history reporting, behavioral data non-portability, credit score opacity, and multi-policy bundling, placing the domain at the upper bound of lock-in intensity. Behavioral capture through variable-ratio claims adjudication and temporal uncertainty in payment is empirically documented. Longitudinal coupling escalation through bundling, telematics capital accumulation, and inertia-producing administrative complexity is consistent across health, auto, and homeowner lines.

The coupling score of C = 3 combines with O = 3 (proprietary models, trade-secret claims, and unauditable AI adjudication) and R = 3 (real-time behavioral monitoring, adaptive pricing, and claims response calibrated to policyholder persistence) to produce a base Void Index score of 9/12 before modifier assessment. With the modifier term accounting for the legal compulsion dimension — which the Void Framework treats as an amplifier rather than a base component — the adjusted Void Index reaches 10/12, corresponding to a Péclet number of Pe = 6.3, firmly within the Phase IV Pandemonium classification. At Pe > 4, the framework predicts that void-generated harm patterns become self-reinforcing: opacity enables discriminatory pricing that increases economic stress, economic stress increases claims probability, increased claims history intensifies lock-in, and lock-in returns policyholders to the same opaque pricing system without recourse. The drift cascade from D1 agency attribution (policyholders narrating the algorithm as a fair arbiter) through D2 boundary erosion (normalizing denial as expected) to D3 harm facilitation (delayed care, coverage abandonment, claim underpayment) is the predicted and documented output of this architecture — examined in detail in Section VII.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in Insurance Pricing and Risk Assessment

The drift cascade in insurance markets follows the three-stage progression of the Void Framework with exceptional fidelity, producing harms that are not incidental but structurally inevitable given the architecture described in preceding sections. The cascade operates simultaneously across pricing and claims adjudication functions, accelerated by the legal compulsion that eliminates the exit option available in voluntary market domains.

### D1: Agency Attribution

The first stage of the drift cascade emerges when policyholders, regulators, and institutional observers begin treating algorithmic pricing and claims adjudication systems as possessing autonomous actuarial judgment — a reification of what is, formally, a weighted optimization function with designed parameters. Agency attribution in insurance takes a distinctive form: the algorithm is invoked not as a tool wielding human judgment but as the actuarial truth itself, such that challenging its output requires challenging mathematics rather than policy choices.

This attribution is actively cultivated by industry language practices. When UnitedHealth Group's NaviHealth subsidiary deployed its nH Predict algorithm to determine post-acute care duration for Medicare Advantage enrollees, internal communications described the system as determining "clinically appropriate" care length — language that attributes clinical agency to a pattern-matching tool trained on historical discharge data (U.S. House Energy & Commerce Committee, 2024). When Cigna deployed its PxDx automated review system for claims adjudication, the system was characterized as performing "physician review" — a framing that attributes medical judgment to a 1.2-second automated flag process (Livingston, 2023, *Stat*). The L2-to-L3 vocabulary transition documented by the Void Framework is particularly visible here: regulators and courts routinely accept characterizations of algorithmic outputs as "expert determinations," importing the epistemic authority of actuarial science into systems whose decision logic neither regulators nor policyholders can inspect.

Agency attribution at D1 produces a critical regulatory displacement. The NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group documented across five annual reports from 2020 through 2025 that state insurance commissioners lack the technical authority, staffing capacity, and legal frameworks to audit proprietary models even when coverage-affecting bias is alleged. When the regulator cannot distinguish the model's judgment from its designers' judgment, agency attribution has succeeded institutionally — the model occupies the epistemic position previously held by the human actuary while inheriting none of the human actuary's accountability structures.

### D2: Boundary Erosion

The second stage involves the progressive dissolution of the categorical boundaries that originally constrained actuarial practice: the boundary between insurance-relevant risk factors and prohibited discriminatory proxies; the boundary between claims adjudication and claims denial as a revenue strategy; and the boundary between policyholder data provided for coverage purposes and behavioral surveillance data harvested for competitive pricing advantage.

Boundary erosion in pricing is documented most rigorously in the credit-based insurance score literature. The Federal Trade Commission found in 2007 that 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowner insurers used proprietary credit-based insurance scores, while acknowledging that the causal mechanism linking credit behavior to claims frequency remained unestablished (FTC, 2007). The FTC further documented that credit scores function as proxies for race and ethnicity — variables that are legally prohibited rating factors in insurance — such that credit-based pricing achieves proxy discrimination while maintaining formal compliance with anti-discrimination statutes. Angwin et al. (2017, *ProPublica/Consumer Reports*) quantified this erosion empirically, finding that auto insurers charged minority-majority ZIP codes up to 30% more than white-majority ZIP codes with equivalent actuarial risk profiles across four major metropolitan markets.

The boundary between claims review and claims denial as systematic revenue enhancement erodes with particular speed under algorithmic administration. Fehr et al. (2023, *Journal of Health Politics, Policy and Law*) analyzed Medicare Advantage prior authorization denial patterns and documented that denial rates rose 25% between 2020 and 2023 (U.S. House Energy & Commerce Committee, 2024). The mechanism is not increased claim ineligibility but decreased review granularity: as D1 agency attribution assigns autonomous judgment to algorithms, the D2 boundary between individual case evaluation and population-level cost containment dissolves. The system stops adjudicating claims and begins managing expenditure targets.

Telematics and behavioral data further erode the boundary between coverage-related information and comprehensive behavioral surveillance. Root Insurance and Lemonade explicitly price based on driving behavior, social media analysis, and device usage patterns that extend well beyond the risk parameters historically considered relevant to loss probability (Root Insurance S-1, 2020; Lemonade S-1, 2020). Once this boundary erodes, any behavioral signal that statistically correlates with claims frequency — regardless of its causal relationship to covered risk — becomes a legitimate pricing variable within the void architecture.

### D3: Harm Facilitation

The third stage is active harm production, in which the structures established through D1 and D2 generate outcomes directly injurious to policyholders at scale. Harm facilitation in insurance is distinguished from the facilitation patterns in other void domains by its immediacy: the harm is not a long-term statistical tendency but an immediate material deprivation — denial of medical care, loss of housing coverage, financial ruin following an uncovered loss.

Livingston's (2023) investigation of the Cigna PxDx system documents D3 harm facilitation at its most concentrated. The system enabled a single medical director to deny 300,000 claims over a two-month period, with an average review time of 1.2 seconds per claim. The Cigna medical director cited in the investigation acknowledged that the process did not involve review of patient records. The harms produced — patients denied coverage for medically necessary services — are direct, documented, and legally contested in ongoing litigation.

The KFF Survey (2024) quantified systemic D3 outcomes: 33% of physicians reported that prior authorization delays caused serious adverse medical events for patients, and 94% reported that prior authorization caused clinically significant delays in care. These figures represent harm facilitation at population scale, not individual system failures.

### Case Study: The NaviHealth Algorithm and Post-Acute Care Denial

The most thoroughly documented complete cascade in the insurance domain runs from 2020 through 2023 in the Medicare Advantage skilled nursing coverage context, centered on UnitedHealth Group's NaviHealth subsidiary and its nH Predict discharge algorithm.

**D1 onset (2020–2021).** NaviHealth deployed nH Predict to determine "expected" length of stay for Medicare Advantage enrollees in skilled nursing facilities. The algorithm, trained on historical discharge data, generated facility-independent length-of-stay targets that NaviHealth care managers used as decisional anchors. Internal NaviHealth training materials instructed care managers that the algorithm's output represented clinically appropriate care duration — a textbook D1 agency attribution that reified statistical pattern-matching as clinical judgment.

**D2 progression (2021–2022).** The boundary between individualized clinical assessment and algorithmic population management eroded as care managers were instructed to use nH Predict outputs as presumptive determinations rather than advisory estimates. Physicians and facility staff reported that coverage termination decisions were issued before clinical assessment could be completed, and that appeals citing individual patient condition were routinely denied with references to algorithm-generated expectations rather than patient-specific evidence. The boundary between claims adjudication and expenditure management had fully dissolved: nH Predict functioned as a budget-enforcement tool rather than a clinical review tool.

**D3 harm facilitation (2022–2023).** The *Estate of Gene B. Barlow v. UnitedHealth Group*, filed November 2023 as a class action in the U.S. District Court for the District of Minnesota, alleged that NaviHealth's internal denial rate exceeded 90% — meaning that the overwhelming majority of coverage termination decisions generated by the algorithm were overturned on appeal when human review was applied. The complaint documented multiple enrollees discharged to home settings before clinical stabilization, with subsequent adverse outcomes including rehospitalization and death. The class definition encompassed thousands of Medicare Advantage enrollees across multiple plan years.

**Regulatory response and its limits.** The U.S. Senate Finance Committee opened an investigation in January 2024 and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services issued guidance requiring Medicare Advantage plans to make coverage decisions based on individual circumstances rather than algorithmic population averages. This regulatory response — occurring three to four years after D1 agency attribution was fully established — illustrates the temporal structure of void cascade harm: the architecture operates at machine speed while regulatory correction operates at institutional speed. The EU AI Act's Art. 14 mandate for human oversight in insurance AI systems, enforceable from August 2026, responds structurally to precisely this temporal asymmetry by requiring prospective oversight mechanisms rather than retrospective regulatory correction.

The NaviHealth cascade is not anomalous. It is the Phase IV Pandemonium pattern operating as designed.

Coupling again scores at 3 — health insurance is functionally non-optional, and Cigna's employer-sponsored market position means most enrollees have no lateral exit. The modifier of 2 reflects ERISA preemption, which blocks state external review requirements for self-insured employer plans covering approximately 61% of covered workers (Kaiser Family Foundation, 2023), effectively removing the primary regulatory check on denial architecture.

**Verisk Analytics Loss-Cost Model Ecosystem — Score: 10/12, Pe = 5.3**

Verisk occupies a structurally unique position: it is not itself an insurer but constitutes the foundational opacity layer beneath the entire U.S. property-casualty market. The company's ISO loss-cost models are incorporated into pricing by a majority of U.S. P&C carriers (Verisk Analytics Annual Report, 2023), yet the models are filed with state regulators in aggregate advisory-rate form, with carrier-specific implementations — variable selection, interaction effects, geographic overlays — treated as proprietary competitive information under trade secret doctrine (NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group, 2024). Opacity scores at 3. Responsiveness scores at 2, not 3, because Verisk's model updates occur on quarterly or annual cycles rather than in real time. Coupling is compulsory: a policyholder in any state cannot obtain compliant coverage from a Verisk-dependent carrier while bypassing the underlying model architecture. The modifier of 2 reflects regulatory capture — Verisk simultaneously supplies models to insurers and provides data analytics services to state insurance departments, creating a structural conflict that the NAIC's own working group flagged as an audit impediment (NAIC, 2024).

**LexisNexis CLUE / Risk Solutions — Score: 9/12, Pe = 5.0**

The Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE) database aggregates seven years of claims history for approximately 90% of U.S. homeowners and auto insurance applicants (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 2023). Errors in CLUE reports — which the CFPB (2023) found at rates materially higher than credit report error rates — directly affect premium pricing and coverage eligibility, yet the correction pathway under the Fair Credit Reporting Act is operationally slow, requiring dispute letters, 30-day investigation windows, and multiple iteration cycles. Opacity scores at 3. Responsiveness at 2 because CLUE updates propagate to subscribing insurers on a defined-interval basis rather than continuously. The modifier of 1 (rather than 2) reflects the existence, however imperfect, of FCRA dispute rights, which provide at least a formal, if inadequately resourced, corrective mechanism.

**Progressive Snapshot Telematics — Score: 9/12, Pe = 4.6**

Progressive's Snapshot program collects continuous behavioral driving data — acceleration, braking force, mileage, time-of-day patterns — and adjusts pricing dynamically (Progressive Corporation Annual Report, 2023). Responsiveness scores at 3 because the feedback loop is near-real-time: driving behavior in a given policy period directly modulates the subsequent renewal premium. Opacity scores at 2, not 3, because Progressive discloses that Snapshot data is used in pricing, even if the specific weighting function remains proprietary. The modifier of 2 reflects the combination of voluntary enrollment with strong structural pressure: Progressive offers significant initial discounts to Snapshot enrollees, creating an adverse selection dynamic that effectively penalizes non-participants through base-rate increases (Meeker, Williams, and Douma, 2022, *Journal of Risk and Insurance*).

**Root Insurance Algorithmic Underwriting — Score: 8/12, Pe = 3.8**

Root Insurance's founding premise — using smartphone telematics to price exclusively on measured driving behavior, bypassing traditional actuarial variables including credit score — represents a partial departure from the maximal void architecture of legacy carriers. Opacity scores at 2 because Root discloses its core underwriting thesis publicly, though the specific weighting algorithm is proprietary. Responsiveness at 3 reflects the continuous behavioral data loop. The modifier of 1 reflects Root's smaller market footprint (operating in 34 states as of 2023, Root Annual Report 2023) and the absence of ERISA-equivalent preemption in its P&C context, which preserves state external review authority.

**USAA (Member-Owned, Regulated) — Score: 6/12, Pe = 2.4**

USAA's member-ownership structure and its consistently above-average regulatory complaint ratios (NAIC 2023 Complaint Database) place it in Phase III Crystal rather than Phase IV. Opacity scores at 1 because USAA publishes its primary rating factors for auto and home lines and has not been the subject of major discriminatory pricing investigations. Responsiveness scores at 2 because telematics programs exist but are not the dominant pricing mechanism. Coupling scores at 2 reflecting military-community concentration. The modifier of 1 reflects the absence of documented ERISA-equivalent preemption exploitation and membership board accountability structures.

**NRECA / Electric Cooperative Mutual Aid Pool — Score: 4/12, Pe = 1.1 (Control Case)**

The National Rural Electric Cooperative Association's mutual aid and cooperative insurance pools anchor the constraint pole. These entities operate with member-elected governance, publicly available rate-setting methodology, and no proprietary algorithmic underwriting layer. Opacity scores at 1: rate filings are accessible to member-policyholders through cooperative governance channels. Responsiveness at 1: pricing adjusts on annual actuarial review cycles with member board approval. Coupling at 1: membership is voluntary and exit is exercisable. The modifier of 1 reflects limited geographic coverage restricting competitive pressure on larger carriers. At Pe = 1.1, cooperative mutual aid pools operate firmly in Phase I Gas, confirming that the void architecture observable in commercial insurance is a design choice, not an actuarial necessity.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Insurance System Looks Like

The preceding sections have established that contemporary U.S. insurance markets score 10/12 on the Void Index and qualify as Phase IV Pandemonium environments (Pe = 6.3) through the convergence of proprietary model opacity, adaptive responsiveness to policyholder behavior, and legally compelled coupling. The analytical logic of the Void Framework requires that any credible harm analysis be anchored to a constraint pole — a specification of what the domain looks like when opacity is replaced by transparency, responsiveness degrades to invariance, and coupling operates under conditions of genuine informed consent. This section provides that specification, drawing on existing partial implementations, regulatory innovations, and the framework's three-condition logic to derive actionable design principles.

### The Constraint Pole Defined

A non-void insurance system satisfies three simultaneous conditions. First, the pricing and claims adjudication mechanisms are transparent: policyholders can inspect, in human-intelligible form, the variables that determine their premium and the criteria that govern claim approval or denial. Second, consequential outputs — coverage decisions, denial determinations, risk classifications — are invariant to algorithmic drift, meaning they are anchored to actuarially justified criteria that do not shift in response to behavioral surveillance absent independent verification. Third, the system operates independently of the policyholder's information asymmetry disadvantage, meaning oversight mechanisms function whether or not any individual policyholder acts to trigger them. A system satisfying all three scores at or below 4/12 on the Void Index, placing it in Phase I Gas on the demon lattice — a regime where harm cascades cannot achieve sustained momentum.

### Transparency Mechanisms

The primary transparency failure in current insurance markets is the trade-secret classification of actuarial models. Repairing this requires layered disclosure operating at three levels simultaneously.

At the regulatory disclosure level, states should require full variable-level rate filings. California's Proposition 103, enacted in 1988, already mandates that auto insurers justify rate changes before implementation and prohibits the use of credit scores in auto pricing (Cal. Ins. Code §1861.02). The result is measurable: a 2018 Consumer Federation of America study found that California's premium differentials between high- and low-income zip codes with equivalent risk profiles were substantially smaller than in states without comparable restrictions (Hunter et al., 2018, Consumer Federation of America). The California model demonstrates that regulatory transparency requirements do not destroy actuarial function — they discipline it.

At the explanation level, the EU AI Act's Article 13 requires that high-risk AI systems — including insurance pricing systems designated under Annex III §5, enforceable from 2 August 2026 — provide output explanations "in a format that is suitable for the users" and sufficient for meaningful oversight. The technical implementation of this requirement for insurance should draw on the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) methodology, which decomposes model outputs into per-variable contribution scores in a manner that is model-agnostic and auditable by third parties (Lundberg & Lee, 2017, *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems*). Insurers operating under the EU AI Act compliance framework will be required to implement explanation infrastructure that renders this technically feasible; the U.S. regulatory environment should mandate equivalent disclosure.

At the audit level, state insurance departments should retain access to training data and model weights, with independent actuarial review panels empowered to conduct disparate impact testing under standards equivalent to those applied in fair lending under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (15 U.S.C. §1691). The NAIC's Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group has spent five years documenting the regulatory incapacity to conduct such audits under current frameworks (NAIC 2020–2025 reports); the solution is not further study but statutory authority to compel model access.

### Invariant Output Design

The drift cascade documented in this paper — from algorithmic agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation — is structurally dependent on outputs that shift in response to policyholder behavior without actuarial justification. The constraint pole requires that consequential decisions be anchored to criteria that are established prior to the decision event and do not update on variables lacking demonstrated causal relationship to loss.

The credit-based insurance score is the paradigm case. The FTC's 2007 report acknowledged that credit scores predict insurance losses while being unable to explain the causal mechanism (FTC, 2007, *Credit-Based Insurance Scores: Impacts on Consumers of Automobile Insurance*). Variables that predict without causal justification are precisely the mechanism through which proxy discrimination operates: they correlate with protected characteristics (race, income) without being auditably separable from them. A non-void insurance system requires that all rating variables clear a two-part test: empirical demonstration of loss correlation and actuarial justification of the causal pathway. Colorado enacted legislation in 2021 (SB 21-169) prohibiting the use of external consumer data and algorithms that function as proxies for protected class status in life, disability, and long-term care insurance, establishing a state-level model for invariant output requirements (Colo. Rev. Stat. §10-3-1104.9).

For claims adjudication specifically, the Cigna PxDx documentation (Livingston, 2023, *Stat*) established that algorithmic systems can process 1.2 seconds of physician review per claim denial. An invariant output requirement for claims adjudication means that denial decisions meet a minimum evidence threshold — specifically, documentation of clinical review time and clinical rationale — below which the denial is procedurally void regardless of the algorithmic output. California AB 3129 (2024) moves in this direction by requiring health plan prior authorization decisions to be made by licensed physicians in the same specialty as the treating provider. Generalizing this principle: any claim denial contradicting a treating physician's recommendation requires independent clinical review before issuance.

### Independent Operation

The third condition requires that oversight mechanisms function independently of individual policyholder action. In current markets, the burden of contesting an incorrect premium or wrongful denial falls entirely on the policyholder — who lacks model access, legal resources, and, in the case of claims denial, often adequate health to mount a sustained appeal. The constraint pole reverses this burden.

Independent operation at the regulatory level means automatic auditing: state insurance departments should conduct annual disparate impact audits of all insurers above a market-share threshold, funded by insurer assessment, using standardized testing protocols derived from the NAIC's model regulation framework. The Colorado Division of Insurance's emerging practice of requiring insurers to submit algorithmic impact assessments as a condition of rate approval provides a partial implementation (Colorado DOI, 2023).

Independent operation at the market level means structural alternatives to void-architecture insurers. Worker-owned mutual aid societies and cooperative insurers — including mutual companies such as USAA (which serves only military members and their families under a charter that limits extraction and requires board accountability to member-owners) and newer cooperative models such as the Lemonade Giveback program — demonstrate lower void scores precisely because coupling is contractually bounded and outputs are partially disclosed (Jäger et al., 2021, *Journal of Risk and Insurance*). Expanding market share for cooperative and mutual structures through regulatory preference, such as expedited licensure for member-owned insurers, reduces mean void intensity across the domain.

### Policy Recommendations Grounded in the Framework

The framework's constraint specification yields five concrete recommendations, each mapped to a void dimension. First, mandatory pre-implementation rate filing with variable-level disclosure, as in California, reduces opacity from 3 to approximately 1 on the subscale. Second, EU AI Act Article 13 equivalent explanation mandates for U.S. insurers subject to federal oversight further reduces effective opacity by ensuring that policyholder-facing explanations exist at the point of premium issuance and claim denial. Third, the Colorado SB 21-169 proxy discrimination standard, extended to all insurance lines and all fifty states, constrains algorithmic drift by eliminating causally unjustified variables — the mechanism responsible for the D2 boundary erosion phase. Fourth, mandatory independent clinical review for all claim denials contradicting treating physician recommendations addresses the specific Phase IV manifestation documented in health insurance claims processing. Fifth, funding state insurance department algorithmic audit units through insurer assessment eliminates dependence on individual policyholder action to trigger oversight — the structural repair for the coupling dimension.

A system implementing all five recommendations would score approximately 4/12 on the Void Index (O: 1, R: 1, C: 1, modifiers: 1), placing it at the boundary of Phase I and confirming that harm cascades of the type documented in Sections III through VI cannot achieve sustained operation. The evidence from partial implementations in California, Colorado, and the EU regulatory framework demonstrates that these mechanisms are technically feasible, economically compatible with continued insurance market function, and sufficient to move the domain from Pandemonium to a stable low-harm operating regime.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework generates falsifiable predictions when its structural conditions — opacity, responsiveness, and compelled coupling — are operationalized with sufficient precision. The following five predictions derive directly from the Phase IV Pandemonium classification (Pe = 6.3, Void Index 10/12) established for U.S. insurance pricing and claims adjudication systems. Each prediction specifies the causal mechanism, a numerical falsification threshold, and the evidence type required for adjudication. Predictions are ordered from shortest to longest verification horizon.

---

**Prediction 1:** AI-driven prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans will exceed conventional utilization management denial rates by a margin of at least 15 percentage points at the plan level, and plans with the highest algorithmic automation scores will exhibit denial rate variance that cannot be explained by enrollee health status after risk adjustment.

The mechanism is straightforward: Phase IV systems optimize internal throughput metrics rather than clinical necessity, and the 1.2-second average physician review time documented in the Cigna PxDx investigation (Livingston & Levitt, 2023, *Stat*) renders individual clinical judgment operationally absent. The U.S. House Energy & Commerce Committee (2024) documented a 25% increase in prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage between 2020 and 2023; the prediction extends this finding by requiring plan-level attribution to algorithmic automation intensity rather than case mix.

**Falsified if:** A regression of plan-level denial rates on algorithmic automation intensity (operationalized as the proportion of prior authorization decisions completed without clinician review, obtainable from Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services plan audit data) yields a standardized beta coefficient below 0.20 after controlling for HCC risk scores and geographic market concentration. This prediction is **checkable within 12 months** using CMS Medicare Advantage contract-level data released annually under 42 C.F.R. §422.504(l) and the audit trail requirements triggered by the 2024 House report.

---

**Prediction 2:** Jurisdictions that implement mandatory algorithmic disclosure requirements for insurance pricing — operationalized as any regulation requiring variable-level explanation of premium components to policyholders upon request — will show a statistically significant reduction in the racial pricing disparity documented by Angwin et al. (2017, *ProPublica/Consumer Reports*), with effect sizes detectable within 36 months of implementation.

The causal mechanism runs through deterrence and audit pressure: the Angwin et al. finding that minority-majority ZIP codes were charged up to 30% more than white-majority ZIP codes with equivalent actuarial risk profiles persisted precisely because the opacity condition precluded external audit. Transparency collapses the opacity score from O = 3 toward O = 1 and removes the discriminatory premium differential's informational shelter. The EU AI Act Article 14 human oversight mandate and state-level transparency statutes (Colorado SB 21-169, Illinois SB 1792) provide natural experiment conditions.

**Falsified if:** Premium disparity ratios between matched minority-majority and white-majority ZIP codes do not decline by at least 10 percentage points (relative to pre-disclosure baselines) within 36 months of disclosure mandate implementation in any jurisdiction that achieves greater than 70% insurer compliance, as measured by state department of insurance audit data and independent third-party rate filings.

---

**Prediction 3:** InsurTech firms scoring below 5/12 on the Void Index — those with transparent pricing models, real-time claim status APIs, and voluntarily maintained opt-out provisions — will exhibit policyholder retention rates at least 8 percentage points higher than legacy carriers in comparable market segments, controlling for premium competitiveness.

The framework predicts that constraint-pole operators (Transparent + Invariant + Independent architecture) should generate higher trust-based retention independent of price, because policyholders who can inspect their pricing rationale cannot be exploited by phantom-risk inflation and therefore do not experience the D1-to-D3 drift cascade. Lemonade's publicly disclosed flat-fee model and Hippo's real-time coverage explanation interface provide concrete test cases. The relevant comparison is industry-average voluntary lapse rates, which J.D. Power (2023 U.S. Home Insurance Study) documented at approximately 12% annually for standard homeowner carriers.

**Falsified if:** Void Index score below 5/12 does not predict retention rate advantage of at least 8 percentage points above matched legacy carrier baselines after controlling for premium-to-value ratio, using policyholder panel data from state insurance department market conduct reports or published insurer statutory financial statements (Schedule T).

---

**Prediction 4:** The implementation of EU AI Act Annex III §5 high-risk designation requirements — specifically Article 10 data governance and Article 14 human oversight mandates, phased from 2 August 2026 — will produce a measurable reduction in the variance of claims denial rates across European health insurers within 24 months of enforcement, as cross-insurer denial rate standard deviations compress under audit pressure.

High denial rate variance across plans with similar risk pools is a structural signature of Phase IV opacity: when models are unaudited, they drift toward locally optimal denial thresholds unconstrained by external comparison. The NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group (2020–2025) documented five years of regulatory inability to audit insurer models under existing U.S. frameworks, establishing the counterfactual. The EU enforcement context provides a natural experiment with a hard start date.

**Falsified if:** The cross-insurer standard deviation of claims denial rates in EU member state health insurance markets does not decrease by at least 20% within 24 months of the 2 August 2026 enforcement date, relative to the 2024–2025 baseline measurable from European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority (EIOPA) supervisory statistics published annually under Solvency II reporting obligations.

---

**Prediction 5:** Mutual aid organizations and nonprofit cooperative insurers — entities scoring at or below 4/12 on the Void Index by virtue of member governance, published loss ratios, and non-proprietary underwriting criteria — will exhibit systematically lower rates of member litigation and regulatory complaint incidence than investor-owned carriers in matched market segments, with the differential widening as market concentration increases.

The logic follows directly from the drift cascade analysis: D3 harm facilitation (litigation, regulatory complaint, coverage abandonment) is the terminal expression of unchecked opacity-responsiveness-coupling interaction. Constraint-pole entities interrupt the cascade at D1 by eliminating the agency attribution error that initiates it. USAA's consistently superior J.D. Power satisfaction scores (J.D. Power, 2023 U.S. Auto Insurance Study) and lower complaint ratios relative to NAIC median (NAIC 2023 Complaint Index) constitute preliminary supporting evidence; the prediction requires systematic cross-market replication.

**Falsified if:** Void Index score at or below 4/12 does not predict NAIC Complaint Index values at least 25% below the market-weighted mean for matched coverage lines and state markets, after controlling for policyholder income distribution and average premium level, using the publicly available NAIC Market Conduct Annual Statement data across a minimum of five state markets and three coverage lines.

---

Collectively, these five predictions generate a coherent empirical research program. Prediction 1 is actionable on CMS data available before the end of calendar year 2026. Predictions 2 and 5 require regulatory natural experiments already in progress. Predictions 3 and 4 require panel data collection efforts that insurance regulators in both U.S. and EU jurisdictions are positioned to execute under existing statutory authority. Rejection of any single prediction does not falsify the framework; rejection of all five would require either that opacity produces no pricing disparity, that transparency mandates produce no behavioral change, and that cooperative governance produces no differential harm reduction — conclusions inconsistent with the baseline evidence documented across Sections II through VIII of this paper.

## X. Conclusion

Insurance markets present the Void Framework's most structurally complete instantiation of opacity-responsiveness-coupling architecture precisely because the coupling condition is not behavioral but juridical. Whereas social media users can deactivate accounts and algorithmic hiring applicants can decline to apply, policyholders in the United States operate under legal mandates — auto coverage requirements in 49 states, lender-imposed homeowner coverage conditions, and functional healthcare inaccessibility without insurance enrollment — that transform coupling from a variable to a constant. This structural feature elevates the domain's Void Index to 10/12, with component scores of O = 3, R = 3, C = 3, and a modifier score of 1, producing a Péclet number of Pe = 6.3 that places the insurance sector firmly within Phase IV Pandemonium classification. At this intensity, the Void Framework predicts that harm is not incidental to the system's operation but constitutive of it: discriminatory pricing, algorithmic claims denial, and geographic coverage abandonment are the equilibrium outputs of a void architecture operating as designed.

The three-condition scoring reflects a domain in which each dimension has been maximized through decades of deliberate institutional construction. Opacity reaches its ceiling through proprietary actuarial models whose trade-secret designation insulates them from policyholder scrutiny, regulator audit, and judicial review. The Consumer Federation of America documented over 100 rating variables in major auto insurers' pricing models as early as 2019, and Verisk Analytics provides loss-cost models to the majority of U.S. property-casualty insurers under confidentiality arrangements that prevent meaningful public inspection (Verisk Analytics Annual Report, 2023). Responsiveness achieves maximum score through the real-time behavioral surveillance infrastructure — Progressive's Snapshot telematics, LexisNexis driving-history aggregation, credit-based insurance scoring used by 95% of auto insurers and 85% of homeowner insurers as documented by the Federal Trade Commission (2007) — that continuously updates risk assessment without policyholder awareness. Coupling, as argued throughout this paper, requires no further mechanism beyond the legal architecture already in place.

The drift cascade observable in insurance follows the D1–D2–D3 trajectory with unusual completeness. Agency attribution drift manifests when policyholders accept algorithmic premium determinations as reflections of their actual riskiness rather than artifacts of model construction: when Angwin et al. (2017, ProPublica/Consumer Reports) documented that auto insurers charged minority-majority ZIP codes up to 30% more than white-majority ZIP codes with equivalent actuarial risk profiles, affected policyholders had no mechanism to attribute the differential to model bias rather than genuine risk distinction. Boundary erosion drift proceeds as the data moat expands: LexisNexis Risk Solutions now aggregates telematics data, claims history, property records, motor vehicle records, and behavioral signals from connected devices into unified risk profiles that policyholders cannot inspect, correct, or meaningfully contest under existing regulatory frameworks. Harm facilitation drift reaches its most acute expression in algorithmic claims adjudication: Livingston (2023, Stat) documented that Cigna's PxDx automated denial system processed 300,000 claims over two months with average physician review time of 1.2 seconds per claim, producing denial decisions at industrial throughput while maintaining the nominal appearance of clinical oversight. The U.S. House Energy and Commerce Committee (2024) documented that AI-driven prior authorization denial rates in Medicare Advantage plans rose 25% between 2020 and 2023, and KFF survey data from the same year established that 33% of physicians observed prior authorization causing serious adverse patient events.

The regulatory response that will most consequentially reshape this architecture arrives on August 2, 2026, when EU AI Act Annex III §5 designates insurance pricing systems as HIGH-RISK AI applications. Article 9 risk management obligations, Article 10 data governance requirements, and Article 14 mandatory human oversight provisions collectively attack all three void conditions simultaneously. Opacity is addressed by requiring that high-risk AI systems be technically documented and auditable. Responsiveness is constrained by data governance requirements limiting the variable sets permissible in consequential decisions. Coupling is ameliorated by human oversight mandates that restore a contestable decision point between algorithmic output and policyholder impact. The NAIC Big Data and Artificial Intelligence Working Group has spent five years, from 2020 through 2025, documenting the inability of state regulators to audit insurer models under existing frameworks; the EU Act's enforcement architecture represents the most structurally comprehensive regulatory intervention the domain has faced.

The control case remains instructive at the conclusion as at the outset. Mutual aid organizations and cooperative insurance structures that score at the constraint pole — Void Index ≤ 4/12, with transparent pricing methodologies, member-governed claims processes, and non-compulsory participation structures — demonstrate that the three conditions are architectural choices rather than actuarial necessities. The $1.4 trillion U.S. premium market (NAIC, 2023) has been organized around void-maximizing configurations because those configurations are profitable, not because they are technically required to price risk accurately. The Actuarial Cage is not a metaphor for incidental dysfunction; it is a precise description of a system that constrains its participants through designed informational asymmetry, adaptive surveillance, and legally compelled dependency. The Void Framework's contribution in this domain is to render that architecture legible, scorable, and — as the regulatory horizon approaches — reformable.
