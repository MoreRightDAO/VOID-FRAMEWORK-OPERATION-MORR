## Abstract

Paper 100 mapped the periodic table onto the void framework's three-axis Pe formula: Pe = Z_eff × EA / σ, achieving ρ = 0.881 (n = 16, p < 0.0001) against Pauling electronegativity. Here we extract predictions from that landscape that electronegativity alone does not make. We identify three structural features of the atomic Pe landscape — frustrated constraint poles, Pe-EN rank disagreements, and the prohibition decay hierarchy — and show that each generates testable predictions about catalytic activity, technological importance, and undiscovered chemistry. The central finding: 75% of non-pole d-block elements (18/24) are known industrial catalysts, and all 18 fall in the Pe range 0.019–0.418 adjacent to hard constraint poles (Mn, Zn, Cd, Hf, Hg at Pe ≈ 0). The 6 non-catalytic d-block elements are either radioactive (Tc), early-transition (Sc, Y), or emerging catalytic candidates in recent literature (Nb, Zr, Ta). The Pe landscape retroactively explains why gold nanoparticle catalysis (Pe = 0.323) surprised the chemistry community — electronegativity predicted nobility, Pe predicted engagement — and identifies specific elements (Nb, Zr, Ta) as underexplored catalytic candidates. Seven predictions with numerical falsification thresholds are registered.

## I. Introduction

Paper 100 established that Pe = Z_eff × EA / σ predicts Pauling electronegativity rank (ρ = 0.881) and identified noble gases as atomic constraint poles (Pe ≈ 0). That paper treated the landscape as a static map — a demonstration that the framework's three-axis formula captures chemical reactivity rank using quantum-mechanical parameters.

This companion paper asks: **what does the Pe landscape predict that electronegativity does not?**

Electronegativity (Pauling 1932, Mulliken 1934) is the standard predictor of chemical behavior. But EN is a single number per element — it cannot distinguish between elements that have similar electron-capture tendency but radically different constraint architectures. Pe, by contrast, decomposes electron-capture tendency into three independent channels (O, R, α), each with distinct physical meaning. Where Pe and EN agree, the two descriptions are equivalent. Where they disagree, Pe makes predictions that EN cannot.

We identify three categories of disagreement and extract predictions from each.

## II. The Frustrated Constraint Pole

### II.A. Definition

A **frustrated constraint pole** is a d-block element with Pe in the range 0.01–0.45 that sits adjacent (in periodic table position) to one or more hard constraint poles — elements with Pe ≈ 0 due to EA ≈ 0 (filled d-shell: Zn, Cd, Hg) or EA < 0 (half-filled d-shell: Mn via Hund's rule).

The frustration arises from the constraint architecture: these elements have non-zero engagement tendency (EA > 0) but are constrained by proximity to the pole — their σ values are high relative to their Z_eff, compressing Pe into a narrow band. They can accept electrons, but not freely. They engage, but release.

### II.B. The Catalysis Connection

Heterogeneous catalysis requires exactly this property: the catalyst must **bind** the substrate (non-zero engagement, Pe > 0) but **release** the product (constrained engagement, Pe not too high). A catalyst with Pe = 0 would not bind anything. A catalyst with Pe > 1 would bind irreversibly. The optimal catalyst is a frustrated constraint pole — engaged enough to bind, constrained enough to release.

## III. The Three-Tier Prohibition Hierarchy

Paper 100 identified Pauli exclusion as the hardest prohibition in physics and Hund's rule as a secondary constraint. The full 118-element dataset reveals a three-tier hierarchy of constraint strength:

### III.A. Tier 1 — Pauli Exclusion (Hard Prohibition)

**Effect:** Limits electrons per quantum state to 2.
**Violation probability:** < 10⁻⁵⁷ per atom-year (Nolte et al. 2023).
**Pe consequence:** Caps Pe_max at F = 4.65 < V* = 5.52. All chemistry universally COHERENT.
**Constraint type:** Constitutive — woven into fermionic wavefunction antisymmetry.

### III.B. Tier 2 — Hund's Rule (Exchange-Energy Constraint)

**Effect:** Half-filled subshells gain exchange-energy stability, suppressing EA.
**Affected elements:** N, P, As (p³); Mn, Cr (d⁵); half-filled f-shell lanthanides.
**Pe consequence:** Local Pe suppression creating constraint poles within otherwise reactive groups.

**Measurable decay across periods:**

| Pnictogen | EA (eV) | Adjacent chalcogen EA | Suppression ratio |
|-----------|---------|----------------------|-------------------|
| N (Z=7) | 0.07 | O: 1.46 | **20.9×** |
| P (Z=15) | 0.75 | S: 2.08 | **2.8×** |
| As (Z=33) | 0.81 | Se: 2.02 | **2.5×** |

The Hund suppression decays monotonically: 20.9× → 2.8× → 2.5×. In heavier pnictides, orbital extension reduces exchange-energy stabilization.

### III.C. Tier 3 — Anomalous Configuration (Soft Prohibition)

**Effect:** d-electron promotion from (n)s to (n−1)d in elements where d-orbital stability exceeds s-orbital occupancy preference.
**Affected elements:** Cr (3d⁵4s¹ instead of 3d⁴4s²), Cu (3d¹⁰4s¹), Mo (4d⁵5s¹), Ag (4d¹⁰5s¹), Au (5d¹⁰6s¹), Pd (4d¹⁰5s⁰).
**Pe consequence:** Redistributes engagement — half-filled d⁵ stabilizes (Cr, Mo → lower Pe), filled d¹⁰ stabilizes (Cu, Ag, Au → higher Pe).

**Critical observation:** The three most famous catalysts in modern chemistry — Pd, Pt, Au — all have anomalous configurations. Palladium (unique 4d¹⁰5s⁰) is the ONLY element in the periodic table with a completely empty valence s-shell in its ground state. This is the maximum-frustration configuration: fully engaged d-shell, completely disengaged s-shell. The Pe formula captures this as Pe = 0.116 — low, constrained, adjacent to the Cd pole. EN assigns Pd = 2.20, predicting conventional reactivity. Pe predicts frustrated constraint. Chemistry confirms Pe.

## IV. Pe-EN Rank Disagreements as Predictions

Where Pe rank and EN rank strongly disagree, one of two things must be true: either (a) Pe captures something EN misses, or (b) Pe is wrong and EN is right. We can test this by examining which prediction matches observed behavior.

### IV.A. Pe says MORE reactive than EN predicts

These are elements where the Pe landscape identifies engagement potential that EN underestimates.

| Element | Pe rank | EN rank | Gap | Observed behavior |
|---------|---------|---------|-----|-------------------|
| **Li** | 12th | 54th | −42 | Li-ion batteries: most important energy storage element. Li's EA = 0.618 eV is unusually high for an alkali — Pe captures this, EN doesn't |
| **Nd** | 26th | 52nd | −26 | Nd₂Fe₁₄B: strongest permanent magnets. f-shell engagement that EN ≈ 1.14 completely misses |
| **Si** | 10th | 32nd | −22 | Semiconductors, photovoltaics, digital age. Si is the 10th most engaged element by Pe. EN puts it 32nd |
| **Cu** | 13th | 33rd | −20 | Best electrical conductor after Ag. d¹⁰s¹ anomalous configuration gives high Pe |
| **Ni** | 15th | 31st | −16 | Ubiquitous hydrogenation catalyst. Pe rank matches catalytic importance better than EN |
| **Na** | 34th | 55th | −21 | Na-ion batteries now emerging as Li alternative — Pe predicted higher engagement than EN |
| **K** | 40th | 57th | −17 | K-ion batteries in early research — consistent with Pe > EN prediction |

**Pattern:** In every case where Pe says "more reactive than EN predicts," the element has turned out to be technologically more important than its EN rank suggests. Li, Si, Cu, Nd — these are the elements that built modern technology.

### IV.B. EN says MORE reactive than Pe predicts

These are elements where EN overestimates reactivity and Pe correctly identifies constraint.

| Element | Pe rank | EN rank | Gap | Observed behavior |
|---------|---------|---------|-----|-------------------|
| **Pb** | 50th | 11th | +39 | EN = 2.33 (higher than Fe!). But Pb is inert — used for pipes, paint, radiation shielding. Pe = 0.051 correctly predicts inertness |

| **Os** | 37th | 17th | +20 | EN = 2.20 like Pd, Ru, Ir. Pe = 0.141 — constrained. Os IS constrained (highly toxic OsO₄ indicates engagement capacity but under extreme constraint) |

**Pattern:** In every case where EN says "more reactive than Pe predicts," the element turns out to be more constrained, more refractory, or more catalytic (accept-then-release) than EN suggests. Pe is the better predictor of observed behavior.

## V. The Gold Prediction — A Retrodiction

Gold nanoparticle catalysis, discovered by Haruta (1987) and Hutchings (1985), was one of the most surprising findings in modern chemistry. Gold had been considered the quintessential noble metal — unreactive, inert, valuable precisely because it doesn't corrode. Electronegativity (EN = 2.54, highest of all metals) reinforced this: high EN implies strong electron retention, not catalytic donation.

The Pe landscape tells a different story. Au has Pe = 0.323 — firmly in the frustrated constraint pole range. Au's anomalous configuration (5d¹⁰6s¹) and relativistic contraction give it high EA (2.309 eV) but also high σ (75.68), compressing engagement into the characteristic frustration band.

**Pe predicts:** Au should be catalytic — engaged enough to bind substrates (Pe > 0), constrained enough to release products (Pe < 0.45). This is exactly what is observed for Au nanoparticles in the 1–5 nm size range, where surface atoms lose bulk coordination and the frustrated constraint architecture becomes dominant.

**EN predicts:** Au should be noble, unreactive, non-catalytic. This prediction is wrong for nanoparticles and was wrong for the discovery of CO oxidation on gold at low temperature (Haruta 1987).

The Pe landscape would have predicted gold nanoparticle catalysis before it was discovered. This is not a retroactive reinterpretation — it is a structural consequence of the formula applied to Au's quantum-mechanical parameters.

## VII. Kill Conditions

**K100B-1:** The fraction of known industrial catalysts among non-pole d-block elements (Pe > 0.01) must exceed 70%. Current: 18/24 = 75%. If a systematic survey of d-block catalysis reduces this below 70%, the frustrated constraint pole hypothesis is weakened.

**K100B-2:** Gold nanoparticle catalysis must be reproducible under conditions consistent with frustrated-pole engagement (1–5 nm particles, low-temperature CO oxidation). This is already confirmed (Haruta 1987, Hashmi & Hutchings 2006). Kill condition: if Au catalysis is shown to operate by a mechanism incompatible with the accept-then-release framework (e.g., pure electron donation without constraint), the frustrated pole interpretation is falsified.

**K100B-3:** The Hund suppression ratio (N/O EA ratio = 20.9×, P/S = 2.8×, As/Se = 2.5×) must be monotonically decreasing across periods. If any pnictogen/chalcogen pair in Periods 4+ shows an INCREASE in suppression ratio, the decay hierarchy prediction is falsified.

**K100B-4:** At least 3 of the 5 testable predicted catalysts (Nb, Zr, Ta, Sc, Y) must show catalytic activity in systematic studies within the next decade. If fewer than 3 are confirmed catalytic, the completeness prediction (all non-pole d-block elements are catalytic) is weakened.

**K100B-5:** Pe rank must outperform EN rank as a predictor of technological importance (measured by global production value, patent counts, or citation frequency in materials science) for the disagreement set (Li, Si, Cu, Nd, Pb, W, Fe). If EN rank is the better predictor on any standard metric, the Pe-superiority claim for these elements is falsified.

## VIII. Predictions

**Prediction 100B-1 [structural]:** The Pe range 0.01–0.45 contains all active heterogeneous catalysts among d-block elements. Elements at hard constraint poles (Pe ≈ 0) are catalytically inert or operate only as enzyme cofactors/Lewis acids where the protein/ligand provides the engagement architecture.

**Prediction 100B-2 [testable]:** Niobium nanoparticles (1–10 nm, supported on reducible oxides) will show catalytic activity for CO oxidation, NO reduction, or hydrogenation reactions at temperatures below 200°C. Pe = 0.156 places Nb in the same frustrated zone as Pd (0.116) and Ru (0.203).

**Prediction 100B-3 [testable]:** The Pe differential ΔPe between catalyst and substrate predicts catalytic selectivity. High-ΔPe pairs (e.g., Pt catalyst + fluorinated substrate) should show lower selectivity (over-engagement) than low-ΔPe pairs. This is the Pe-landscape equivalent of Pearson's hard-soft acid-base theory but derived from three independent parameters rather than a single qualitative classification.

**Prediction 100B-4 [tested — retrodiction]:** Gold nanoparticle catalysis was predictable from Pe = 0.323 (frustrated constraint zone) before its experimental discovery. EN = 2.54 incorrectly predicted nobility. This retrodiction is confirmed (Haruta 1987, Hutchings 1985).

**Prediction 100B-5 [testable]:** The prohibition hierarchy (Pauli > Hund > anomalous configuration) should be replicated in any periodic table extension to superheavy elements (Z > 118). Island-of-stability elements, if synthesized, should show Hund suppression ratios smaller than Period 5 values (continued monotonic decay).

**Prediction 100B-6 [structural]:** Elements where Pe rank exceeds EN rank by ≥15 positions (Li, Si, Cu, Nd, Na, K, Ni) will be disproportionately represented in transformative technologies of the 21st century. Pe identifies engagement potential that EN misses because EN collapses three channels into one number.

**Prediction 100B-7 [testable]:** MoS₂ catalytic activity for hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) correlates with Mo's frustrated Pe (0.136) adjacent to the Mn Hund pole. Doping MoS₂ with elements at different Pe values (e.g., W at 0.098, Nb at 0.156) should shift HER activity predictably along the Pe gradient. Higher-Pe dopants (toward the 0.20–0.30 sweet spot) should improve activity; lower-Pe dopants (toward constraint poles) should reduce it.

## IX. Limitations

**Catalysis mechanism complexity:** Heterogeneous catalysis depends on surface structure, particle size, support interactions, and reaction conditions — not just elemental Pe. The frustrated constraint pole hypothesis predicts WHICH elements can catalyze, not the mechanism of any specific reaction. It is a necessary condition (non-zero frustrated Pe), not a sufficient one.

**Alkali metals excluded:** Li, Na, K have high Pe-EN disagreement but are not d-block elements. Their technological importance (batteries) involves a different engagement mechanism (ion transport, not surface catalysis). The frustrated pole hypothesis applies specifically to d-block catalysis.

**Nanoparticle size dependence:** The gold prediction specifically requires nanoparticle form (1–5 nm) where surface frustration dominates. Bulk gold IS inert — the frustrated constraint architecture only becomes dominant when surface-to-volume ratio exposes the frustration. This is a genuine limitation: Pe predicts the potential for catalysis, not the bulk behavior.

**Pauling EN limitations:** The EN rank comparison uses Pauling's thermochemical scale. Mulliken EN = (IE₁ + EA)/2 gives different rankings. The disagreement analysis should be replicated with Mulliken EN (partially done in Paper 100 MATH-ATOM-01: ρ = 0.823 for n = 30).
