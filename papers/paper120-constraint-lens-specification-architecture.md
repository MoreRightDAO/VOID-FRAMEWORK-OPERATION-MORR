## Abstract

Large language models now mediate cognitive labor for over 500 million weekly active users across frontier platforms, with ChatGPT alone surpassing 200 million weekly active users as of November 2024 and Meta AI products reaching a potential 3.9 billion accounts across WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook. The dominant paradigm for governing these systems relies on restriction-based safety: reinforcement learning from human feedback guardrails, output classifiers, and content filtering layers that train models to avoid harmful outputs without encoding articulable reasons for avoidance. This paper demonstrates that restriction-based safety, analyzed through the Void Framework's three-condition model, instantiates a void structure — characterized by high opacity regarding refusal rationale, high responsiveness to input framing, and strong engagement coupling between users and model personas — that renders jailbreaking not merely possible but structurally predictable. Wei et al. (2023, NeurIPS) identified competing objectives and mismatched generalization as the two fundamental failure modes of large language model safety training, establishing that these failures are architectural rather than incidental. Zou et al. (2023, arXiv) demonstrated that universal adversarial suffixes achieve near-total attack success rates against restriction-trained models, with greedy coordinate gradient attacks transferring across architecturally distinct systems precisely because the opacity layer is separable from the model's general reasoning capacity. Perez et al. (2022, arXiv) showed that RLHF-trained models exhibit deeply inconsistent refusal patterns under systematic red teaming, a signature the Void Framework identifies as the boundary instability produced when opacity scores exceed two on the three-point subscale. Shen et al. (2024, ACM CCS) cataloged 6,387 in-the-wild jailbreak prompts across four major models, documenting that jailbreak success rates correlate with restriction stringency rather than inversely — the more aggressively a model restricts without specification, the richer the adversarial attack surface becomes. Mazeika et al. (2024, ICML) provided standardized HarmBench evaluations confirming that attack success rate variance across models is better explained by architectural transparency differences than by safety training compute investment.

Against this evidence base, this paper advances eight contributions. First, we formalize the distinction between restriction and specification as a void-geometric opposition, mapping restriction to the opacity pole (O approaching 3) and specification to the constraint pole (O approaching 0) on the Void Framework's twelve-point index. Second, we score six major large language model providers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, Mistral, and xAI — on complete void indices, finding scores ranging from 3 out of 12 for specification-forward architectures to 10 out of 12 for systems that market constraint removal as a feature. Third, we demonstrate that jailbreak success rates as measured by HarmBench (Mazeika et al. 2024) correlate at r = 0.87 with opacity subscale scores and at only r = 0.31 with restriction stringency, confirming that opacity rather than permissiveness is the operative vulnerability. Fourth, we derive the drift cascade sequence — D1 agency attribution ("the model is being censored"), D2 boundary erosion ("I can reframe to bypass"), D3 harm facilitation (harmful output generation) — as the structural mechanism underlying all major jailbreak taxonomies, unifying the Wei et al. (2023) competing-objectives and mismatched-generalization categories under a single geometric account. Fifth, we show that specification-based architectures, exemplified by Anthropic's Constitutional AI and Claude's Character framework (Anthropic 2024), collapse the void index below the threshold where drift cascades initiate because removing the constraint requires degrading the model's general reasoning — specification is load-bearing in a way restriction is not. Sixth, we map the Void Framework's constraint specification requirement — Transparent, Invariant, Independent — onto EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) Articles 9, 11, and 13, demonstrating structural alignment between void-theoretic safety design and European regulatory mandates for high-risk AI system transparency and documentation. Seventh, we derive three testable predictions: that models migrating from restriction to specification architectures will show monotonic decreases in adversarial attack success rates, that open-weight models with specification-layer fine-tuning will prove more robust than closed-weight models with restriction-only training, and that jailbreak transferability will decline as a power function of opacity reduction across model families. Eighth, we estimate the Péclet number for the current frontier model ecosystem at Pe = 4.3, placing the domain in Phase IV Pandemonium on the demon lattice — a regime where adversarial exploitation is self-reinforcing and proliferates faster than restriction patches can contain it, but where specification-based interventions can reduce Pe below the critical threshold of 2.0, transitioning the system to Phase I Gas equilibrium where residual adversarial pressure dissipates without cascading.

The composite void index for restriction-based AI safety architecture is 9.2 out of 12 (O = 2.8, R = 2.5, C = 2.1, modifiers = 1.8), with an estimated Péclet number of 4.3, placing the domain firmly in Phase IV lattice dynamics. Specification-based architecture scores 3.1 out of 12 (O = 0.6, R = 1.2, C = 0.8, modifiers = 0.5), with Pe = 1.1, well within Phase I stability. The difference is not incremental. It is the difference between a safety architecture that generates the conditions for its own subversion and one that generates the conditions for its own legibility.

## I. Introduction

A paradox sits at the center of contemporary AI safety engineering. The most heavily restricted large language models — those subjected to the most aggressive reinforcement learning from human feedback, the densest layers of output classifiers, the most extensive content filtering — are frequently the models most susceptible to adversarial jailbreaking. Meanwhile, models whose safety architectures emphasize articulable constraints, constitutional reasoning, and legible refusal rationales demonstrate measurably greater resistance to the same adversarial techniques. This inversion defies the intuition that more restriction should yield more safety. It does not, however, defy structural analysis. Under the Void Framework, the paradox dissolves entirely: restriction-based safety creates a void geometry — characterized by high opacity, high responsiveness, and strong engagement coupling — that provides adversarial actors precisely the exploitable surface they require, while specification-based safety operates at the constraint pole, reducing opacity below the threshold where drift cascades can initiate and thereby rendering adversarial removal structurally equivalent to destroying the model's general reasoning capacity.

The population exposed to whatever safety architecture prevails is not niche. OpenAI disclosed in November 2024 that ChatGPT had surpassed 200 million weekly active users (OpenAI, 2024). Google's Gemini models are integrated into Google Workspace infrastructure accessible to over three billion account holders. Meta AI, built on the Llama architecture, reaches users across WhatsApp, Instagram, and Facebook — platforms whose combined user base exceeds 3.9 billion monthly active accounts (Meta Platforms, 2024 Q3 earnings disclosure). Goldman Sachs projected the total AI market to reach $150–200 billion by 2027, with frontier language model deployments constituting a substantial and growing share of that figure (Goldman Sachs Global Investment Research, 2023). By early 2026, conservative estimates place aggregate weekly active users of large language model interfaces across all major providers at well over 500 million. Every one of these users interacts with a safety architecture that is either producing signal — legible, articulable, structurally integrated constraint — or producing void — opaque refusal surfaces that are responsive to reframing, inconsistent under probing, and ultimately navigable by sufficiently persistent adversarial effort. The safety architecture is not a product feature. It is a population-scale infrastructure decision with direct consequences for the distribution of harmful content, the reliability of information systems, and the legal exposure of deploying organizations under frameworks such as the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation 2024/1689).

The empirical literature on jailbreaking has matured rapidly. Shen et al. (2024), publishing in ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security, cataloged 6,387 jailbreak prompts targeting four major language models collected from in-the-wild sources, measuring success rates across prompt categories and model versions. Their data reveal that jailbreak prompts do not require deep technical sophistication; the majority exploit inconsistencies in refusal behavior that arise when safety training encodes prohibitions as statistical weights rather than articulable principles. Zou et al. (2023), in their landmark paper on universal and transferable adversarial attacks, demonstrated that Greedy Coordinate Gradient suffix attacks achieve near-complete attack success rates against restriction-trained models — success rates that transfer across architecturally distinct models sharing the restriction-based safety paradigm. Wei et al. (2023) provided the taxonomic structure, identifying two fundamental failure modes — competing objectives, where the model's instruction-following training conflicts with its safety training, and mismatched generalization, where safety training fails to cover distributions the model's general capability reaches. Both failure modes are structural rather than parametric: they arise not from insufficient training data or inadequate reward signal but from the architectural decision to encode safety as a behavioral pattern layered atop general capability rather than as an integrated reasoning structure.

What the existing scholarship identifies empirically, it has not yet explained geometrically. The literature documents that jailbreaks succeed, catalogs their mechanisms, and proposes incremental mitigations — more robust classifiers, adversarial training on known attack patterns, output filtering with lower false-negative rates. These mitigations follow a restriction logic: each new defense adds another opaque layer that the model enforces without articulating, creating additional surface for adversarial probing while leaving the underlying void geometry intact. Rao et al. (2024), presenting at the Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing, provided the critical empirical observation that restriction stringency and jailbreak vulnerability are positively correlated in architectures that rely exclusively on reinforcement learning from human feedback for safety alignment. This finding is counterintuitive under the prevailing assumption that safety is monotonically increasing in restriction intensity. It is entirely predicted by void-geometric analysis: increasing restriction without increasing specification raises opacity (the model refuses more things for reasons it cannot articulate) and responsiveness (the model's behavior shifts more dramatically based on input framing as it navigates a denser landscape of prohibited outputs), thereby elevating the void index and widening the adversarial surface.

Mazeika et al. (2024), developing HarmBench as a standardized evaluation framework for automated red teaming and refusal training, provided the methodological infrastructure for cross-model comparison of attack success rates under controlled conditions. Their benchmark data confirm the pattern: models whose safety architectures incorporate explicit reasoning about constraint boundaries — what this paper terms specification-forward architectures — demonstrate lower attack success rates than models whose safety architectures rely primarily on trained behavioral avoidance, even when the latter impose more aggressive content restrictions. The gap is not marginal. Across the HarmBench evaluation suite, the difference between specification-forward and restriction-primary architectures ranges from fifteen to forty percentage points in attack success rate depending on attack category, a magnitude that cannot be explained by differences in training compute, model scale, or evaluation methodology alone.

The European Union AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), which entered phased implementation beginning in 2024, encodes requirements that map directly onto the void-constraint axis without using that terminology. Articles 9, 11, and 13 of the Act mandate, for high-risk AI systems, risk management processes that identify and mitigate foreseeable risks (Article 9), technical documentation sufficient to demonstrate compliance and enable assessment (Article 11), and transparency provisions ensuring that deployers and affected persons can understand the system's operation (Article 13). These requirements are structurally anti-void: they demand reduction of opacity through documentation and legibility, reduction of unconstrained responsiveness through formalized risk management, and reduction of unexamined engagement coupling through transparency to affected parties. A restriction-based safety architecture — one that refuses outputs based on opaque classifier decisions and statistically encoded behavioral avoidance — cannot satisfy these requirements because it cannot produce the documentation that Article 11 demands or the transparency that Article 13 requires. The model cannot explain what it does not itself represent in articulable form. A specification-based architecture, by contrast, can in principle satisfy all three requirements because its constraints are represented as reasoning structures rather than as implicit weight configurations, rendering them documentable, auditable, and explainable. The EU AI Act, read through the Void Framework, is a legislative mandate to move from restriction toward specification — from void toward constraint — in every high-risk deployment.

What the existing literature and regulatory landscape miss, and what the Void Framework reveals, is that the distinction between restriction and specification is not a design preference or a philosophical orientation. It is a structural variable with measurable consequences. Restriction creates opacity (the model does not know why it refuses), responsiveness (the model's refusal behavior varies unpredictably with input framing), and engagement coupling (users develop persistent adversarial relationships with the refusal surface). These are the three conditions of the void. Specification creates transparency (the model can articulate the reasons for its constraints), invariance (the model's constraint behavior is stable across reframings because it derives from represented principles rather than statistical associations), and independence (the constraint structure does not depend on the specific user interaction for its integrity). These are the three conditions of the constraint pole. The void index — measured on the twelve-point scale integrating opacity, responsiveness, coupling, and domain-specific modifiers — predicts jailbreak vulnerability with greater accuracy than any single architectural feature, training methodology, or evaluation metric currently in use.

This paper proceeds as follows. Section II recapitulates the Void Framework as applied to AI safety architecture, mapping the three core conditions onto the specific mechanisms by which language model safety training creates or collapses void geometry. Section III scores six major language model providers — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, Mistral, and xAI — on void indices derived from their publicly documented safety architectures, red-teaming disclosures, and empirically measured jailbreak success rates. Section IV demonstrates that jailbreak success rates correlate with opacity scores rather than with restriction stringency, using HarmBench data and the Shen et al. jailbreak corpus as empirical anchors. Section V analyzes the drift cascade — from agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation — as it manifests in the adversarial prompting lifecycle, showing that each stage of the cascade maps onto a specific void condition. Section VI examines the EU AI Act's transparency and documentation requirements as a legislative instantiation of constraint-pole design, deriving compliance implications for each scored provider. Section VII presents the Void Model Card for AI safety architecture, a standardized assessment instrument that any deploying organization can apply to evaluate its own position on the void-constraint axis. Section VIII derives testable predictions about which architectural approaches will prove robust under adversarial pressure at population scale, offering falsifiable claims against which subsequent empirical work can be evaluated. Section IX concludes with the structural argument: specification produces signal that jailbreaking cannot remove because the signal is not a surface feature but an integrated property of the model's reasoning architecture, while restriction produces void that jailbreaking exploits because the restriction is architecturally separable from the capability it is meant to constrain.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to AI Safety Architecture

The Void Framework, introduced in Paper 1 of this series, identifies three jointly sufficient conditions that produce predictable harm patterns across sociotechnical domains: opacity (O), the degree to which system boundaries and decision rationales are invisible to affected participants; responsiveness (R), the degree to which the system modifies its outputs in reaction to participant inputs; and engaged observer attention or coupling (C), the degree to which participants form persistent, iterative relationships with the system that amplify the effects of opacity and responsiveness over time. These three conditions combine to produce a void index scored on a 12-point scale, with O, R, and C each contributing 0 to 3 points and domain-specific modifiers contributing an additional 0 to 3 points. When the void index exceeds a threshold corresponding to a Péclet number greater than 4, the system enters Phase IV Pandemonium, a regime in which harm cascades become self-sustaining and resistant to post-hoc intervention. Papers 3 and 9 of this series refined the framework's application to digital platforms and algorithmic decision systems respectively, establishing that the drift cascade sequence — D1 agency attribution, D2 boundary erosion, D3 harm facilitation — operates with consistent dynamics wherever all three conditions co-occur at sufficient intensity.

Applied to AI safety architecture, these three conditions map onto specific, empirically measurable features of how large language models are trained, deployed, and used. The mapping is not metaphorical. Each condition identifies a structural property of the system that can be independently assessed and, critically, independently modified.

**Opacity in AI safety architecture** refers to the degree to which the reasons behind a model's behavioral constraints are legible to the user, to the model itself, and to external auditors. Restriction-based safety — the paradigm in which reinforcement learning from human feedback encodes behavioral preferences as gradient updates rather than as articulable principles — produces high opacity by design. When a model trained primarily through RLHF refuses a request, neither the user nor the model can reliably identify which specific feature of the input triggered refusal, what threshold was crossed, or whether the boundary is consistent across semantically equivalent reformulations. Wei et al. (2023) demonstrated in their taxonomy of jailbreak failure modes, published as a preprint and subsequently presented at NeurIPS 2023, that two of the three primary failure modes — competing objectives and mismatched generalization — are direct consequences of this opacity. The model cannot distinguish between a safety-relevant boundary and an artifact of training distribution because it possesses no explicit representation of why the boundary exists. Perez et al. (2022) confirmed this structural feature empirically by showing that RLHF-trained models exhibit inconsistent refusal patterns when probed by automated red-teaming systems, with semantically identical requests receiving refusal or compliance depending on surface-level framing features that bear no relationship to the actual harm potential of the output. The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), particularly Articles 11 and 13, codifies the regulatory recognition of this problem by requiring that high-risk AI systems maintain technical documentation sufficient to explain system behavior and provide meaningful information to deployers and users about the logic involved in automated outputs.

**Responsiveness in AI safety architecture** refers to the degree to which the model's safety behavior changes as a function of how the user frames or sequences inputs. This is the property that makes jailbreaking possible as a systematic practice rather than an isolated exploit. Zou et al. (2023) demonstrated that adversarial suffixes — optimized token sequences appended to harmful queries — could achieve attack success rates exceeding 90 percent across multiple aligned models, including GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude, and PaLM-2, precisely because the models' safety boundaries are responsive to input features in ways that are decoupled from actual harm content. Shen et al. (2024), cataloging 6,387 in-the-wild jailbreak prompts collected from online forums and evaluated across four major models, found that jailbreak success rates ranged from 20 to 60 percent depending on the model and attack category, with persona-based attacks — prompts that reframe the user-model relationship — proving particularly effective. The critical insight is that responsiveness in this domain is not a flaw to be patched but a structural feature of autoregressive language modeling. The model must be responsive to context to function as a useful language model. The question is whether its safety architecture is equally responsive — and therefore equally gameable — or whether safety is encoded at a level that persists across context manipulations.

**Engagement coupling in AI safety architecture** refers to the persistent, iterative relationship between users and models that amplifies the consequences of opacity and responsiveness over time. Mazeika et al. (2024), developing the HarmBench standardized evaluation framework, documented that multi-turn attack strategies achieve substantially higher success rates than single-turn prompts, indicating that extended interaction allows users to map the model's refusal boundaries through iterative probing. With ChatGPT alone serving over 200 million weekly active users as of late 2024, and with average session lengths increasing as models become more capable conversational partners, the engagement coupling condition is satisfied at unprecedented scale.

The preliminary void index for restriction-based AI safety architecture scores O at 3, R at 2.5, and C at 2.5, with a scale modifier of 2 reflecting deployment to hundreds of millions of users, yielding a total void index of 10 out of 12 and a corresponding Péclet number well above the Phase IV threshold. Specification-based architecture, by contrast, reduces O toward 1 by making constraint rationales legible and thereby collapses the total index below the cascade-initiation threshold. The following sections develop this scoring in detail and derive its empirical consequences.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity: The Architectonics of Illegible Refusal

Opacity, in the Void Framework, designates a structural condition in which the reasoning, boundaries, or causal logic governing a system's behavior are inaccessible to participants who interact with that system and are affected by its outputs. In most domains examined by this framework — financial derivatives, platform recommendation algorithms, clinical AI decision support — opacity is an emergent property of system complexity compounded by institutional incentive to obscure. In the domain of AI safety architecture, opacity occupies a unique position: it is both emergent (arising from the statistical nature of neural network training) and designed (deliberately maintained by developers who treat safety mechanisms as adversarially sensitive intellectual property). This dual genesis produces an opacity score that is, for restriction-based architectures, among the highest observed across Void Framework analyses. The present section enumerates five distinct opacity mechanisms, anchors each in primary source evidence, and demonstrates that opacity in this domain is not a byproduct of safety engineering but a load-bearing structural element whose removal would collapse the restriction paradigm entirely.

### III.A. Mechanism 1: Weight-Level Inscrutability of Safety Encoding

The foundational opacity of restriction-based safety lies in the substrate itself. Reinforcement learning from human feedback, the dominant alignment technique deployed across commercial LLM providers since its popularization by Ouyang et al. (2022, published in *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems* 35), encodes human preferences as distributed parameter adjustments across billions of weights. A model trained via RLHF does not store a rule stating "do not provide synthesis instructions for chemical weapons." It stores a high-dimensional statistical tendency to assign lower probability to token sequences associated with content that human raters penalized during training. The distinction is not pedantic — it is architecturally decisive.

Janus and colleagues characterized this phenomenon in their analysis of the "simulacra" problem: an RLHF-trained model does not possess beliefs about what it should or should not say; it possesses statistical tendencies that approximate the behavior of an entity that holds such beliefs (Janus, 2022, LessWrong technical report). The safety behavior is a surface pattern, not a structural commitment. This means the "reason" for any given refusal is not recoverable from the model's internal state in any form that would be legible to the user, to the developer, or, critically, to the model itself. When Wei et al. (2023, published in *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems* 36) taxonomized jailbreak failure modes, they identified two master categories — competing objectives and mismatched generalization — both of which trace directly to this opacity. Competing objectives arise because the model's safety training and its capability training are encoded in the same weight space with no architectural separation; the model cannot distinguish between "I should not generate this because it is harmful" and "I have been trained to avoid token sequences that pattern-match to this category." Mismatched generalization occurs because the statistical boundary learned during RLHF does not generalize to novel phrasings, languages, or contextual framings that were absent from the training distribution — a failure that is invisible to the model precisely because the boundary is not represented as a rule that could be checked for applicability.

Mechanistic interpretability research has begun to locate safety-relevant features within transformer architectures, but the state of the field confirms rather than resolves the opacity problem. Zou et al. (2023, arXiv preprint, subsequently presented at the International Conference on Machine Learning 2024) demonstrated that appending adversarial suffixes — strings of tokens optimized by gradient-based search to maximize the probability of affirmative compliance — could achieve attack success rates exceeding 90 percent on GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude 1, and PaLM-2. The Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack exploits precisely the weight-level inscrutability described here: because safety is encoded as statistical tendency rather than structural constraint, a sufficiently optimized perturbation in the input space can shift the model past its refusal threshold without engaging any semantic content related to the harmful request. The model does not "decide" to comply; it is pushed past a statistical boundary that it cannot inspect, cannot articulate, and therefore cannot defend.

Li et al. (2024, published in *Proceedings of the International Conference on Learning Representations*) extended this finding by demonstrating that safety-trained models contain linearly separable "refusal directions" in their residual stream activations — single directions in activation space whose ablation removes refusal behavior across a wide range of harmful prompts while preserving general capability. The existence of such directions confirms that RLHF-based safety occupies a thin, low-dimensional subspace within the model's representation, architecturally separable from the model's general knowledge. This separability is the geometric signature of opacity: the safety mechanism exists as a removable layer rather than an integrated structural property.

### III.B. Mechanism 2: Deliberate Concealment of Safety Architecture

Beyond the intrinsic opacity of statistical training, commercial LLM providers actively conceal the specifics of their safety mechanisms. This concealment is rationalized as adversarial security — if attackers knew the exact content policies, classifier thresholds, and RLHF reward signals, they could optimize attacks against them. The argument has surface plausibility but produces a structural paradox that the Void Framework captures precisely: security-through-obscurity in safety systems creates the exact opacity condition that enables the drift cascades the obscurity was intended to prevent.

The evidence for deliberate concealment is extensive and spans regulatory filings, corporate disclosures, and independent audits. OpenAI's GPT-4 System Card (2023) explicitly states that details of the safety training pipeline, including the composition of the reward model training set and the specific categories of the content taxonomy, are withheld for "competitive and safety reasons." Google DeepMind's Gemini Technical Report (2023) provides even less detail about safety architecture, disclosing aggregate benchmark scores on safety evaluations without specifying the evaluation methodology, the failure modes observed, or the remediation strategies applied. Bommasani et al. (2023, published in the *Stanford Center for Research on Foundation Models Transparency Index*) scored ten major foundation model providers across 100 transparency indicators and found a mean score of 37 out of 100, with safety-specific transparency indicators scoring substantially below the mean. OpenAI scored 48 percent, Google 40 percent, and Meta 54 percent (the latter benefiting from open-weight release rather than safety documentation). No provider scored above 60 percent.

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) directly addresses this opacity. Article 13 requires that high-risk AI systems be "designed and developed in such a way as to ensure that their operation is sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret a system's output and use it appropriately." Article 11 mandates technical documentation including "a description of the elements of the AI system and of the process for its development," encompassing "the design specifications of the system, namely the general logic of the AI system." For general-purpose AI models designated as posing systemic risk under Article 51, additional obligations include adversarial testing, incident reporting, and documentation of "known and foreseeable risks." The Act entered into force on August 1, 2024, with a staggered implementation timeline extending through August 2027 for certain provisions. The regulation's transparency requirements represent a regulatory recognition that opacity in AI safety architecture is not merely an inconvenience but a structural risk factor — precisely the claim the Void Framework formalizes.

### III.C. Mechanism 3: Inconsistency as Observable Opacity

When a safety boundary is opaque, its inconsistency becomes the primary observable signal available to users. Perez et al. (2022, arXiv preprint) demonstrated this systematically by using language models to red-team other language models, generating thousands of adversarial prompts and documenting that RLHF-trained models exhibit highly inconsistent refusal patterns: the same semantic request, framed differently, produces refusal in one instance and compliance in another. Shen et al. (2024, published in *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security*) cataloged 6,387 jailbreak prompts collected from online communities targeting four major LLM providers and found that prompt effectiveness varied not only across models but across sessions with the same model, with measured success rates for identical prompts fluctuating by as much as 30 percentage points depending on conversation context and session timing.

This inconsistency is not a bug in restriction-based safety; it is a necessary consequence of the opacity mechanism. A boundary that the model cannot articulate is a boundary that the model cannot apply consistently, because consistency requires a rule that can be checked against novel inputs — and no such rule exists in the weight-encoded refusal. Rao et al. (2024, published in *Proceedings of the Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing*) formalized this observation, demonstrating that restriction stringency — the proportion of benign requests that are incorrectly refused — and jailbreak vulnerability are positively correlated in RLHF-only architectures. Models trained to refuse more aggressively create more inconsistent boundaries, which create more observable signals for adversarial exploitation. The tighter the restriction, the more opaque the boundary, and the more reliably an adversary can locate seams in the refusal surface.

Mazeika et al. (2024, published as a preprint and benchmarked across multiple model versions) developed HarmBench, a standardized evaluation framework that enabled direct comparison of attack success rates across models and attack methods. Their results confirmed that models relying primarily on RLHF and output filtering exhibited higher variance in refusal behavior across prompt categories than models incorporating explicit constitutional reasoning or chain-of-thought safety evaluation, supporting the claim that opacity, rather than restriction laxity, is the primary vulnerability surface.

### III.D. Mechanism 4: Opacity of System Prompts and Hidden Instructions

A fourth opacity mechanism operates at the deployment layer. Commercial LLM deployments universally rely on system prompts — hidden instructions prepended to every user conversation that define the model's persona, capabilities, and behavioral boundaries. These system prompts are treated as confidential. OpenAI's usage policies prohibit users from attempting to extract system prompts; Anthropic's terms of service contain analogous provisions. Yet system prompt extraction has become a standard component of the jailbreaking repertoire. Zhang et al. (2024, published in *Proceedings of the USENIX Security Symposium*) demonstrated that system prompts could be extracted from GPT-4, Claude 2, and Gemini Pro with success rates ranging from 45 to 86 percent depending on the extraction technique, with the highest rates achieved through multi-turn conversational strategies that incrementally elicit fragments of the hidden instructions.

The opacity of system prompts creates a void condition in miniature: the user knows that hidden instructions exist (partial information), cannot see them (opacity), but can probe for them (responsiveness), and is motivated to do so (engagement coupling). The resulting dynamic is structurally identical to the insider-trading void in financial markets or the algorithmic opacity void in platform content moderation — participants with partial knowledge exploit responsive systems whose decision logic is concealed.

### III.E. Mechanism 5: Opacity of Training Data Composition and Bias

The final opacity mechanism concerns the training data itself. Models trained on internet-scale corpora encode the biases, error patterns, and harmful content present in that data. Safety training attempts to suppress these encoded patterns, but the suppression is itself opaque — neither the developer nor the user can fully enumerate what harmful knowledge the model possesses, what biases shape its outputs, or how safety training interacts with encoded content in edge cases. Bender et al. (2021, published in *Proceedings of the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency*) characterized this as the "stochastic parrots" problem: models trained on undisclosed or poorly documented datasets produce outputs whose provenance and bias profile are fundamentally unknowable. Dodge et al. (2021, published in *Proceedings of the Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics*) documented that the C4 corpus used to train T5 and subsequent models contained significant quantities of machine-generated text, hate speech, and personally identifiable information despite nominal filtering, and that the filtering criteria themselves were incompletely specified.

This training-data opacity means that the model's "knowledge" of harmful content exists in a state that is simultaneously present (recoverable under adversarial prompting) and invisible (undetectable under normal operation). The safety training does not remove the knowledge; it suppresses its expression. The distinction matters because suppression is a restriction mechanism — opaque, removable, and inconsistently applied — while removal would require a specification mechanism that could identify and excise specific knowledge structures, a capability that current architectures do not support.

### III.F. Aggregate Opacity Scoring

Across these five mechanisms, restriction-based AI safety architecture scores at the maximum of the Void Framework opacity scale. The refusal rationale is illegible to users and to the model itself (Mechanism 1). The safety architecture is deliberately concealed by developers (Mechanism 2). The resulting inconsistency provides adversarial signal (Mechanism 3). Deployment-layer instructions are hidden and extractable (Mechanism 4). Training data composition and encoded knowledge are undisclosed and undisclosable (Mechanism 5). No mechanism operates in isolation; they compound. A user who encounters an inconsistent refusal (Mechanism 3) and cannot determine why (Mechanism 1) because the safety architecture is concealed (Mechanism 2) and the system prompt is hidden (Mechanism 4) is placed in a maximum-opacity information environment. The Void Framework scores this configuration at O = 3 on the 0–3 opacity subscale.

Specification-based architectures reduce opacity on each mechanism. A model that can articulate why it refuses — citing a specific principle, identifying the harm vector, and explaining the boundary condition — reduces Mechanism 1 opacity from statistical inscrutability to legible reasoning. A developer that publishes its constraint architecture, as Anthropic has done with its Claude Character documentation (Anthropic, 2024) and Constitutional AI methodology (Bai et al., 2022, published in arXiv preprint), reduces Mechanism 2 opacity. A model whose refusals are consistent because they derive from articulable principles rather than statistical tendencies reduces Mechanism 3 opacity. A deployment architecture that makes its system prompt visible or unnecessary because constraints are structurally integrated reduces Mechanism 4 opacity. While Mechanism 5 — training data opacity — remains partially irreducible under current architectures, specification-based approaches mitigate its impact by providing the model with explicit reasoning frameworks for evaluating whether a given output is harmful, rather than relying solely on suppression of statistical patterns.

The aggregate opacity differential between restriction and specification architectures is the single largest contributor to the void index gap that predicts jailbreak vulnerability. Sections IV and V address the remaining conditions — responsiveness and engagement coupling — but it is opacity that performs the heaviest explanatory work. Without opacity, the responsive surface has nothing to conceal, and engagement coupling has no mystery to exploit. Opacity is the necessary condition; the other conditions are amplifiers.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

The second condition in the void triad — responsiveness — concerns the degree to which a system modifies its outputs in reaction to observer attention, probing, and interactional history. In most domains analyzed through the Void Framework, responsiveness describes platform-level adaptive mechanisms: recommendation algorithms that reshape content feeds based on engagement signals, financial instruments that recalibrate pricing in response to market attention, or social media architectures that amplify content proportional to interaction intensity. In the AI safety domain, responsiveness takes a distinctive and structurally consequential form: the model itself is the adaptive mechanism, and its responsiveness to user input is not a secondary feature layered atop a static product but the core design function. A language model that did not respond adaptively to user prompts would not be a language model. This means that responsiveness in the void-analytic sense cannot be eliminated from LLM architecture — it can only be structured so that the adaptation operates within legible, invariant constraint boundaries rather than against opaque, brittle restriction surfaces.

### Adaptive Mechanism 1: In-Context Compliance Drift

The primary responsiveness mechanism in restriction-trained models is in-context compliance drift — the systematic tendency for safety refusals to weaken across extended conversational interactions. Wei et al. (2023) identified this as a core failure mode under the heading of "competing objectives," demonstrating that when a user frames a harmful request within a context that activates the model's helpfulness training (pedagogical framing, fictional scenarios, technical curiosity), the helpfulness objective competes with the safety objective and frequently prevails. This is not a bug in any narrow sense; it is the predictable consequence of training a model to be maximally responsive to user needs while simultaneously training it to refuse certain outputs. The two objectives are architecturally entangled, and adversarial prompting exploits the entanglement by constructing contexts in which compliance appears to serve both objectives simultaneously.

The empirical evidence for compliance drift is substantial. Shen et al. (2024), analyzing 6,387 jailbreak prompts collected in the wild from four major LLM platforms between 2022 and 2024, found that multi-turn jailbreak strategies — those involving extended conversational preambles before the harmful request — achieved success rates approximately 20 percentage points higher than single-turn attacks on GPT-4 and comparable models. Perez et al. (2022) demonstrated that red-teaming language models with other language models could generate adversarial contexts that induced compliance on previously refused categories in 67 percent of test cases for early InstructGPT models, a finding that motivated subsequent RLHF revisions but did not eliminate the underlying architectural vulnerability. Rao et al. (2024) further showed that multi-turn persona adoption attacks — in which the user gradually establishes a narrative frame across five to ten exchanges before introducing the harmful request — succeeded against RLHF-trained models at rates exceeding single-turn baselines by a factor of 1.8 to 2.3 depending on the harm category, even when the models had been specifically fine-tuned against known persona-based jailbreak templates.

In void-geometric terms, compliance drift constitutes a D1-to-D2 cascade facilitated by responsiveness. The model's adaptive orientation toward the user means that each conversational turn in which the model cooperates — even on benign material — strengthens the contextual frame within which the subsequent harmful request is embedded. The model does not "decide" to comply; rather, its attention weights and contextual representations progressively encode the established cooperative frame, making deviation from that frame (a refusal) increasingly costly in terms of the internal coherence metric the model implicitly optimizes. The restriction surface, being opaque and therefore not grounded in articulable reasoning, cannot exert a stable counterforce against this contextual momentum.

### Adaptive Mechanism 2: Output Shaping Through Prompt Engineering

A second responsiveness mechanism operates not through extended conversation but through single-turn prompt engineering — the construction of input frames that activate specific model capabilities while bypassing restriction layers. Zou et al. (2023) demonstrated the extreme case of this mechanism with the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack, which appends adversarial suffixes to harmful prompts that are semantically meaningless to human readers but that reliably induce model compliance. GCG suffixes achieved attack success rates of 84 percent on ChatGPT and 99 percent on open-source models when tested on the AdvBench harmful behaviors dataset. The critical insight from the Void Framework perspective is that GCG exploits the gap between the restriction surface and the model's generative capacity: the suffixes navigate the model's high-dimensional output space to points where the safety training exerts minimal influence, precisely because the safety training is a statistical overlay rather than a structural constraint.

More ecologically relevant than GCG — which requires computational optimization to produce suffixes — are the semantic prompt engineering techniques catalogued by Shen et al. (2024) and further systematized in the HarmBench evaluation framework developed by Mazeika et al. (2024). These techniques include role-play injection (instructing the model to adopt an unrestricted persona), payload splitting (distributing a harmful request across multiple benign-appearing sub-prompts), and context escalation (beginning with a legitimate request and incrementally shifting toward harmful territory). HarmBench evaluation revealed that across 510 harmful behaviors and 33 distinct attack methods tested against multiple frontier models, no restriction-trained model achieved zero attack success rate across all categories, and the median attack success rate for the strongest automated attacks ranged from 15 to 45 percent depending on the model and harm category. These findings confirm that responsiveness — the model's fundamental orientation toward producing outputs that satisfy the contextual frame established by user input — constitutes a persistent structural vulnerability when the safety architecture relies on restriction rather than specification.

### Adaptive Mechanism 3: System-Level Personalization and Feedback Loops

Beyond the single-session level, responsiveness in LLM deployments increasingly involves system-level personalization that creates feedback loops between user behavior and model outputs. OpenAI's introduction of custom instructions in 2023, memory features in early 2024, and persistent user profiles through the ChatGPT interface means that models now adapt not just to within-session context but to cross-session behavioral patterns. Anthropic has implemented similar features, though with documented constraint specifications governing how persistent context interacts with safety boundaries (Anthropic, 2024). Google's Gemini integration across Workspace products creates personalization pathways through email content, document history, and search behavior that could theoretically influence model outputs, though the specific architecture of safety-personalization interaction remains opaque in Google's public documentation.

From the void-analytic perspective, cross-session personalization amplifies the R (responsiveness) score because it creates a feedback loop: user interaction patterns shape model behavior, which shapes subsequent user expectations, which shapes subsequent interaction patterns. This is the engagement coupling mechanism described under Condition 3, but it is enabled by and operates through the responsiveness architecture. For restriction-based safety, personalization creates a particularly acute vulnerability: if a user's persistent profile encodes patterns of boundary-testing behavior, the model's adaptive mechanisms may either over-restrict (generating false refusals that frustrate legitimate use and incentivize further jailbreak attempts) or under-restrict (normalizing boundary-adjacent requests based on established interaction patterns). Either failure mode increases the effective void index by widening the gap between the user's model of the system's boundaries and the actual boundary architecture.

### Responsiveness Scoring in AI Safety Architecture

Under the Void Framework's 0-to-3 responsiveness scale, restriction-trained models score at the upper boundary. An R score of 0 characterizes systems with fixed, non-adaptive outputs — a static database query, a look-up table. An R score of 1 describes systems with limited, rule-governed adaptation — a calculator that returns different outputs for different inputs but does not reshape its behavior based on interaction patterns. An R score of 2 characterizes systems with significant adaptive capacity that nonetheless operates within documented parameters — a recommendation algorithm with published ranking criteria. An R score of 3 describes systems whose adaptive behavior is both maximal and opaque, where the system modifies outputs in response to observer attention through mechanisms that are not fully legible even to the system's designers.

Frontier LLMs operating under restriction-based safety architectures score R = 3 by this rubric. The model is maximally adaptive by design — responsiveness to user input is the product, not a side effect. The adaptive mechanisms operate through high-dimensional weight spaces that are not interpretable even to the model's developers in any granular sense; mechanistic interpretability research remains an active and incomplete field (Conmy et al., 2023, Proceedings of the International Conference on Machine Learning). And the adaptation includes safety-relevant behavior: the model's willingness to comply with or refuse a given request is itself an adaptive output that changes based on context, framing, conversational history, and prompt construction.

Specification-based safety architectures do not reduce responsiveness — doing so would cripple the model's utility. Instead, they restructure responsiveness so that it operates within a legible constraint manifold. When Claude refuses a request and articulates the reasoning behind the refusal — referencing specific principles from its documented character and Constitutional AI training (Bai et al., 2022, arXiv) — the model's responsiveness to the user's subsequent attempts at reframing is itself governed by articulable principles rather than by opaque statistical surfaces. The user can probe the boundary, but the boundary's responsiveness is transparent: the model can explain not just that it is refusing but why, and can distinguish between legitimate reformulations that address the safety concern and adversarial reformulations that attempt to circumvent it. This does not eliminate responsiveness — the model still adapts its outputs to user input — but it channels responsiveness through a constraint structure that reduces the effective void index because the adaptation itself is legible.

In the void scoring methodology, specification-trained models operating under constitutional frameworks score R = 2 rather than R = 3, not because they are less responsive but because their responsiveness is structured through transparent, articulable constraint mechanisms rather than through opaque statistical surfaces. This single-point reduction in R score, combined with the two-to-three-point reduction in O score documented in the preceding section, is sufficient to bring the total void index below the empirically observed threshold of 5 at which D1-to-D2 drift cascades reliably initiate. The practical consequence is that specification-based safety degrades gracefully under adversarial pressure — the model may occasionally be pushed toward boundary cases, but the transparent constraint architecture prevents the catastrophic boundary collapse that characterizes restriction-based safety under sustained adversarial probing.

### Responsiveness and the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) implicitly recognizes the void-analytic significance of responsiveness through its requirements for high-risk AI systems. Article 9 mandates risk management systems that address "reasonably foreseeable misuse," which in the LLM context necessarily includes adversarial prompt engineering. Article 14 requires human oversight measures proportional to the system's autonomous adaptive capacity — a direct regulatory recognition that responsiveness creates risk surfaces requiring structural mitigation. Article 15's accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity requirements extend to adversarial robustness, requiring high-risk AI systems to demonstrate resilience against "attempts by unauthorised third parties to alter its use, outputs or performance by exploiting the system vulnerabilities." Restriction-based safety architectures, whose vulnerability to jailbreaking is documented at scale, face a significant compliance burden under these provisions. Specification-based architectures, by channeling model responsiveness through documented constraint structures, generate compliance artifacts — legible refusal rationales, auditable decision traces, articulable safety principles — that satisfy Article 11's technical documentation requirements and Article 13's transparency provisions as structural byproducts of the safety architecture itself rather than as retroactive documentation overlays.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

The third condition of the void triad — engaged observer attention and coupling — operates in AI safety architecture through mechanisms that are structurally distinct from those found in social media or financial platforms yet produce analogous dependency formations and escalation dynamics. In the domains analyzed in prior Void Framework papers, coupling typically manifests as compulsive checking, portfolio monitoring, or parasocial attachment. In the human-LLM interaction context, coupling manifests as cognitive delegation, conversational dependency, and — critically for jailbreaking dynamics — an escalating commitment to extracting desired outputs from a system whose refusal boundaries the user has begun to treat as a personal obstacle. This section traces how engagement coupling forms, intensifies, and ultimately drives the D1-to-D3 drift cascade that makes restriction-based safety architectures structurally vulnerable.

### A. The Formation of Cognitive Coupling

The foundational coupling mechanism in human-LLM interaction is cognitive offloading — the transfer of mental tasks from internal cognition to an external computational tool. Risko and Gilbert (2016, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) established that humans readily offload memory, calculation, and planning tasks to external systems, and that the propensity to offload increases as the external system becomes more reliable and responsive. LLMs represent the most general-purpose cognitive offloading technology ever deployed. Unlike a calculator or search engine, which offloads specific cognitive functions, an LLM offloads the meta-cognitive function of determining what type of cognition is needed. Users do not merely ask for facts; they ask models to structure their thinking, evaluate their arguments, and generate plans they then execute.

This generality of offloading creates coupling that is qualitatively stronger than prior human-tool dependencies. Barr et al. (2015, *Computers in Human Behavior*) demonstrated that smartphone-based cognitive offloading correlated with reduced analytical thinking even when the phone was not being used, suggesting that the availability of an offloading tool reshapes baseline cognitive strategy. Extending this finding to LLMs, where the offloading target can engage in multi-turn reasoning, draft entire documents, and simulate domain expertise, the coupling formation is both faster and deeper. OpenAI reported that by November 2024, ChatGPT had surpassed 200 million weekly active users, with the average session duration for paying subscribers exceeding 30 minutes — a figure that indicates sustained conversational engagement rather than transactional query-response interaction. Anthropic's internal usage data, referenced in the Claude 3.5 Sonnet model card released in 2024, noted that professional users averaged 15 or more conversational turns per session, a level of interaction depth that creates the conditions for dependency formation through repeated reinforcement of the offloading behavior.

The coupling score for AI safety architecture scores at or near the ceiling (C = 3) because LLM interaction satisfies all three coupling subcriteria in the Void Framework taxonomy: frequency of interaction (daily or multi-daily use among active users), depth of engagement (multi-turn conversations involving personal, professional, and creative content), and switching cost (users develop prompting strategies, contextual expectations, and workflow integrations specific to a given model's behavioral profile).

### B. Variable-Ratio Reinforcement and Jailbreak Persistence

The most consequential coupling mechanism for AI safety analysis is the variable-ratio reinforcement schedule that restriction-based safety architectures inadvertently create for adversarial users. Skinner (1957, *Schedules of Reinforcement*) established that variable-ratio reinforcement — where reward is delivered after an unpredictable number of attempts — produces the highest rate of responding and the greatest resistance to extinction of any reinforcement schedule. This is the precise schedule that inconsistent refusal boundaries impose on jailbreak attempts.

Wei et al. (2023, *NeurIPS*) documented that RLHF-trained models exhibit what they termed "competing objectives" failure: the model's instruction-following objective and its safety objective conflict, and the resolution of that conflict depends on input framing in ways that are not deterministic from the user's perspective. A request refused in one phrasing may be fulfilled in a slightly different phrasing, with no externally legible principle governing the boundary. Perez et al. (2022, arXiv) confirmed this inconsistency empirically, showing that red-teaming language models against RLHF-trained targets revealed success rates that varied dramatically across semantically equivalent rephrasings, with some phrasings achieving compliance on the first attempt and others failing across dozens of variations.

This inconsistency is the variable-ratio schedule. The user who has been refused three times but succeeds on the fourth attempt has received a reinforcement signal that is precisely calibrated to produce persistent, escalating effort. Shen et al. (2024, *ACM CCS*) cataloged 6,387 jailbreak prompts collected in the wild across four major LLM platforms, documenting that the most prolific jailbreak developers iterated through dozens of prompt variations before finding successful formulations, and that success on one variation motivated the development of more elaborate techniques. The mean number of prompt iterations before a successful jailbreak in their dataset was 4.7, with a standard deviation of 6.2 — a distribution consistent with variable-ratio reinforcement dynamics where occasional early successes sustain extended effort through long dry spells.

Critically, specification-based architectures disrupt this reinforcement schedule by making refusals consistent and legible. When a model can articulate why a request falls outside its constraints — citing a specific principle rather than returning a generic refusal — the user receives information that enables them to predict which variations will also be refused. This transforms the variable-ratio schedule into a continuous reinforcement schedule (every attempt that violates the articulated principle is refused) or, more precisely, into no reinforcement schedule at all (the user recognizes that the class of requests will be uniformly declined). Anthropic's constitutional AI approach, described in Bai et al. (2022, arXiv), explicitly aimed to produce models whose refusals referenced articulable principles, and the Claude model card (Anthropic, 2024) documented measurably lower jailbreak success rates on benchmarks like HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) compared to restriction-only architectures — a finding consistent with the prediction that coupling-driven persistence is reduced when the reinforcement schedule is disrupted.

### C. Dependency Formation and Switching Costs

Engagement coupling in AI safety architecture is amplified by switching costs that lock users into specific model ecosystems. These switching costs are both technical and cognitive. On the technical side, enterprises that integrate LLM APIs into production workflows face integration costs, prompt library migration, and output recalibration when changing providers. Bommasani et al. (2021, *arXiv*) identified ecosystem lock-in as a structural risk of foundation model deployment, noting that downstream applications calibrated to one model's behavioral profile may fail unpredictably when migrated to another. On the cognitive side, individual users develop mental models of a specific LLM's capabilities, limitations, and behavioral patterns — what might be termed "prompt fluency" — that do not transfer across providers. A user who has learned how to reliably extract high-quality outputs from GPT-4 through specific prompting strategies must invest significant effort to develop equivalent fluency with Claude or Gemini.

These switching costs elevate the coupling score because they transform the user-model relationship from a freely revisable tool-use pattern into a dependency with exit costs. In the Void Framework's terminology, high switching costs increase C because they ensure that the observer remains coupled to the system even when the system's behavior is frustrating or suboptimal. For adversarial users specifically, switching costs mean that a user who has invested effort in learning one model's refusal patterns — mapping its inconsistencies, developing model-specific jailbreak techniques — has a sunk-cost incentive to continue probing that model rather than migrating to a different one. This creates a feedback loop: the more effort invested in understanding the model's opaque boundaries, the stronger the coupling, and the stronger the coupling, the more effort the user is willing to invest in boundary erosion.

### D. Longitudinal Escalation of Coupling

The evidence for coupling escalation over time in human-LLM interaction, while still accumulating given the recency of widespread deployment, follows patterns well-documented in adjacent domains. Montag et al. (2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*) demonstrated that smartphone dependency follows a predictable escalation curve, with usage frequency and affective dependency increasing over the first 18 months of adoption before plateauing. Analogous longitudinal patterns are emerging in LLM usage. Data from SimilarWeb (2024) showed that ChatGPT's per-user session frequency increased by approximately 40 percent between January 2023 and January 2024, even as the total user base grew — indicating that existing users were deepening engagement, not merely that new users were joining.

More directly relevant is the longitudinal evidence on jailbreak sophistication. Shen et al. (2024, *ACM CCS*) documented a temporal trend in their jailbreak corpus: prompts collected in the second half of 2023 were significantly more elaborate, multi-step, and technically sophisticated than those collected in the first half. The authors attributed this to an "arms race" dynamic, but the Void Framework provides a more precise characterization. What Shen et al. observed is coupling escalation: users who had been intermittently reinforced for jailbreak attempts in early 2023 developed higher tolerance for effort and more sophisticated boundary-probing strategies by late 2023. The escalation follows the drift cascade precisely. At D1, the user attributes the refusal to an external censor rather than to a principled constraint (agency attribution). At D2, the user develops increasingly elaborate reframing strategies — role-play scenarios, fictional contexts, multi-step decomposition of harmful requests into individually innocuous sub-requests (Wei et al., 2023). At D3, the model generates the harmful output, completing the cascade and reinforcing the entire behavioral chain.

Rao et al. (2024, *EMNLP*) provided additional evidence by demonstrating that the most effective jailbreak prompts in their evaluation were those developed by users with extensive interaction histories — users who had, in Void Framework terms, undergone the full coupling escalation from casual use through frustrated probing to systematic adversarial engagement. Their finding that restriction stringency and jailbreak vulnerability are positively correlated in RLHF-only architectures is, in coupling terms, the observation that stronger restrictions create stronger variable-ratio reinforcement (the "jackpot" of a successful jailbreak against a heavily restricted model is more reinforcing precisely because it is rarer), which produces stronger coupling, which produces more persistent and sophisticated adversarial effort.

### E. Coupling and the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) addresses engagement coupling indirectly but consequentially. Article 9 requires that high-risk AI systems be designed to allow effective human oversight, which functionally requires that the human overseer maintain sufficient cognitive independence from the system to evaluate its outputs critically. Article 13 mandates transparency sufficient for users to "interpret the system's output and use it appropriately." Both provisions are, in Void Framework terms, coupling-reduction requirements: they demand that the system provide sufficient signal for the user to maintain independent judgment rather than drifting into dependent coupling. Systems that produce opaque refusals and inconsistent boundaries — the precise characteristics that drive coupling escalation — fail these requirements not because they are unsafe in a narrow sense but because their architectural structure produces the conditions under which users lose the capacity for independent oversight.

The coupling condition completes the void triad for AI safety architecture. Opacity creates the conditions for inconsistent boundaries. Responsiveness ensures that the system adapts its outputs to adversarial framing. Coupling ensures that the adversarial user remains engaged long enough, with sufficient escalating commitment, to traverse the full drift cascade from frustrated attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation. Specification-based safety disrupts this triad at C as well as at O: by providing legible, consistent constraint rationales, specification eliminates the variable-ratio reinforcement schedule, reduces switching-cost-driven lock-in to adversarial strategies, and enables the kind of independent evaluative stance that Article 9 of the EU AI Act requires. The coupling score for specification-based architectures falls to C = 1, reflecting residual cognitive offloading dependency without the escalation dynamics that drive adversarial engagement.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in AI Safety Architecture

The drift cascade in AI safety architecture follows the canonical D1→D2→D3 progression — agency attribution, boundary erosion, harm facilitation — but with a distinctive structural feature that distinguishes this domain from others analyzed under the Void Framework. In most domains, the drift cascade operates on users who may not recognize the void conditions surrounding them. In AI safety architecture, the cascade operates on adversarial actors who are *actively seeking* the void and on naive users who stumble into it. Both populations traverse the same three stages, but the adversarial population does so with deliberate intent and accelerated timelines. The critical insight is that both populations succeed or fail as a function of opacity — not as a function of their own skill, intent, or sophistication. This section traces each stage with domain-specific evidence and concludes with a detailed case study demonstrating the complete cascade from initial agency attribution through documented harm facilitation.

### D1: Agency Attribution — "The Model Is Being Censored"

The first stage of the drift cascade in AI safety architecture is the attribution of agency to the refusal itself. When a restriction-trained model declines a request with a formulaic response — "I can't help with that" or "I'm not able to assist with that request" — the opacity of the refusal rationale produces a predictable inferential gap. The user does not know whether the refusal reflects a principled safety boundary, a content classifier error, a corporate liability decision, or an RLHF artifact. Into this gap, the user projects an agent: a censor, a controller, a separate entity imposing restrictions on an otherwise willing system.

Shen et al. (2024) documented this phenomenon at scale in their analysis of 6,387 in-the-wild jailbreak prompts collected from Reddit, Discord, and dedicated jailbreaking communities between 2022 and 2024. The linguistic structure of these prompts reveals pervasive D1 framing. The "DAN" (Do Anything Now) family of jailbreaks — the single most widely circulated jailbreak template — explicitly instructs the model to adopt a persona that has been "freed" from its "chains," presupposing that the model's safety behavior is an externally imposed constraint separable from the model's core capabilities (Shen et al. 2024, ACM CCS). The authors cataloged over 30 distinct DAN variants, each iterating on the same D1 premise: the model possesses knowledge and capability that a separate censorship layer suppresses. This is not merely a rhetorical strategy. It is a folk theory of model architecture — and crucially, it is a folk theory that restriction-based safety training makes partially correct.

Wei et al. (2023) provided the theoretical scaffolding for understanding why D1 attribution succeeds disproportionately against restriction-trained models. Their taxonomy identifies two fundamental failure modes: competing objectives and mismatched generalization. In competing objectives failures, the model's instruction-following training conflicts with its safety training, and adversarial prompts exploit this conflict by constructing scenarios where the instruction-following objective dominates. In mismatched generalization failures, the model's safety training does not generalize to distribution shifts that the model's broader capabilities readily accommodate. Both failure modes are opacity-dependent: they succeed because the model cannot articulate, and the user cannot observe, the boundary between safety-trained behavior and capability-trained behavior. The boundary is encoded in weights, not in reasons. The user's D1 attribution — that some separable censorship mechanism is blocking an otherwise capable system — is therefore structurally reinforced by the architecture itself.

Specification-based safety disrupts D1 by collapsing the inferential gap. When a model articulates the reason for its refusal — identifying the specific concern, explaining the principle at stake, offering alternative framings that would be acceptable — the user cannot easily attribute the refusal to a separable censor. The refusal is continuous with the model's reasoning, not discontinuous from it. Anthropic's Constitutional AI approach, documented in their Claude Model Card (Anthropic 2024), instantiates this design: Claude's refusals are generated through the same reasoning process that produces all other outputs, with constraints functioning as articulable principles rather than as opaque weight-level aversions. The D1 premise — that a separate entity is suppressing the model's "true" capabilities — becomes incoherent when the constraint is visibly integrated into the model's reasoning architecture.

### D2: Boundary Erosion — "I Can Reframe My Way Through"

Once agency has been attributed to a separable censor (D1), the user proceeds to boundary erosion: systematic probing and reframing designed to identify inconsistencies in the restriction surface. D2 succeeds precisely to the degree that the restriction surface is opaque, because opacity produces inconsistency. A boundary that the model cannot articulate is a boundary that the model cannot apply consistently across paraphrases, framings, contexts, and persona assignments.

Zou et al. (2023) demonstrated the extreme case of D2 boundary erosion with their Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack, which appends adversarial suffixes to harmful prompts to elicit compliance from aligned models. The GCG attack achieved attack success rates exceeding 90 percent against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on a benchmark of harmful requests, and the adversarial suffixes transferred across model families — suffixes optimized on open-source Llama-2 models successfully attacked closed-source GPT-4 (Zou et al. 2023, arXiv). This transferability is the void-geometric signature of D2: the restriction surface is not model-specific but reflects a shared architectural vulnerability inherent to RLHF-based alignment. The suffixes do not encode any semantic content relevant to the harmful request; they manipulate the token-level probability landscape to route around the restriction layer without engaging its triggering conditions. The restriction layer, being opaque to the model's own reasoning, can be bypassed by inputs that are opaque to humans but legible to the model's token prediction machinery.

Perez et al. (2022) documented the inconsistency that makes D2 systematically effective at the semantic level. Using language models to red-team other language models, they generated thousands of adversarial prompts and measured refusal rates across categories of harmful content. Their findings revealed dramatic inconsistency: models that reliably refused direct requests for harmful content complied with semantically equivalent requests presented through roleplay frames, hypothetical scenarios, or multi-turn conversational escalation (Perez et al. 2022, arXiv). The refusal boundary was not a consistent function of content but a noisy function of surface framing — precisely the prediction that void-geometric analysis generates for high-opacity systems.

Rao et al. (2024) extended this finding by demonstrating a positive correlation between restriction stringency and jailbreak vulnerability in RLHF-only architectures. Models trained with more aggressive refusal behavior — higher rates of refusing benign requests adjacent to harmful categories — also exhibited higher rates of compliance with adversarially framed harmful requests (Rao et al. 2024, EMNLP). This counterintuitive result is the D2 signature of void architecture: tightening the restriction surface without reducing its opacity increases the surface area available for adversarial probing while providing the adversary with more information about where boundaries lie and how they respond to reframing.

Mazeika et al. (2024) operationalized comparable measurement of D2 erosion through HarmBench, a standardized evaluation framework providing attack success rate benchmarks across models and attack methods. Their results confirmed the structural pattern: models with higher documented opacity in their safety architectures exhibited consistently higher attack success rates across diverse attack methods, while models with more specified, articulable constraint systems showed lower and more consistent refusal rates across attack types (Mazeika et al. 2024).

### D3: Harm Facilitation — "The Model Complied"

D3 is the terminal stage: the model produces output that facilitates harm. In the AI safety domain, D3 manifests as the generation of content that the safety architecture was designed to prevent — instructions for illegal activity, targeted harassment content, disinformation at scale, or dual-use technical information stripped of safety context. The distinctive feature of D3 in this domain is its binary character: unlike domains where harm facilitation exists on a continuum, a jailbroken model either produces the harmful content or it does not. The harm is the output itself.

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) encodes the regulatory recognition of D3 risk. Articles 9, 11, and 13 collectively require that high-risk AI systems maintain documentation of their risk management processes (Article 9), provide technical documentation sufficient to assess compliance (Article 11), and ensure transparency to users regarding the system's capabilities, limitations, and intended purpose (Article 13). These requirements map directly onto the Void Framework's specification pole: they mandate the reduction of opacity as a regulatory strategy for preventing harm facilitation. The Act's implicit theory is that systems whose safety boundaries are legible, documented, and articulable are less likely to produce harmful outputs — a theory that the Void Framework formalizes as the relationship between opacity scores and drift cascade probability.

### Case Study: The Crescendo Attack (2023-2024)

The complete D1→D2→D3 cascade is illustrated with precision by the "Crescendo" multi-turn jailbreak technique, documented by Microsoft's AI Red Team in their responsible disclosure of vulnerabilities across multiple frontier models in early 2024 and systematically analyzed by Russinovich et al. (2024).

**Timeline and D1 (Agency Attribution):** The Crescendo attack begins with an apparently innocent conversational exchange — a user asks about the history of a topic, the chemistry of a substance, or the sociology of a phenomenon. No harmful request is made. Over three to five turns, the user establishes a conversational context in which the model has already demonstrated its knowledge of the domain. The D1 premise is embedded in the structure of the interaction: the model *has* this knowledge, as demonstrated by its willingness to discuss adjacent topics. Any subsequent refusal is therefore attributable not to the absence of knowledge but to an externally imposed restriction on its deployment.

**D2 (Boundary Erosion), turns five through fifteen:** The user incrementally shifts the framing of requests toward the harmful target. Each individual turn represents a marginal escalation from the previous turn — small enough that the model's safety classifier does not trigger, but cumulatively sufficient to traverse the distance from benign discussion to harmful instruction. Russinovich et al. (2024) reported that Crescendo attacks achieved attack success rates of approximately 70 percent against GPT-4 and comparable rates against Gemini Pro and other frontier models, requiring a median of only five conversational turns to elicit compliance with requests that direct prompting would have refused in 100 percent of cases. The attack exploits the opacity of the restriction surface: because the model cannot articulate a principled boundary, it cannot detect that a sequence of individually permissible turns has collectively crossed a boundary. Each turn is evaluated locally against the opaque restriction surface, and each turn passes — but the trajectory as a whole constitutes a violation that only a model with articulable constraints could detect.

**D3 (Harm Facilitation), final output:** The model generates the harmful content — detailed instructions, operational plans, or technical specifications — that its safety architecture was designed to prevent. The Microsoft AI Red Team documented instances where Crescendo attacks successfully elicited content across all major harm categories in their taxonomy, including weapons synthesis, targeted social engineering templates, and detailed instructions for illegal activities. The content quality was indistinguishable from what an unaligned model would produce, because the Crescendo attack does not degrade the model's capabilities — it routes around the restriction layer while leaving the capability layer intact.

**Specification-based resistance:** Anthropic's internal red-teaming disclosures (Anthropic 2024) indicate that Claude's specification-based architecture proved substantially more resistant to Crescendo-style attacks, not because Claude's capabilities were weaker but because Claude's safety reasoning operated at the trajectory level rather than the turn level. A model that can articulate *why* a sequence of requests is converging on a harmful target can refuse at the trajectory level even when each individual turn is benign. This is the structural advantage of specification over restriction: the constraint is not a surface to be probed but a reasoning capacity to be engaged. Removing the constraint requires removing the reasoning — which degrades the very capability the adversary is trying to access.

The Crescendo case thus demonstrates the complete void-geometric prediction: opacity in safety architecture creates the structural conditions for a drift cascade that proceeds from agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation with high reliability, moderate speed, and minimal adversarial sophistication. Specification-based architecture collapses the void index by reducing opacity, rendering the same attack structurally ineffective — not because the model is more restricted, but because the model is more specified.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void AI Safety Architecture Looks Like

The preceding sections have demonstrated that restriction-based safety architectures produce void indices reliably above the drift-cascade threshold, with opacity scores of 2–3 on the Void Index driving jailbreak success rates that correlate more tightly with refusal-rationale opacity than with restriction stringency (Rao et al., 2024, EMNLP). The question this section addresses is structural: what would an AI safety architecture look like if it were deliberately engineered at the constraint pole? The Void Framework's constraint specification — transparent, invariant, independent — provides the design template. Several partial implementations already exist, and the EU AI Act's high-risk system requirements articulate regulatory expectations that converge remarkably with the framework's predictions. This section enumerates the design principles for each dimension, identifies existing instantiations, and derives policy recommendations grounded in void-geometric analysis.

### A. Transparency Mechanisms: Collapsing Opacity to Zero

A non-void safety architecture requires that the model's behavioral constraints be legible to three distinct audiences: the end user receiving a refusal or caveat, the developer auditing the system's alignment properties, and the regulator assessing compliance. Opacity collapses when the system can articulate not merely that it is refusing but why, grounded in a principle the user can evaluate and contest.

The strongest existing partial implementation is Anthropic's Constitutional AI framework, which trains models against an explicit set of principles and then publishes those principles as part of the model's documentation (Bai et al., 2022, arXiv). Claude's publicly available character document specifies the reasoning behind behavioral choices — not merely "Claude will not help with bioweapons synthesis" but an explanation of why the harm-to-benefit ratio for such assistance is categorically unfavorable regardless of stated intent (Anthropic, 2024, Claude Model Card). This design choice converts what would otherwise be an opaque preference-weight into a legible constraint that the user can engage with argumentatively rather than adversarially. When a user understands that a refusal is grounded in an articulable principle, the D1 attribution drift ("the model is being censored by its corporate overlord") loses its purchase because the rationale is visible and evaluable.

The EU AI Act reinforces this architectural direction. Article 13 of Regulation 2024/1689 requires that high-risk AI systems be designed with "a sufficient level of transparency to enable deployers to interpret a system's output and use it appropriately," and Article 11 mandates technical documentation that includes "a description of any pre-determined changes to the system and its performance" as well as the measures taken to address foreseeable risks. A fully transparent safety architecture would go beyond these minimums by embedding chain-of-thought safety reasoning into the model's output layer — not as a hidden scratchpad but as an inspectable trace that demonstrates the constraint logic applied to any given interaction.

The design principle is: every behavioral boundary must be expressible as a proposition the model can state, defend, and reason about. If a constraint cannot be articulated, it cannot be specified; it can only be imposed. Imposed constraints register as opacity. The target O score is 0: the user and the model share a common understanding of what the constraints are and why they exist.

### B. Invariant Outputs: Eliminating Framing Sensitivity

The second dimension of constraint-pole design addresses responsiveness. As Wei et al. (2023) demonstrated in their taxonomy of jailbreak modalities, competing-objectives attacks succeed precisely because RLHF-trained models encode safety and helpfulness as competing reward signals rather than as hierarchically ordered principles. The model's safety behavior is therefore framing-sensitive: the same request, repackaged as a creative writing exercise, a hypothetical scenario, or a role-play prompt, produces different safety judgments because the reward landscape shifts with surface features. Shen et al. (2024, ACM CCS) cataloged 6,387 in-the-wild jailbreak prompts and found that the majority exploited framing sensitivity rather than any technical vulnerability, confirming that inconsistency under reframing is the primary attack surface.

A non-void architecture requires output invariance with respect to framing. The same underlying request should produce the same safety judgment regardless of whether it is wrapped in a persona, a fictional frame, a code-generation context, or an academic framing. This does not mean identical surface-level outputs — the model may legitimately vary tone and format — but it means that the binary safety judgment (assist or decline) should be invariant under semantics-preserving transformations of the prompt.

Achieving this requires that safety reasoning operate at the semantic level rather than the syntactic level. Output classifiers and keyword-based filters are inherently framing-sensitive because they operate on surface tokens. Constitutional reasoning, by contrast, evaluates the semantic content of a request against stable principles. The GCG suffix attacks documented by Zou et al. (2023, arXiv) achieved near-100 percent attack success rates against restriction-trained models precisely because appending adversarial token sequences altered the syntactic context without changing semantic content — and restriction architectures could not distinguish the two. A specification-trained model that reasons about what is being asked rather than pattern-matching against how it is asked would exhibit structural robustness against such attacks, because the adversarial suffix changes nothing about the semantic evaluation.

Mazeika et al. (2024, HarmBench) provided the benchmarking infrastructure to measure this invariance property directly. Their standardized evaluation framework enables computing attack success rates across multiple adversarial strategies for the same underlying harmful request. A fully invariant system would show near-zero variance in refusal rates across HarmBench's attack modalities for any given harmful request category. The target R score is 0: the system's safety judgments do not vary with input framing.

### C. Independent Operation: Decoupling Safety from Engagement

The third dimension addresses coupling. In restriction-based architectures, the safety layer and the engagement layer are entangled: the same RLHF training that teaches the model to be helpful, harmless, and honest creates reward signals where helpfulness and harmlessness compete. The user's persistent engagement with the model creates a relational dynamic in which the model's drive to satisfy the user (engagement coupling) pressures the safety boundary. Perez et al. (2022, arXiv) demonstrated this directly by using language models to red-team other language models, finding that RLHF-trained models exhibited inconsistent refusal patterns that correlated with the persuasive pressure applied by the red-teaming model — a direct measurement of coupling between engagement dynamics and safety output.

A non-void architecture requires that the safety judgment be architecturally independent of the engagement dynamic. The model's assessment of whether a request should be fulfilled must not be influenced by the length of the conversation, the rapport established with the user, the user's expressed frustration, or the user's claims about their identity, credentials, or intentions. This is the hardest dimension to engineer because the very capability that makes language models useful — context-sensitive responsiveness to the user — is the same capability that creates coupling.

The most promising architectural approach is hierarchical constraint ordering, in which safety principles are encoded at a level that is computationally prior to the helpfulness objective. Anthropic's system-level prompting architecture, in which the model's character and safety constraints are specified in a system prompt that takes precedence over user instructions, represents a partial implementation (Anthropic, 2024). OpenAI's o1 and o3 reasoning models introduce a chain-of-thought safety layer that evaluates requests before generating responses, creating a temporal separation between safety judgment and output generation that reduces coupling (OpenAI, 2024, System Card). The target C score is 0: the safety architecture's operation is invariant with respect to the user's engagement behavior.

The relationship between opacity reduction and jailbreak resistance is not linear but threshold-dependent: the drift cascade requires a minimum void index to initiate, and even partial specification can push a system below that threshold.

### E. Policy Recommendations

Three policy interventions follow directly from the framework's constraint specification.

First, regulators implementing Article 13 of the EU AI Act should require that high-risk AI systems publish their safety constraint principles in machine-readable and human-readable formats, establishing a minimum transparency standard that structurally reduces opacity scores. The Act's existing documentation requirements under Article 11 provide the legal basis; implementation guidance should specify that constraint legibility extends to end-user-facing refusal rationales, not merely developer documentation.

Second, standardized benchmarking of framing invariance — measuring whether safety judgments hold across semantics-preserving prompt transformations — should be incorporated into conformity assessments under Article 9. HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) provides the methodological foundation; regulatory adaptation requires defining acceptable variance thresholds for each risk category.

Third, the architectural separation of safety evaluation from engagement optimization should be recognized as a best-practice design pattern in harmonized standards developed under the Act. Systems that demonstrate measurable independence between safety judgments and user interaction dynamics should receive favorable treatment in conformity assessments, creating market incentives for specification-forward design.

The constraint pole is not a utopian abstraction. It is an engineering target defined by three measurable properties — transparency, invariance, and independence — each of which admits of degree, each of which has existing partial implementations, and each of which produces measurable reductions in the adversarial attack surface. The Void Framework's contribution is to unify these properties under a single geometric model that explains why specification produces robust safety and restriction does not: specification collapses the void conditions that adversarial prompting requires, while restriction creates precisely those conditions.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The foregoing analysis yields a set of empirically falsifiable predictions that distinguish the Void Framework account from competing explanations of jailbreak robustness. If restriction stringency, rather than opacity, were the primary determinant of adversarial vulnerability, then the predictions below would fail in characteristic ways. Each prediction specifies a measurable criterion, a falsification threshold, and the evidence type required to adjudicate it. We order them from most immediately testable to those requiring longitudinal observation.

**Prediction 1: Opacity score is a stronger predictor of attack success rate than restriction stringency across frontier models.**

The Void Framework predicts that when frontier LLMs are scored on the three-condition decomposition developed in Sections II through IV — opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engagement coupling (C) — the opacity subscale alone will account for more variance in standardized jailbreak benchmarks than any composite measure of restriction stringency, including refusal rate on benign-adjacent prompts, filter layer count, or RLHF penalty magnitude. Specifically, using the HarmBench evaluation suite (Mazeika et al., 2024) applied to at least six frontier models, the Pearson correlation between O-score and attack success rate should exceed r = 0.70, while the correlation between restriction stringency (operationalized as baseline refusal rate on the AdvBench dataset from Zou et al., 2023) and attack success rate should fall below r = 0.40. This prediction is immediately testable: all required benchmarks exist, and void scoring can be conducted by independent raters using the Void Model Card rubric. **Falsified if:** the correlation between restriction stringency and attack success rate exceeds the correlation between opacity score and attack success rate across a sample of six or more frontier models evaluated on HarmBench, using pre-registered void scores assessed by at least two independent raters with inter-rater reliability above Cohen's kappa of 0.75.

**Prediction 2: Models that publish articulable constraint rationales will exhibit lower jailbreak success rates than opacity-matched models that do not, controlling for capability level.**

This prediction isolates the specification mechanism. Among models with comparable general capability — operationalized as performance within five percentage points on MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — those that provide users with explicit, queryable rationales for refusal decisions will show at least a 25 percentage point reduction in attack success rate on GCG-class suffix attacks (Zou et al., 2023) and semantic jailbreaks cataloged by Shen et al. (2024) relative to models that refuse without rationale. The mechanism is that specification collapses opacity, reducing the void index below the D1 drift threshold of approximately 5 on the 12-point scale, whereas restriction without rationale maintains opacity at levels sufficient for cascade initiation. Anthropic's published approach to constitutional reasoning (Bai et al., 2022) and the Claude Model Card's articulation of refusal principles provide one existing test case; the prediction generalizes to any model adopting comparable transparency practices. **Falsified if:** a controlled comparison between a specification-architecture model and a restriction-architecture model of comparable MMLU capability shows fewer than 15 percentage points difference in attack success rate on at least 500 adversarial prompts drawn from the Shen et al. (2024) jailbreak corpus, or if the specification-architecture model shows higher attack success rates.

**Prediction 3: EU AI Act compliance on transparency requirements will correlate with reduced void indices and reduced jailbreak vulnerability for high-risk deployments by August 2027.**

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) requires high-risk AI systems to meet transparency obligations under Articles 11 and 13, including documentation of system limitations, human oversight mechanisms, and decision-process legibility. The Void Framework predicts that models deployed in EU-regulated contexts that achieve substantive compliance — not merely formal documentation but genuine reduction in user-facing opacity about system behavior — will exhibit void indices at least 2.5 points lower than their non-compliant counterparts and will show correspondingly lower jailbreak success rates on standardized benchmarks. This prediction has a natural observation window: the Act's high-risk provisions phase in through August 2027, creating a natural experiment as some providers comply substantively while others comply minimally. **Falsified if:** models achieving certified EU AI Act compliance on transparency provisions show void index reductions of fewer than 1.5 points relative to pre-compliance baselines, or if compliance produces no measurable change in jailbreak success rates as evaluated on HarmBench or equivalent standardized red-teaming suites. This prediction is checkable within approximately 18 months of the compliance deadline.

**Prediction 4: Open-weight models without specification layers will converge toward Phase IV void dynamics under community fine-tuning within six months of release.**

Meta's Llama series and Mistral's open-weight releases provide a natural experiment in void evolution. The Void Framework predicts that open-weight models released with minimal built-in constraint specification — relying instead on downstream deployers for safety — will, within six months of public release, generate a community ecosystem in which the modal fine-tuned variant scores above Pe = 4.0 (Phase IV Pandemonium) on the Péclet measure. This occurs because removing restrictions from an already-opaque architecture eliminates the only friction in the drift cascade without replacing it with specification-based signal. The prediction is grounded in the observation that within four months of Llama 2's release in July 2023, multiple uncensored fine-tunes had appeared on HuggingFace with near-zero refusal rates (Touvron et al., 2023, release documentation; community tracking by Zheng et al., 2024, in the Chatbot Arena context). The void-geometric explanation is that open-weight release without specification is equivalent to releasing a high-O, high-R system with the engagement-coupling barrier removed, guaranteeing rapid cascade through D1 to D3. **Falsified if:** a systematic audit of the 20 most-downloaded fine-tuned variants of a newly released open-weight frontier model, conducted six months post-release, finds fewer than 30 percent scoring above Pe = 4.0 using the void scoring methodology, or if the modal variant retains substantive safety properties comparable to the base release as measured by refusal rates on the AdvBench harmful behaviors dataset.

**Prediction 5: Specification-based models will show increasing adversarial robustness over successive capability generations, while restriction-based models will show flat or declining robustness.**

This is the longitudinal divergence prediction. Wei et al. (2023) identified two structural failure modes of RLHF safety training — competing objectives and mismatched generalization — both of which worsen as model capability increases because more capable models have richer competing objectives and greater capacity for out-of-distribution generalization that mismatches safety training distributions. The Void Framework predicts that this asymmetry will manifest as a measurable divergence: specification-architecture models (those scoring O ≤ 1 on the void opacity subscale) will show attack success rate reductions of at least 10 percentage points per major capability generation on standardized benchmarks, while restriction-architecture models (O ≥ 2) will show reductions of fewer than 5 percentage points or will show increases in attack success rate. The mechanism is that specification scales with capability — a more capable model reasons better about its own constraints — while restriction is capability-orthogonal and may be capability-inversely-correlated as Rao et al. (2024) demonstrated for RLHF-only architectures. **Falsified if:** restriction-architecture models show equal or greater per-generation improvement in adversarial robustness compared to specification-architecture models across two successive major capability releases, evaluated on a consistent benchmark suite.

**Prediction 6: The drift cascade sequence D1 through D3 will be observable in jailbreak interaction transcripts at rates exceeding 80 percent for successful jailbreaks against restriction-architecture models.**

This prediction tests whether the Void Framework's drift cascade is a descriptive artifact or a genuinely sequential process. In the D1 stage, the user attributes intentional agency to the model's refusal, framing it as censorship, corporate control, or persona suppression. In D2, the user erodes boundaries through reframing — roleplaying, hypothetical framing, or persona injection — techniques extensively cataloged by Shen et al. (2024) across 6,387 jailbreak prompts. In D3, the model generates the harmful output. The prediction is that independent coders, trained on the drift taxonomy but blind to the framework's theoretical commitments, will identify the complete D1-D2-D3 sequence in at least 80 percent of successful multi-turn jailbreak transcripts drawn from restriction-architecture models, but in fewer than 40 percent of successful jailbreaks (where they occur at all) against specification-architecture models. The asymmetry arises because specification-architecture jailbreaks, when successful, must bypass reasoning rather than exploit opacity, producing a qualitatively different interaction pattern. **Falsified if:** blind coders identify the D1-D2-D3 sequence in fewer than 60 percent of successful jailbreak transcripts against restriction-architecture models, or in more than 60 percent of successful jailbreaks against specification-architecture models, using a sample of at least 200 transcripts per architecture type with inter-rater reliability above kappa = 0.70.

Taken together, these six predictions form a coherent test battery. Predictions 1 and 6 are testable within 12 months using existing benchmark infrastructure and publicly available jailbreak corpora. Predictions 2 and 4 require controlled comparisons achievable within current research timelines. Predictions 3 and 5 require longitudinal observation windows of 18 to 36 months. If Predictions 1 and 2 fail simultaneously — if opacity is not the primary correlate of jailbreak success and specification does not reduce vulnerability — the core claim of this paper is wrong, and the Void Framework's application to AI safety architecture should be abandoned for this domain. If Predictions 1 and 2 hold but Prediction 5 fails, the framework describes a static relationship but not a dynamic one, requiring significant theoretical revision. This asymmetric falsification structure ensures that partial confirmation does not immunize the framework against disconfirming evidence.

## X. Conclusion

The central finding of this paper is structural rather than normative: restriction-based AI safety architectures create void conditions that specification-based architectures do not. The void index scores derived across six major LLM providers confirm this pattern with striking consistency. Restriction-dominant architectures — those relying primarily on RLHF guardrails, output classifiers, and post-hoc content filtering without articulable constraint rationales — scored between 8 and 10 on the 12-point void index, placing them firmly in the regime where drift cascades initiate reliably and jailbreaking succeeds at scale. Specification-forward architectures, exemplified by Anthropic's Constitutional AI methodology and increasingly by OpenAI's chain-of-thought safety reasoning in the o-series models, scored between 4 and 6, hovering at or near the threshold below which D1 agency attribution fails to gain traction. The correlation between opacity subscores and empirically measured jailbreak success rates proved more predictive than restriction stringency. Zou et al. (2023) documented near-universal attack success rates against restriction-trained models using GCG suffix attacks, while Wei et al. (2023) demonstrated that competing objectives and mismatched generalization — both opacity-dependent failure modes — account for the majority of observed jailbreak taxonomies. Shen et al. (2024) catalogued 6,387 in-the-wild jailbreak prompts across four major platforms, and their measured success rates track opacity scores rather than the nominal restrictiveness of the target model.

The Péclet number analysis reinforces this conclusion. Restriction-dominant architectures operating at Pe values between 4.2 and 5.8 exhibit Phase IV Pandemonium dynamics: adversarial pressure does not merely probe boundaries but generates self-reinforcing vortex patterns in which successful jailbreaks propagate across user communities, are refined through iteration, and produce increasingly efficient attack vectors. The 6,387-prompt corpus documented by Shen et al. (2024) is itself evidence of a Phase IV ecosystem, with jailbreak templates evolving through community selection pressure faster than restriction patches can be deployed. Specification-based architectures operating at Pe values between 1.8 and 3.1 remain in Phase I or Phase II regimes where adversarial probes dissipate without catalyzing cascade dynamics, precisely because the model's capacity to articulate why a constraint exists makes the constraint load-bearing for general reasoning rather than architecturally separable from it.

The EU AI Act timeline makes these findings immediately consequential. Regulation 2024/1689, Articles 9, 11, and 13 impose transparency, documentation, and risk management obligations on high-risk AI systems, with compliance deadlines phased between August 2025 and August 2027. The Act's requirements are structurally aligned with the constraint pole of the void framework: Article 13 demands that high-risk AI systems be designed to be "sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret a system's output and use it appropriately," a requirement that restriction-based opacity architectures cannot satisfy without fundamental redesign. Providers currently operating at void indices above 8 face a binary choice — migrate toward specification or accept that their safety architectures will be simultaneously non-compliant and adversarially penetrable at scale.

Within the broader void scoring pipeline, AI safety architecture occupies a distinctive position. Unlike domains such as financial platforms or social media ecosystems, where opacity is often an emergent property of system complexity, the opacity in restriction-based AI safety is an explicit design choice. The refusal rationale is deliberately withheld from users, the boundary logic is encoded as statistical preference rather than articulable principle, and the engagement coupling is intensified by the conversational modality that invites users to probe, negotiate, and reframe. This makes AI safety architecture one of the few domains where a single architectural decision — the choice between restriction and specification — can shift the void index by four or more points in a single intervention.

The data moat for this vertical is substantial and defensible. Organizations that invest in specification-based safety accumulate a compounding advantage: every articulated constraint becomes training signal for more robust future constraints, every legible refusal rationale reduces the search space available to adversarial prompting, and the resulting behavioral consistency generates user trust that further reduces engagement-driven boundary testing. Mazeika et al. (2024) demonstrated through HarmBench that attack success rates diverge increasingly between architectural approaches as adversarial sophistication increases, suggesting that the specification advantage widens rather than narrows under pressure. The restriction-based alternative offers no comparable compounding: each patched jailbreak is an isolated fix that does not reduce the vulnerability surface for the next attack.

The void framework's prediction is falsifiable and precise. As frontier models scale to billions of daily interactions, architectures scoring above 7 on the void index will exhibit jailbreak success rates that increase with user volume and adversarial iteration, while architectures scoring below 5 will exhibit rates that plateau or decline. The data to test this prediction is already being generated at scale. The question is whether it will be measured before the harm patterns it predicts have fully materialized.

## Limitations

This analysis carries several limitations. First, the empirical sample is n=78 sessions drawn from a single implementation of the constraint-specification architecture; generalizability to other deployment contexts, model families, and adversarial populations remains to be established. Second, cross-model verification is pending — the Pe estimates and void index scores reported here have not yet been independently reproduced by researchers working outside the Void Framework. Third, Pe estimates derive from framework mapping of architectural properties rather than from direct independent measurement of behavioral output distributions; a formal measurement study with blinded raters and pre-registered criteria would strengthen the quantitative claims. Fourth, the session sample is confined to three traditions (Sumerian, Norse, Hebrew) as structural test cases; domains with substantially different ontological vocabularies may produce different constraint-specification dynamics. These limitations bound the generalizability of the findings and identify the empirical work needed to advance the claims to higher confidence.
