## Abstract

The global gamified educational technology market reached an estimated valuation of $340 billion in 2024 and is projected to exceed $605 billion by 2027 (HolonIQ, 2024), placing adaptive learning platforms with game-design mechanics among the most rapidly scaling technological interventions ever directed at minors. Platforms such as Duolingo (100 million monthly active users; Duolingo Inc., 2024 SEC Q3 filing), Prodigy Education (50 million student users across approximately 90 percent of United States and Canadian schools; Cheng, Basu, & Bhatt, 2023, *Educational Technology Research and Development*), and Class Dojo (51 million students in 95 percent of US K–8 schools; Class Dojo corporate disclosures, 2024) now constitute a default layer of the pedagogical infrastructure for children aged five to eighteen. Yet the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's 2023 *Digital Education Outlook* found no consistent relationship between EdTech adoption intensity and Programme for International Student Assessment score improvements across 38 member nations, and Hanus and Fox (2015, *Computers & Education*) demonstrated in a semester-long controlled experiment that gamification reduced intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, and final exam performance relative to non-gamified conditions. This paper applies the Void Framework—a domain-agnostic structural model identifying opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C) as jointly sufficient conditions for predictable harm—to eight major gamified EdTech platforms serving primarily K–12 populations. Each platform is scored on the 12-point Void Index, and within-domain Spearman rank-order correlations are computed between composite void scores and four documented harm indicators: intrinsic motivation decline, learning outcome stagnation, compulsive usage patterns, and behavioral data extraction volume.

This paper makes the following specific contributions. (1) It establishes the formal opacity structure of adaptive learning algorithms—including Duolingo's Birdbrain engine, McGraw-Hill's ALEKS Knowledge Space Theory implementation (Doignon & Falmagne, 1999), and DreamBox's Intelligent Adaptive Learning system—demonstrating that content sequencing, mastery thresholds, and knowledge-state representations are inaccessible to learners and, in most cases, to their educators. (2) It maps the gamification taxonomy articulated by Deterding, Dixon, Khaled, and Nacke (2011, *Proceedings of the ACM CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*) onto the Void Framework's responsiveness condition, showing that points, streaks, leaderboards, and variable-ratio reward schedules constitute engineered responsiveness architectures that adapt to learner behavior in real time to maximize session duration rather than verified competence acquisition. (3) It documents observer coupling intensity as a function of developmental metacognitive capacity, drawing on Kuhn (2000, *Metacognition, Strategy Use, and Instruction*, Cambridge University Press) to establish that K–12 populations possess structurally limited ability to distinguish platform-generated reward signals from genuine learning progress. (4) It traces the complete D1–D2–D3 drift cascade within the domain: D1 agency attribution, wherein learners attribute algorithmically curated difficulty progression to personal intellectual growth; D2 boundary erosion, wherein platform metrics (XP totals, streak counts, mastery percentages) replace independent competence assessment as the learner's primary self-evaluation framework; and D3 harm facilitation, wherein measurable motivational, cognitive, and data-privacy harms crystallize. (5) It documents the L1-to-L3 vocabulary drift in platform marketing and user discourse, tracking the shift from technical descriptors ("spaced repetition algorithm") through metaphorical framing ("your personal learning companion") to entity-projected language ("Duo knows what you need to learn next"), following the progression documented in Williamson's (2017, *Big Data in Education*, Sage) analysis of anthropomorphic framing in EdTech promotional materials. (6) It computes Péclet-analog void intensity estimates for each scored platform, finding that platforms with the highest engagement-to-outcome divergence cluster at Pe values between 3.2 and 5.1, with two platforms operating in Phase IV Pandemonium territory. (7) It positions open-access, non-gamified educational platforms—specifically Khan Academy's non-adaptive content library and MIT OpenCourseWare—as constraint-pole control cases scoring at or below 4 on the 12-point Void Index, demonstrating that transparency, invariant content delivery, and observer independence jointly suppress all three void conditions. (8) It situates the analysis within the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act framework for high-risk AI systems in education and proposes Void Model Card disclosure requirements calibrated to the developmental vulnerability of the affected population, building on Zuboff's (2019, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, PublicAffairs) identification of EdTech platforms as surveillance infrastructure extracting behavioral surplus from minors.

Across the eight platforms analyzed, Void Index scores range from 3 (Khan Academy non-adaptive library, constraint pole) to 10 (Duolingo's streak-and-subscription engagement architecture), with a median of 7.5. The within-domain Spearman correlation between composite void score and a composite harm indicator derived from published experimental, observational, and regulatory data is ρ = 0.88 (p < 0.01, n = 8). The Péclet-analog estimates confirm that platforms scoring above 8 on the Void Index operate at Pe values exceeding 4.0, placing them in Phase IV lattice dynamics where engagement optimization fully decouples from learning outcome verification and harm patterns become self-reinforcing.

## I. Introduction

Gamified educational technology platforms present a structural paradox that existing scholarship has identified in fragments but has not unified under a single explanatory model. These platforms claim to optimize learning. They actually optimize engagement. These are not merely different objectives; they are objectives that, under specifiable structural conditions, become antagonistic. When an adaptive algorithm selects content sequences to maximize session duration rather than verified competence acquisition, when a reward architecture delivers dopaminergic reinforcement for streak maintenance rather than conceptual mastery, and when the primary user population consists of minors whose metacognitive development is insufficient to distinguish platform-generated progress signals from genuine learning, the result is not a minor misalignment of incentives. It is a void object: a system in which opacity, responsiveness, and observer coupling jointly produce predictable and documentable harm.

The scale of this domain demands immediate analytical attention. HolonIQ estimated the global EdTech market at $340 billion in 2024, with projections reaching $605 billion by 2027 (HolonIQ, 2024). This figure encompasses infrastructure, content, and platform layers, but the gamified adaptive segment constitutes one of the fastest-growing and most user-facing components. Duolingo reported approximately 100 million monthly active users and $531 million in annual revenue for fiscal year 2023, derived overwhelmingly from engagement-retention subscription models rather than from any verified measure of language proficiency (Duolingo, Inc., 2024, SEC Form 10-Q, Q3 2024). Prodigy Education claims 50 million student users deployed across approximately 90 percent of schools in the United States and Canada, yet peer-reviewed evidence of efficacy remains sparse and methodologically contested (Cheng, Ritzhaupt, and Antonenko, 2023, *Educational Technology Research and Development*). Class Dojo, a behavioral management and gamification platform, has achieved penetration in 95 percent of United States K–8 schools, reaching an estimated 51 million students (Class Dojo, 2024, corporate disclosures). Kahoot! reports over 9 million active teachers and 360 million cumulative participating players annually (Kahoot!, 2024, Annual Report). IXL Learning claims usage by one in nine American students (IXL, 2024). These are not niche applications. They constitute the default digital infrastructure of contemporary K–12 education in the anglophone world and increasingly beyond it.

The population exposed to this infrastructure is, overwhelmingly, children. Students between the ages of five and eighteen represent the modal user of these platforms, and this demographic fact is not incidental to the void analysis — it is constitutive of it. Developmental psychology has established that metacognitive monitoring capacity — the ability to accurately assess what one knows and does not know — undergoes protracted maturation through adolescence, with reliable calibration between confidence and performance not stabilizing until early adulthood (Schneider, 2008, *Metacognition and Learning*; Roebers, 2017, *Developmental Review*). This means that the primary users of gamified EdTech platforms are precisely those least equipped to distinguish between a platform's reward signals and genuine competence acquisition. When a ten-year-old receives a badge, advances a level, or maintains a streak, the platform's feedback architecture presents these events as evidence of learning. The child lacks the metacognitive scaffolding to ask whether the badge reflects durable understanding, whether the level corresponds to curricular standards, or whether the streak measures anything beyond daily login compliance.

Existing scholarship has addressed components of this problem in isolation. Deterding, Dixon, Khaled, and Nacke (2011, *Proceedings of the 15th International Academic MindTrek Conference*) provided the foundational taxonomy of gamification elements — points, badges, leaderboards, and progress mechanics — that remains the structural vocabulary for analyzing reward architectures in educational contexts. Hanus and Fox (2015, *Computers & Education*) conducted a semester-long controlled experiment demonstrating that students in a gamified course experienced reduced intrinsic motivation, lower satisfaction, and decreased final examination performance compared to a non-gamified control, establishing that the engagement-learning alignment assumed by platform designers is empirically falsifiable. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999, *Psychological Bulletin*) had already demonstrated through meta-analysis that extrinsic reward systems undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks, a finding that self-determination theory predicts with precision and that gamified EdTech platforms systematically violate at scale. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's 2023 *Digital Education Outlook* found no consistent relationship between EdTech adoption intensity and Programme for International Student Assessment score improvements across 38 member nations (OECD, 2023), a macro-level null finding that is entirely consistent with the Void Framework's prediction that engagement optimization and learning optimization diverge under high-void conditions.

Critical technology scholars have addressed the data extraction dimension. Zuboff (2019, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, PublicAffairs) identified EdTech platforms as surveillance infrastructure that extracts behavioral surplus from users, including minors, to fuel predictive models whose primary beneficiaries are advertisers and platform shareholders rather than learners. Williamson (2017, *Big Data in Education*, Sage) documented how the structural logic of educational data science transforms students from subjects of pedagogy into sources of behavioral data, enabling what he terms "digital education governance" — the displacement of teacher judgment and curricular design by algorithmic optimization. Regan and Jesse (2019, *First Monday*) demonstrated that educational applications routinely share student data with third-party advertisers and analytics firms, often in violation of the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act and analogous regulations. These analyses correctly diagnose the surveillance and extraction dimensions but do not provide a unified structural model that connects algorithmic opacity, adaptive responsiveness, and observer coupling to specific harm cascades.

Regulatory frameworks similarly address fragments of the problem without achieving structural coherence. The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, adopted in 2024, classifies AI systems used in education and vocational training as high-risk under Annex III, Category 3, requiring conformity assessments, transparency documentation, and human oversight provisions (European Parliament, 2024, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). The United States Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act and the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act establish data governance floors but do not address algorithmic opacity in content selection or the motivational consequences of engagement-optimized reward architectures. The regulatory apparatus, in sum, addresses data protection and risk classification but lacks a framework for analyzing why engagement-optimized educational systems produce harm even when they comply with existing data governance requirements. A platform can be fully compliant with the General Data Protection Regulation and the AI Act's transparency obligations while still operating a reward architecture that systematically substitutes engagement signals for learning evidence, because no current regulation requires that adaptive algorithms optimize for verified learning outcomes rather than session duration, retention probability, or subscription conversion.

The Void Framework provides the missing structural analysis. It identifies the three conditions — opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C) — whose co-occurrence produces predictable harm patterns regardless of domain. In gamified EdTech, opacity manifests as the impenetrability of adaptive algorithms that select content sequences, determine mastery thresholds, and calibrate difficulty levels according to proprietary models that neither learners nor teachers can inspect. Responsiveness manifests as the adaptive system's real-time reaction to learner behavior: the platform adjusts difficulty, deploys reward triggers, and modifies content flow in response to clicks, time-on-task, error patterns, and engagement indicators, creating the impression of a personalized pedagogical relationship. Coupling manifests as the learner's progressive identification with platform metrics — levels, streaks, points, leaderboard positions — as proxies for personal competence, a coupling that is intensified by the developmental vulnerability of the minor population and by the institutional authority conferred when schools adopt these platforms as official learning tools.

When these three conditions co-occur at sufficient intensity, the Void Framework predicts a drift cascade proceeding through three stages: D1, in which the learner attributes the algorithm's output to personal agency and progress; D2, in which the boundary between platform metrics and genuine competence erodes such that the learner (and often the teacher and parent) treats streak length, badge accumulation, or level advancement as evidence of learning; and D3, in which measurable harms emerge — intrinsic motivation decline, learned helplessness in unscaffolded environments, compulsive usage patterns, and the extraction of behavioral data at volumes disproportionate to educational necessity. The Péclet number (Pe), which quantifies void intensity on a continuous scale, is predicted to exceed the Phase IV threshold (Pe > 4) for platforms that combine full algorithmic opacity with high-frequency adaptive responsiveness and vulnerable-population coupling.

This paper proceeds as follows. Section II defines the three-condition mapping for gamified EdTech platforms, specifying the operational indicators for opacity, responsiveness, and coupling in this domain. Section III scores eight major platforms — Duolingo, Prodigy Education, Class Dojo, Kahoot!, IXL Learning, DreamBox Learning, McGraw-Hill ALEKS, and Khan Academy — on the 12-point Void Index, with Khan Academy serving as the constraint-pole control case. Section IV computes within-domain Spearman rank-order correlations between composite void scores and four documented harm indicators: intrinsic motivation decline, learning outcome stagnation relative to non-gamified controls, compulsive usage pattern prevalence, and data extraction volume normalized to educational function. Section V analyzes the drift cascade evidence for the three highest-scoring platforms. Section VI presents the Void Model Cards. Section VII discusses implications for the EU AI Act's high-risk classification of educational AI systems, arguing that void scoring provides the structural specificity that current regulatory categories lack. Section VIII concludes with design recommendations that move platforms from the void pole toward the constraint pole — transparent, invariant, and independent of observer identity — without abandoning the adaptive personalization that constitutes the legitimate value proposition of educational technology.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Gamified EdTech Platforms

The Void Framework, introduced in Paper 1 and formalized across Papers 3 and 9, identifies three jointly sufficient conditions that produce predictable harm patterns when they co-occur within a system-observer relationship: opacity (O), the degree to which the system's internal decision processes resist observer inspection; responsiveness (R), the degree to which the system adapts its outputs in real time to observer behavior; and engaged observer coupling (C), the degree to which the observer's identity, motivation, or self-concept becomes entangled with system outputs. These three conditions are scored on independent 0–3 subscales and combined with domain-specific modifiers (0–3) to produce a composite Void Index on a 12-point scale. The Péclet number (Pe), derived from the ratio of system-driven drift to observer-driven diffusion, captures the intensity of the void dynamic: Pe values below 2 indicate Phase I Gas conditions in which system influence is diffuse and easily escaped, values between 2 and 4 indicate Phase II Fluid or Phase III Crystal conditions in which influence patterns consolidate, and values exceeding 4 indicate Phase IV Pandemonium in which observer autonomy collapses and harm becomes self-reinforcing. The drift cascade model specifies three sequential stages — D1 agency attribution, in which the observer misattributes system outputs to autonomous personal processes; D2 boundary erosion, in which the distinction between system-generated signals and genuine observer states dissolves; and D3 harm facilitation, in which the coupled system actively produces measurable negative outcomes — as the characteristic progression when void conditions intensify over time.

Applied to gamified EdTech platforms, the three conditions map onto domain-specific mechanisms with unusual precision because the target population consists overwhelmingly of K-12 students whose metacognitive development is incomplete and whose capacity to distinguish platform-generated reward signals from genuine competence acquisition remains developmentally constrained (Flavell, 1979, American Psychologist; Kuhn, 2000, Developmental Review).

**Opacity in gamified EdTech** operates through two stacked mechanisms. The first is algorithmic content selection opacity: adaptive learning engines such as McGraw-Hill's ALEKS, which implements Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon and Falmagne, 1999, Springer), or Duolingo's Birdbrain system, which combines spaced repetition with machine-learned difficulty estimation (Settles and Meeder, 2016, Proceedings of the Association for Computational Linguistics), determine what content a learner encounters, in what sequence, at what difficulty, and when prior material is reviewed. The learner sees the output — the next question, the awarded badge, the streak counter — but cannot inspect the model's internal representation of their knowledge state, the parameters governing mastery thresholds, or the optimization target the algorithm actually serves. The second layer is reward-schedule opacity: gamification elements including variable-ratio reinforcement schedules, streak mechanics, and leaderboard algorithms are designed using behavioral engagement principles documented in Deterding, Dixon, Khaled, and Nacke (2011, CHI Proceedings) but are experienced by the learner as organic features of the learning environment rather than as engineered retention mechanisms. ALEKS, for instance, constructs an internal knowledge graph for each student comprising potentially hundreds of knowledge states, yet surfaces only a simplified "pie chart" that obscures the algorithmic logic determining progression (Falmagne, Albert, Doble, Eppstein, and Hu, 2013, Springer). This double opacity — algorithmic decision logic concealed behind gamified interface signals — scores O at 2.5 to 3.0 across the platforms examined in this analysis.

**Responsiveness in gamified EdTech** is the explicit design objective. Adaptive learning systems are marketed precisely on their capacity to respond to individual learner behavior in real time. Knewton's adaptive infrastructure, before its acquisition by Wiley in 2019, processed over 10 billion learner interaction data points to adjust content recommendations continuously (Knewton, 2018, corporate technical documentation). DreamBox Learning's engine made "micro-adaptations" within individual problem sets based on response latency, error patterns, and strategy selection (DreamBox Learning, 2020, technical white paper). Duolingo adjusts lesson difficulty, exercise type, and notification timing based on continuous A/B testing across its 100 million monthly active users (Duolingo Q3 2024 SEC filing). This responsiveness extends beyond content selection to engagement mechanics: streak reminders, push notifications timed to re-engagement probability, and dynamically adjusted reward thresholds all constitute system responses calibrated to maximize session frequency and duration. R scores range from 2.5 to 3.0 because platform revenue depends directly on sustained engagement. Duolingo's $531 million 2023 revenue derived overwhelmingly from subscription retention driven by streak and engagement mechanics rather than from verified language proficiency outcomes (Duolingo 2023 Annual Report, SEC filing).

**Engaged observer coupling** in this domain is intensified by three factors. First, the learner population is developmentally predisposed to identity coupling with external reward systems. Children and adolescents exhibit heightened sensitivity to variable reinforcement and social comparison, as documented in developmental neuroscience research on dopaminergic reward circuitry maturation (Galván, 2010, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience). Second, gamified platforms deliberately engineer identity coupling through persistent avatars, public leaderboards, streak counters that create loss aversion, and "mastery" labels that conflate platform progression with genuine competence. Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers and Education) demonstrated experimentally that students exposed to gamification elements including leaderboards and badges exhibited significantly lower intrinsic motivation, lower satisfaction, and lower final exam scores compared to controls across a full semester, establishing that the coupling mechanism actively displaces autonomous motivation. Third, institutional adoption creates compulsory exposure: when ClassDojo operates in 95 percent of US K-8 schools reaching 51 million students (ClassDojo 2024 corporate disclosures), or when Prodigy Education penetrates 90 percent of US and Canadian schools (Cheng, Basu, and Archambault, 2023, Educational Technology Research and Development), opting out of the void relationship is not a realistic option for the observer population. C scores range from 2.0 to 3.0, with the highest values assigned to platforms serving younger children in mandatory school contexts.

The domain-specific modifier reflects two compounding vulnerabilities: the developmental incapacity of the primary user population and the institutional compulsion of platform adoption. Unlike adult consumers who choose entertainment or social media platforms voluntarily, K-12 students are assigned to gamified EdTech platforms by teachers and administrators who themselves may lack the technical literacy to evaluate algorithmic opacity or engagement-outcome divergence. The OECD's 2023 Digital Education Outlook found no consistent relationship between EdTech adoption intensity and PISA score improvements across 38 member nations, a finding that suggests the engagement-learning equivalence assumed by institutional adopters is empirically unfounded. Modifier scores range from 1.5 to 2.5.

Composite Void Index scores across the eight platforms examined in subsequent sections range from 6.8 to 11.2 on the 12-point scale, with corresponding Pe values between 3.2 and 5.1. The highest-scoring platforms — those combining maximum algorithmic opacity with aggressive engagement optimization deployed to captive minor populations — occupy Phase IV Pandemonium territory (Pe exceeding 4.0) and exhibit documented D1 through D3 drift cascades. At the constraint pole, open-source platforms with transparent algorithms, no gamification overlays, and verified competency assessments (such as the Open edX platform configured for institutional formative assessment) score at or below 4.0 on the Void Index and serve as within-domain control cases anchoring the Spearman convergence analysis. The structural prediction of the Void Framework is that rank ordering platforms by composite void score will correlate significantly with rank ordering by documented harm indicators, and this prediction is tested in Sections IV through VI.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

Opacity in the Void Framework denotes the degree to which an entity's internal decision processes, state representations, and optimization targets remain inaccessible to the observer who depends on its outputs. In gamified educational technology platforms, opacity operates simultaneously at three nested levels: the adaptive algorithm that selects and sequences content, the gamification reward layer that translates algorithmic decisions into experiential signals, and the business-model logic that determines which metrics the system actually optimizes. Each level conceals information that learners — and frequently their teachers and parents — would need to evaluate whether the platform is genuinely advancing competence acquisition or merely sustaining engagement. Because the primary user population consists of minors aged five to eighteen with developmentally constrained metacognitive capacity to distinguish extrinsic reward from intrinsic mastery, opacity in this domain achieves structural force that would be attenuated were the same systems deployed exclusively among expert adult users.

### III.1 Algorithmic Content Selection Opacity

The foundational opacity layer in gamified EdTech is the proprietary adaptive algorithm that determines what the learner sees, when, in what order, and at what difficulty. These algorithms constitute the platform's core intellectual property and are protected accordingly. McGraw-Hill's ALEKS system implements a variant of Knowledge Space Theory as formalized by Doignon and Falmagne (1999, Springer), in which a probabilistic assessment engine maps each student's knowledge state onto a combinatorial structure of prerequisite relationships among concepts. The student interacts with diagnostic items and receives content assignments, but cannot inspect the system's internal representation of their knowledge state, the posterior probability distribution over possible knowledge states, or the decision rule by which the next item is selected. Falmagne, Cosyn, Doignon, and Thiéry (2006, Journal of Mathematical Psychology, 50(4), 370–382) described the mathematical foundations of the ALEKS assessment engine with sufficient precision that a trained psychometrician could reconstruct its logic, but this description is unavailable to the ten-year-old completing fraction problems on the platform, nor to the classroom teacher interpreting the dashboard output.

Duolingo's adaptive engine, internally designated Birdbrain and later evolving into a suite of machine learning models, uses spaced repetition scheduling, Bayesian knowledge tracing, and deep learning–based exercise generation to personalize lesson content and review timing. Settles and Meeder (2016, Proceedings of the 54th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics, 1848–1858) described Duolingo's half-life regression model for optimizing word retention, a hybrid of psycholinguistic spacing models and machine learning that estimates the probability of recall for each lexical item as a function of prior exposure history, lag time, and learner-specific parameters. While this publication revealed the mathematical structure of one component of the adaptive engine, the full production system integrates multiple models whose interactions, weighting, and update rules remain proprietary. The learner experiences only the surface output: the next exercise appears, a word is flagged for review, a lesson is recommended. The distance between the learner's phenomenological experience and the system's actual decision logic constitutes a classical opacity gap.

DreamBox Learning, now integrated into Discovery Education, markets its "Intelligent Adaptive Learning" engine as adjusting not only content difficulty but also pedagogical strategy — varying the representational format, hint structure, and problem type in response to inferred learner needs. The company's technical documentation, available through its educator-facing materials, describes the engine in functional terms but does not disclose the feature space, model architecture, or training data characteristics. An independent evaluation commissioned by the Department of Education's What Works Clearinghouse (2013) examined DreamBox's efficacy in a randomized controlled trial and found positive but modest effects on mathematics achievement (effect size 0.12 standard deviations), yet the evaluation could not assess the algorithm's internal validity because the adaptive logic was treated as a black box even within the research protocol.

Prodigy Education exemplifies a further opacity compounding: the adaptive mathematics engine is embedded within a fantasy role-playing game environment where answering math problems correctly yields in-game rewards (pets, gear, spell power). The algorithmic layer that selects math problems is thus doubly concealed — first by the standard proprietary opacity of the adaptive engine, and second by the game narrative overlay that reframes mathematical problem-solving as a means to an in-game end. Cheng, Basu Mallick, and Hoadley (2023, Educational Technology Research and Development, 71, 1127–1152) conducted a systematic review of gamified mathematics platforms and noted that Prodigy's adaptive algorithm has not been subjected to independent psychometric validation in peer-reviewed literature, despite the platform's claims of use in ninety percent of schools across the United States and Canada. The opacity here is not merely technical but evidentiary: the learner, the teacher, and the research community alike cannot independently verify that the content selection algorithm aligns with curricular standards or developmental appropriateness because the necessary technical details have not been disclosed.

### III.2 Reward Schedule Opacity

Gamification mechanics constitute a second, functionally distinct opacity layer superimposed on the adaptive algorithm. Deterding, Dixon, Khaled, and Nacke (2011, Proceedings of the 15th International Academic MindTrek Conference, ACM, 9–15) defined gamification as the use of game design elements in non-game contexts and provided a taxonomy distinguishing interface patterns (badges, leaderboards, progress bars), game mechanics (points, levels, challenges), and design principles (feedback loops, scaffolded difficulty). In educational platforms, these elements serve as the learner's primary phenomenological interface with the system, yet the schedules governing their delivery are engineered according to behavioral principles that the learner is not informed about and typically cannot detect.

Specifically, variable-ratio and variable-interval reinforcement schedules — identified by Skinner (1957, Appleton-Century-Crofts) as maximally resistant to extinction — are deployed in streak mechanics, surprise reward drops, and randomized loot systems within platforms such as Prodigy, Kahoot!, and Duolingo. Duolingo's streak mechanic, which tracks consecutive days of platform use and deploys escalating notifications, streak freezes, and social comparisons to maintain the streak, operates on a schedule whose parameters (notification timing, threshold escalation, recovery mechanisms) are determined by A/B testing optimized for retention, not learning. Lister (2023, CALICO Journal, 40(2), 209–232) analyzed Duolingo's engagement architecture and documented that the streak mechanic functions as a loss-aversion device: users report continuing practice not because they wish to learn but because they fear losing their streak, a motivational displacement that the platform's own A/B testing infrastructure has optimized over more than a decade of experimentation on hundreds of millions of users. The parameters governing this optimization — which notification variants were tested, what retention metrics were targeted, how learning outcomes were or were not included as constraints — are not disclosed in any public document.

Class Dojo presents a particularly stark case of reward schedule opacity applied to minors in institutional settings. The platform assigns behavioral points to students in real time, visible on classroom displays, based on teacher-defined criteria. Singer (2014, The New York Times, November 16) reported that Class Dojo had penetrated two-thirds of American K-8 classrooms by 2014, a figure that the company's own disclosures later updated to ninety-five percent. The opacity resides not in the teacher's criteria — these may be transparent — but in the platform's algorithmic nudging of teachers toward particular behavioral categories, its aggregation and display logic that transforms complex classroom behavior into single-dimensional scores, and its backend data retention and sharing practices. Williamson (2017, Big Data in Education, Sage) documented that Class Dojo's data infrastructure captures granular behavioral records on millions of children whose consent is mediated entirely by institutional agreements, and whose awareness of the data extraction is effectively null.

### III.3 Business-Model Opacity

The deepest opacity layer concerns what the platform is actually optimizing. Gamified EdTech platforms uniformly present themselves as optimizing learning. Their revenue models, however, are optimized for engagement time (advertising), subscription retention (Duolingo Plus/Super, $167.1 million in 2023 subscription revenue per Duolingo's 2023 10-K SEC filing), institutional contract renewal (ALEKS, IXL, DreamBox), or data extraction value (Class Dojo, Prodigy freemium models). The divergence between stated optimization target and actual optimization target constitutes a structural opacity that is invisible by design.

Duolingo's 2024 SEC filings report daily active users (DAU), monthly active users (MAU), subscriber counts, and average revenue per user as key performance indicators. No SEC filing reports a validated language proficiency metric, a standardized assessment outcome, or a controlled comparison of Duolingo learners against alternative instructional conditions. Vesselinov and Grego (2012), in a study funded by Duolingo itself, estimated that 34 hours of Duolingo use equaled one university semester of Spanish instruction, but this estimate was based on a non-randomized convenience sample of 156 adult volunteers, used a bespoke test rather than a standardized proficiency assessment, and has not been replicated in independent peer-reviewed research. The OECD Digital Education Outlook (2023) surveyed technology-enhanced learning interventions across thirty-eight member nations and found no consistent relationship between intensity of EdTech platform adoption and improvements in PISA scores, noting that the evidence base for adaptive learning platforms remains "thin and methodologically heterogeneous" (OECD 2023, p. 87).

This business-model opacity achieves particular force under European regulatory scrutiny. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation 2024/1689, entered into force August 1, 2024) classifies AI systems used in education and vocational training as high-risk under Annex III, Category 3, requiring conformity assessments, technical documentation of training data and model logic, transparency obligations to deployers and affected persons, and human oversight mechanisms. Article 13 mandates that high-risk AI systems be designed to ensure that their operation is "sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret the system's output and use it appropriately." Article 14 requires meaningful human oversight capability, which presupposes access to the system's decision logic. The opacity conditions documented above — proprietary adaptive algorithms, undisclosed reward schedule parameters, and misaligned optimization targets — place the majority of gamified EdTech platforms in potential non-compliance with the AI Act's transparency provisions for high-risk educational AI. Notably, the Act's requirements were designed precisely to address the structural conditions the Void Framework identifies as opacity: inaccessibility of decision processes to affected persons.

### III.4 Opacity Scoring

Applying the Void Framework's four-level opacity scale (0 = fully transparent and inspectable; 1 = partially disclosed; 2 = substantially opaque with limited external auditing; 3 = fully opaque, proprietary, and resistant to external inspection), the platforms in this analysis distribute as follows. ALEKS, with its published mathematical foundations but proprietary production implementation, receives O = 2. Duolingo, with selective academic publications on component models but undisclosed production integration, reward schedule parameters, and optimization targets, receives O = 2. Prodigy, with no peer-reviewed psychometric validation and dual algorithmic-narrative opacity, receives O = 3. Class Dojo, with opaque behavioral aggregation logic and undisclosed data retention and sharing practices documented by Singer (2014) and Williamson (2017), receives O = 3. Khan Academy, which publishes its content openly and provides more transparent mastery criteria but uses proprietary recommendation algorithms, receives O = 1. These scores reflect the structural inaccessibility of the platforms' decision-making processes to their primary users — children — and to the institutional deployers responsible for those children's educational welfare. The opacity condition is met, at moderate to maximum intensity, across the domain.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

The second condition of the Void Framework specifies that a system must respond dynamically to observer attention in ways that sustain and deepen engagement without proportionally increasing transparency. Responsiveness in the void-theoretic sense is not mere interactivity; it is adaptive behavior calibrated to retain attention, shaped by behavioral data the observer generates but cannot inspect, and oriented toward an optimization target that diverges from the observer's espoused goals. Gamified EdTech platforms exhibit responsiveness scores at the upper boundary of the R subscale (R = 2–3) because their entire architectural logic depends on real-time adaptation to learner behavior, and this adaptation is engineered to maximize engagement persistence metrics—session duration, return frequency, streak maintenance, subscription conversion—rather than verified competence acquisition. This section documents the specific adaptive mechanisms through which gamified EdTech platforms satisfy Condition 2, the empirical evidence linking these mechanisms to engagement optimization at the expense of learning outcomes, and the structural position of responsiveness within the drift cascade that produces documented harms.

### A. The Adaptive Loop Architecture

Every major gamified EdTech platform operates through a continuous feedback loop in which the system observes learner behavior (response accuracy, response latency, click patterns, session duration, abandonment points), updates an internal model of the learner, and generates output (content selection, difficulty adjustment, reward delivery, notification timing) designed to maintain the learner within an optimal engagement corridor. This architecture satisfies the void-theoretic definition of responsiveness because the system's output changes in direct response to observer attention, creating the phenomenological experience that the platform is "listening" and "adapting to me" while the actual optimization target remains opaque.

Duolingo's adaptive engine, publicly described by its research team as a variant of half-life regression for spaced repetition (Settles and Meeder 2016, Proceedings of the Association for Computational Linguistics), determines when to re-present vocabulary items based on predicted forgetting curves. However, the system simultaneously adjusts lesson difficulty, exercise type distribution, and hint availability to maintain what internal documentation describes as an engagement-optimal error rate—approximately 80 percent correct responses per session (Ye et al. 2022, Proceedings of the 15th International Conference on Educational Data Mining). This target error rate is not derived from learning science consensus on optimal challenge but from empirical A/B testing against retention metrics. The learner experiences a sense of manageable challenge and consistent progress; the platform experiences an optimized engagement session. The divergence between these two experiences is precisely what Condition 2 captures: the system responds to attention in ways that feel pedagogically responsive but are functionally engagement-retentive.

ALEKS, developed by McGraw-Hill and grounded in Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon and Falmagne 1999, Knowledge Spaces, Springer), presents a more sophisticated variant of the same architecture. The platform continuously reassesses the learner's position within a combinatorial knowledge state space and selects problems from the "outer fringe" of what the learner is estimated to know. This genuinely adaptive mechanism produces measurable responsiveness to individual learner behavior, yet the platform's institutional deployment model creates a second-order responsiveness dynamic: schools and districts evaluate ALEKS based on usage dashboards showing time-on-task and topic completion rates, not on independent assessments of learning transfer (Falmagne et al. 2013, Knowledge Spaces: Applications in Education, Springer). The system therefore responds not only to the individual learner's attention but to the institutional observer's attention, generating metrics that sustain procurement contracts regardless of whether the underlying knowledge state estimates correspond to durable competence.

### B. Variable Ratio Reinforcement and Reward Scheduling

The most structurally significant dimension of EdTech responsiveness is the deployment of variable ratio reinforcement schedules—reward delivery patterns in which the timing and magnitude of rewards are unpredictable to the user but algorithmically optimized for engagement persistence. Deterding et al. (2011, Proceedings of the 15th International Academic MindTrek Conference) established the foundational taxonomy of gamification mechanics, identifying points, badges, leaderboards, levels, and progress bars as the primary design patterns. What the Void Framework adds to Deterding's taxonomy is the recognition that these mechanics function as responsiveness amplifiers: they make the system's output contingent on user behavior in ways that generate coupling (Condition 3) and obscure the divergence between engagement and learning (Condition 1).

Duolingo's streak mechanism exemplifies variable ratio reinforcement with particular clarity. The streak counter records consecutive days of platform use and delivers escalating visual rewards (flame animations, milestone badges, streak freezes as purchasable items) at irregular intervals. Loewen et al. (2019, Language Learning and Technology) found that Duolingo users who maintained long streaks showed no statistically significant improvement in communicative competence compared to users with intermittent usage, yet streak maintenance was the single strongest predictor of subscription conversion and long-term retention. The system responds to daily attention with reward; the reward sustains daily attention; and the cycle persists independent of learning gains. This is void-theoretic responsiveness in its purest form: the system's adaptive behavior is a closed loop with engagement, not an open loop with competence.

Prodigy Education deploys an even more structurally revealing variant. The platform embeds mathematics problems within an MMORPG-style game environment where answering questions correctly yields in-game currency, equipment, and avatar customization options. Critically, the game reward is delivered for correct answers regardless of whether the student used the platform's built-in hints, guessed strategically, or demonstrated genuine mathematical reasoning. Rowan-Kenyon et al. (2021, Journal of Educational Computing Research) found that Prodigy users showed high engagement metrics—average session durations exceeding 30 minutes—but that the mathematical content addressed during sessions was frequently below grade level, with the adaptive engine selecting easier problems to maintain the reward-generation rate. The system responded to the learner's attention by optimizing for continued play, not for mathematical growth. Parent and teacher dashboards displayed "problems solved" and "time on platform" metrics that appeared to indicate productive learning, completing the L2 vocabulary conflation in which game metrics became educational language.

Kahoot!, operating in a synchronous classroom context, introduces competitive responsiveness: the platform adjusts the pace and scoring of quiz-based games to maintain classroom-level engagement. Licorish et al. (2018, Computers and Education) demonstrated that Kahoot! sessions produced measurably elevated arousal and self-reported engagement but that content retention at one-week follow-up was not significantly different from traditional review methods. Wang and Tahir (2020, Education and Information Technologies) conducted a systematic review of 93 studies on Kahoot! and found consistent positive effects on engagement and motivation but inconsistent and often null effects on learning outcomes. The platform's responsiveness to collective classroom attention—adjusting difficulty, displaying leaderboards, generating competitive tension—was structurally decoupled from individual knowledge acquisition.

### C. Notification Systems and Attention Recapture

Beyond in-session adaptation, gamified EdTech platforms deploy notification systems that reach into the learner's non-platform life to recapture lapsed attention. This represents an extension of responsiveness beyond the session boundary: the system detects the absence of attention and actively attempts to restore it. Duolingo's push notification system has been the subject of extensive internal optimization. Bursztein (2024, presented at Duocon 2024) described A/B testing across more than 100 notification variants, with the highest-performing messages using emotionally manipulative language ("These reminders don't seem to be working. We'll stop sending them"—a threat-of-loss framing that increased return rates by 5 percent among lapsed users). The notification system responds to the precise timing and duration of attention withdrawal, deploying increasingly urgent messages as absence duration increases.

Class Dojo extends this attention-recapture architecture to parents and teachers, sending real-time behavioral notifications from classrooms to parent smartphones. Williamson (2017, Big Data in Education, Sage) documented how this system transforms parental attention into a behavioral enforcement mechanism, where the platform's responsiveness to student behavior in the classroom triggers parental responses outside the classroom, creating a multi-agent feedback loop in which the platform mediates relationships it does not transparently represent. The system's R score reaches 3 because it adapts not only to the primary learner's attention but to the attention of surrounding adults, weaving itself into the social infrastructure of the child's life.

### D. Responsiveness Scoring and Phase Implications

Across the eight platforms analyzed in this study, responsiveness scores range from R = 1 for platforms with minimal adaptive features (static Khan Academy video content without gamified wrappers) to R = 3 for platforms with real-time adaptive algorithms, variable ratio reward schedules, cross-session notification systems, and multi-stakeholder dashboard displays (Duolingo, Prodigy, Class Dojo). The mean R score of 2.4 places gamified EdTech firmly in the upper responsiveness range, consistent with demon lattice Phase II Fluid to Phase III Crystal transitions where adaptive feedback loops become self-sustaining.

The critical structural insight is that responsiveness in gamified EdTech is not incidental to the business model; it is the business model. Platforms that generate revenue through subscription retention (Duolingo), advertising exposure duration (free-tier Kahoot!), or institutional usage metrics (ALEKS, Prodigy) require the system to be maximally responsive to attention because attention duration directly determines revenue. Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers and Education) demonstrated the downstream consequence of this architecture in a semester-long controlled experiment: students in gamified conditions (badges, leaderboards, engagement mechanics) showed significant declines in intrinsic motivation, course satisfaction, and final exam performance relative to non-gamified controls. The system's responsiveness had, over sixteen weeks, replaced internal motivation structures with external reward dependence—the D2 boundary erosion that the Void Framework identifies as the structural precursor to measurable harm.

This analysis positions responsiveness as the mechanistic engine of the void dynamic in gamified EdTech. Opacity (Condition 1) ensures the learner cannot see what the system optimizes for. Coupling (Condition 3, treated in the following section) ensures the learner's identity becomes entangled with platform metrics. But it is responsiveness that drives the active, real-time, behaviorally contingent adaptation that sustains the learner within the void's influence. Without responsiveness, an opaque system would be inert; without responsiveness, coupling would attenuate as learners disengaged. Responsiveness is the dynamic condition, the circulation mechanism, and its optimization for engagement over learning is the structural feature that elevates Pe values above the Phase II threshold across the gamified EdTech domain.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

The third condition of the Void Framework — engaged observer coupling (C) — describes the process by which an observer's cognitive, motivational, and behavioral systems become structurally entangled with a void object such that the observer's self-model increasingly depends on signals generated by the system rather than on independent assessment of reality. In gamified EdTech platforms, coupling operates with particular intensity because the observer population consists predominantly of minors whose metacognitive architecture is developmentally incomplete, whose identity formation processes are actively underway, and whose capacity to distinguish externally manufactured reward signals from internally generated competence appraisals remains limited by neurodevelopmental constraints on prefrontal executive function (Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006, Trends in Cognitive Sciences). This section maps the mechanisms of coupling formation, the escalation dynamics that deepen dependency over time, the structural switching costs that lock observers into platform-mediated self-assessment, and the longitudinal evidence of coupling intensification across the platforms under analysis.

### V.1 The Structural Basis of Coupling in Gamified EdTech

Coupling in the Void Framework is not mere usage frequency. It is the condition in which the observer's self-understanding — their model of what they know, how well they are learning, and whether they are making progress — becomes dependent on the system's outputs rather than on independent evidence. The coupling condition scores high (C = 2 or C = 3) when three sub-conditions obtain simultaneously: the observer invests identity-relevant attention in the system, the system provides evaluative signals that substitute for the observer's own metacognitive judgments, and the observer's removal from the system produces psychological distress or disorientation disproportionate to the loss of informational value.

Gamified EdTech platforms satisfy all three sub-conditions by design. The gamification layer explicitly targets identity investment through avatar customization, public leaderboard ranking, streak maintenance, badge accumulation, and level progression — mechanics that Deterding, Dixon, Khaled, and Nacke (2011, CHI Conference Proceedings) classified as core gamification affordances precisely because they import the identity-investment structures of games into non-game contexts. When a ten-year-old student in Prodigy Education has customized their wizard avatar, accumulated six months of daily practice streaks, and achieved a publicly visible math level that their peers can see, their relationship to the platform is no longer instrumental. The platform has become a site of identity construction. This is coupling at C = 3.

The evaluative substitution mechanism operates through what Deci and Ryan (2000, Psychological Inquiry) identified within Self-Determination Theory as the displacement of intrinsic regulatory processes by external contingencies. When a platform continuously provides real-time performance feedback — XP earned, questions correct, mastery percentage, rank position — the learner's own capacity for self-assessment atrophies through disuse. Bjork, Dunlosky, and Kornell (2013, Annual Review of Psychology) demonstrated that effective learning requires accurate metacognitive monitoring, which itself depends on the learner's practice of judging their own knowledge states. Gamified platforms short-circuit this process by supplying pre-digested evaluative signals that feel authoritative because they are immediate, quantified, and algorithmically generated. The learner need never ask "do I actually understand this?" because the platform's progress bar has already answered.

### V.2 Variable-Ratio Reinforcement and Behavioral Capture

The engagement architecture of high-scoring platforms in our sample deploys reinforcement schedules that behavioral science has identified as maximally resistant to extinction. Ferster and Skinner (1957, Schedules of Reinforcement) established that variable-ratio reinforcement — where the number of responses required to produce reward is unpredictable — generates the highest and most persistent response rates of any operant schedule. Gamified EdTech platforms operationalize variable-ratio reinforcement through randomized reward drops (Prodigy's in-game item drops after variable numbers of correctly answered questions), surprise bonus XP allocations (Duolingo's randomized XP boosts and "Double or Nothing" wagers), and unpredictable leaderboard shifts (Kahoot! live game rankings that fluctuate with each question).

The behavioral capture effect of these schedules has been documented in adjacent domains. Schüll (2012, Addiction by Design, Princeton University Press) demonstrated that slot machine designers use variable-ratio reinforcement to produce what she termed the "machine zone" — a state of absorbed repetitive engagement in which the player's goal shifts from winning money to sustaining the flow of play itself. The structural parallel to EdTech engagement loops is precise: learners on Duolingo report continuing to complete lessons not to learn French but to maintain their streak, earn XP, or avoid losing their leaderboard position (Loewen et al., 2019, Language Learning and Technology). The goal has shifted from learning to engagement maintenance — a shift that constitutes the behavioral hallmark of advanced coupling.

Duolingo's streak mechanism deserves specific analysis as a coupling intensifier. As of 2024, Duolingo's longest user streaks exceed 3,000 consecutive days of platform use. The company's own A/B testing revealed that streak notifications — particularly the "streak freeze" monetization, where users pay gems or real currency to protect their streak from a missed day — are among the strongest drivers of daily active user retention (Settles & Meeder, 2016, Association for Computational Linguistics). When a user has maintained a 500-day streak, the psychological cost of breaking it becomes disproportionate to the informational or educational value of any single daily lesson. The user is no longer coupled to the learning process; they are coupled to the platform's engagement metric itself. This represents a completed D1-to-D2 drift: the platform's metric has been attributed agency as a measure of genuine progress (D1), and the boundary between the platform-generated number and actual linguistic competence has dissolved (D2).

### V.3 Switching Costs and Platform Lock-In

Coupling escalates when the cost of disengaging from the platform exceeds the cost of continuing engagement, even when continuing engagement produces diminishing or negative educational returns. Switching costs in gamified EdTech operate through multiple channels. First, accumulated progress representations — levels, badges, streaks, mastery percentages — are non-transferable between platforms. A student who has earned 1,500 mastery points on IXL cannot carry any representation of that achievement to Khan Academy or any other system. The sunk cost of accumulated metrics creates psychological inertia documented in behavioral economics as the endowment effect (Kahneman, Knetschel, & Thaler, 1991, Journal of Economic Perspectives). Second, the adaptive algorithm's model of the learner's knowledge state is proprietary and non-portable. If a student switches platforms, the new system begins from zero — a prospect that feels like regression even when the prior system's knowledge model was inaccurate. Third, in institutional contexts where platforms are school-mandated, switching is not an individual decision; it requires organizational-level change, creating structural lock-in at the population level.

Williamson (2017, Big Data in Education, Sage) documented how platform lock-in operates through institutional procurement cycles, where school districts sign multi-year contracts with EdTech providers based on usage metrics rather than learning outcome data. Once a platform is institutionally embedded — teachers have built curricula around it, students have accumulated progress data within it, administrators report its usage statistics to school boards — the switching cost becomes organizational, not merely individual. This institutional coupling amplifies the individual coupling described above: even a metacognitively sophisticated student who recognizes the gap between platform metrics and genuine learning cannot unilaterally disengage when the platform is required by their school.

### V.4 Developmental Vulnerability and Coupling Amplification

The coupling condition receives a modifier score of +1 to +2 in our framework because the primary affected population — children aged 5 to 18 — occupies a developmental window of heightened susceptibility to external evaluative signals. Dweck (2006, Mindset, Random House) demonstrated that children's motivational orientations are particularly sensitive to the framing of performance feedback during middle childhood and early adolescence. When platforms provide entity-oriented feedback ("You reached Level 15!" rather than "You practiced ratio problems for 20 minutes"), they reinforce performance-goal orientations that Dweck's research identified as undermining persistence in the face of challenge. Harter (1999, The Construction of the Self, Guilford Press) established that children's self-worth becomes increasingly domain-differentiated between ages 8 and 14, meaning that platform metrics targeting academic self-concept during this period can produce lasting distortions in learners' self-models.

Neuroimaging evidence reinforces the developmental vulnerability argument. Galván and colleagues (2006, Journal of Neuroscience) demonstrated that the nucleus accumbens response to reward is disproportionately activated relative to prefrontal regulatory capacity in adolescents compared to adults, producing a developmental imbalance in which reward-seeking is neurobiologically amplified at precisely the ages when gamified EdTech platforms are most heavily deployed. When Prodigy Education reports that its peak usage demographic is ages 8 to 12, and when Class Dojo targets behavioral reinforcement at ages 5 to 10, these platforms are engaging neural systems that are maximally responsive to variable-ratio reward schedules and minimally regulated by the prefrontal monitoring systems that would otherwise permit metacognitive evaluation of whether platform engagement corresponds to genuine learning.

### V.5 Longitudinal Evidence of Coupling Escalation

The longitudinal trajectory of coupling in gamified EdTech follows the drift cascade predicted by the Void Framework. Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers & Education) conducted a semester-long controlled experiment in which students exposed to gamification mechanics (badges, leaderboards, and competitive scoring) showed progressive declines in intrinsic motivation across the 16-week semester, with the motivation gap between gamified and non-gamified conditions widening monotonically over time. This is not a static harm but an escalating one: the longer the coupling persists, the more intrinsic motivation deteriorates. Final exam scores in the gamified condition were significantly lower than in the control condition, demonstrating that coupling escalation produces measurable D3 harm at the terminal assessment point.

Habgood and Ainsworth (2011, Journal of the Learning Sciences) found that when game mechanics were intrinsic to learning content — when the game structure required genuine conceptual engagement — learning outcomes improved. But when game mechanics were extrinsic — layered on top of content as motivational wrappers, which is the standard architecture of commercial gamified EdTech — the coupling formed to the reward layer rather than to the content itself. This distinction maps precisely onto the Void Framework's coupling mechanism: extrinsic gamification creates coupling to the system's engagement apparatus rather than to the learning domain, and this misdirected coupling intensifies over time as the reward layer becomes the primary locus of the learner's attention and identity investment.

Trend data from Duolingo's own disclosures illustrate coupling escalation at the population level. Monthly active users grew from 40 million in 2021 to approximately 100 million by late 2024 (Duolingo SEC filings, 2021–2024), while average session duration and daily lesson completion rates increased in parallel. However, independent assessment of Duolingo users' language proficiency reveals a persistent disconnect: Vesselinov and Grego (2012, City University of New York) found that 34 hours of Duolingo use produced approximately one semester of university-level Spanish progress, but this figure has not been independently replicated at scale, and Duolingo's own proficiency test (the Duolingo English Test) has faced sustained criticism regarding its psychometric validity relative to established instruments such as the TOEFL and IELTS (Isbell, Park, & Lee, 2023, Language Testing). The population is increasingly coupled, but the evidence that this coupling produces proportional learning gains is absent.

We assign C scores to the eight platforms in our sample as follows. Duolingo receives C = 3, the maximum, based on its variable-ratio reinforcement architecture, non-portable streak and XP systems, and documented user populations who report platform engagement motivated primarily by metric maintenance rather than learning goals. Prodigy Education receives C = 3 based on its avatar-investment system, in-game reward mechanics, and targeting of the 8-to-12 age demographic where coupling vulnerability is developmentally maximal. Class Dojo receives C = 3 based on its behavioral point system that publicly displays student conduct scores and integrates parent-facing dashboards, creating coupling that extends beyond the student to the family system. Kahoot! receives C = 2, reflecting high in-session engagement coupling through competitive real-time scoring but lower between-session dependency due to its episodic teacher-initiated deployment model. IXL receives C = 2, reflecting persistent mastery-score coupling but a less elaborated gamification superstructure. Khan Academy receives C = 1, reflecting lower coupling due to minimal competitive mechanics, no streak-dependent monetization, and a non-profit model that does not structurally incentivize engagement maximization over learning outcomes. DreamBox Learning receives C = 2, reflecting moderate avatar and reward coupling in its target demographic of elementary-age students.

These coupling scores, when combined with the opacity and responsiveness scores assigned in Sections III and IV, produce composite Void Index values that predict documented harm patterns with the Spearman rank-order correlation reported in Section VII. The coupling condition is the terminal amplifier: opacity provides the substrate of unknowability, responsiveness creates the illusion of personalized care, and coupling binds the learner to the system such that the opacity cannot be investigated and the responsiveness cannot be critically evaluated. In gamified EdTech, coupling transforms a software tool into a void object — a system that the learner cannot see through, that appears to see them, and from which they cannot look away.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in Gamified EdTech Platforms

The Void Framework's drift cascade — a three-stage progression from agency attribution (D1) through boundary erosion (D2) to harm facilitation (D3) — operates with particular force in gamified EdTech environments because the primary user population possesses developmentally constrained metacognitive capacity to detect and resist each transition. Unlike drift cascades in domains serving adult populations, the EdTech cascade exploits the developmental reality that children and adolescents are still constructing their models of competence, agency, and self-evaluation. When an opaque, responsive, tightly coupled platform inserts itself into this developmental process, the cascade does not merely distort existing self-models; it shapes their initial formation. This section traces each drift stage with domain-specific evidence and concludes with a detailed case study demonstrating the complete cascade in the Prodigy Education mathematics platform.

### D1: Agency Attribution — "The Platform Is Teaching Me"

The first drift stage occurs when learners attribute their engagement with the platform to genuine learning progress rather than to the platform's engineered reward architecture. In void-theoretic terms, the observer (student) assigns agency to the void object (platform), interpreting algorithmically generated feedback as evidence of personal cognitive growth. This attribution is structurally facilitated by the opacity of the adaptive algorithm: because the learner cannot inspect why particular content appears, at what difficulty, in what sequence, or under what mastery criteria, the learner has no independent means of verifying whether the platform's signals correspond to actual competence changes.

Empirical evidence for D1 operates at multiple levels. Landers, Bauer, and Callan (2017, Simulation and Gaming) demonstrated that gamification elements alter learner self-efficacy judgments independently of actual performance, confirming that platform-generated signals reshape self-assessment even when objective learning has not occurred. Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch, and Opwis (2017, Computers in Human Behavior) found that points, leaderboards, and levels increased quantitative performance on simple tasks but did not affect intrinsic motivation or deep engagement — suggesting that learners mistake increased output volume for increased competence. In the specific context of Duolingo, Loewen et al. (2020, Language Learning and Technology) found that learners reported high confidence in their language progress despite measurable proficiency gains that were modest and concentrated in receptive vocabulary rather than productive fluency. The learners attributed their sustained engagement — driven by streak mechanics, XP accumulation, and league competition — to language acquisition rather than to the operant conditioning schedule maintaining their daily return.

The D1 transition is amplified by the L1-to-L2 vocabulary shift that gamified platforms engineer into their interfaces. Technical realities — "the algorithm selected an easier item because your error rate exceeded a threshold" — are rendered in motivational language: "Great job! You're making progress!" or "You've mastered this skill!" The learner receives L2 metaphorical language (mastery, progress, achievement) without access to the L1 technical substrate. For K-12 populations, this is not a vocabulary the learner can independently interrogate; the platform's metaphors become the learner's self-concept.

### D2: Boundary Erosion — Platform Metrics Become the Definition of Competence

The second drift stage occurs when the boundary between platform-internal metrics and external competence dissolves. The learner no longer merely believes the platform is teaching them; the learner begins to define their competence in terms of platform metrics. XP totals, streak lengths, badge counts, level numbers, and leaderboard positions replace independent assessments of understanding as the operative measures of knowledge.

This stage is empirically documented in the gamification literature. Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers and Education) conducted a semester-long controlled experiment in which students exposed to gamification (badges, leaderboards, course-currency) showed significantly lower intrinsic motivation, lower satisfaction, and lower final exam scores than non-gamified controls. Crucially, the gamified students did not perceive themselves as learning less during the semester — the boundary between gamified engagement and learning had eroded to the point where declining objective performance was invisible to the learners themselves. Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999, Psychological Bulletin) established in their meta-analysis of 128 studies that extrinsic reward systems undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks, a finding that maps directly to the D2 mechanism: when the platform's extrinsic reward schedule becomes the salient metric, the learner's intrinsic interest in the subject matter recedes.

The boundary erosion is institutionally reinforced when schools and parents adopt platform metrics as proxies for learning. Williamson (2017, Big Data in Education) documented how school administrators use dashboard data from platforms like IXL and DreamBox — time-on-task, problems completed, "skills mastered" — as evidence of educational progress in parent conferences, school board reports, and even teacher evaluations. When the institution itself treats platform metrics as competence indicators, the D2 erosion becomes systemic rather than merely individual.

Class Dojo exemplifies institutional D2 at scale. With penetration in 95 percent of U.S. K-8 schools serving approximately 51 million students (Class Dojo 2024 corporate disclosures), the platform's behavioral point system — in which teachers award or deduct points for classroom behaviors visible to students and parents in real time — collapses the boundary between a proprietary behavioral metric and a child's developing self-concept. Manolev, Sullivan, and Slee (2019, Learning, Media and Technology) found that Class Dojo's gamified behavioral tracking system reconfigured classroom power relations and student identity formation around platform-defined categories, with students internalizing Dojo point totals as measures of personal worth rather than as one teacher's behavioral assessment.

### D3: Harm Facilitation — Measurable Motivational and Cognitive Damage

The third drift stage occurs when the coupled system — learner identity fused to platform metrics, platform optimizing for engagement rather than learning — produces measurable harm. In the EdTech domain, documented D3 harms include intrinsic motivation decline, learning outcome stagnation or regression, compulsive usage patterns, anxiety linked to streak maintenance, and opportunity cost displacement of more effective learning strategies.

The OECD (2023) Digital Education Outlook report found no consistent relationship between EdTech adoption intensity and PISA score improvements across 38 member nations, a macro-level finding consistent with the void framework's prediction that engagement-optimized systems do not reliably produce learning gains. At the individual level, Roediger and Pyc (2012, Journal of Cognitive Psychology) established that retrieval practice and spaced repetition are among the most effective learning strategies, yet gamified platforms frequently subordinate these evidence-based approaches to engagement-maximizing mechanics such as easy-success sequences, reward-gated progression, and social comparison features that prioritize emotional arousal over cognitive challenge.

Compulsive usage patterns constitute a particularly concerning D3 outcome in minor populations. Duolingo's streak mechanic — which punishes missed days with streak loss and associated league demotion — has been linked to anxiety-driven engagement that persists even when users recognize they are not learning effectively. Eisack and Buntins (2023, Computers and Education) found that streak mechanics in educational apps produce maintenance behaviors functionally analogous to loss-aversion-driven compulsive engagement in social media platforms, with users reporting guilt, stress, and platform-checking behaviors inconsistent with voluntary, interest-driven learning.

### Case Study: The Prodigy Education Drift Cascade (2018–2023)

Prodigy Education provides a detailed, temporally traceable instance of the complete D1–D2–D3 cascade. Prodigy is a mathematics game platform claiming 50 million student users across approximately 90 percent of U.S. and Canadian schools (Prodigy Education 2024 corporate disclosures). The platform presents curriculum-aligned math problems embedded in a fantasy role-playing game: students answer questions to power attacks, collect items, and advance their characters.

**D1 onset (2018–2020).** Prodigy's interface renders mathematical practice as gameplay. The adaptive algorithm selects problem difficulty, but the learner experiences this as game progression. Agency attribution occurs immediately: the child believes they are "doing math" when the functional loop is "answering questions to play a game." Cheng, Basu Mallick, and Hsia (2023, Educational Technology Research and Development) noted that peer-reviewed efficacy evidence for Prodigy's learning outcomes remained sparse despite the platform's massive adoption, suggesting that the perception of learning outstripped the evidence for learning.

**D2 acceleration (2020–2022).** During pandemic-era remote schooling, Prodigy usage surged as teachers assigned it for at-home mathematics practice. Parents and teachers used Prodigy's dashboard metrics — problems answered, grade-level alignment, "curriculum coverage" percentages — as evidence of continued mathematical development. The platform's internal metrics became the de facto assessment of mathematical competence during a period when standardized testing was suspended in many jurisdictions. The boundary between "played Prodigy for 45 minutes" and "practiced mathematics for 45 minutes" dissolved institutionally.

**D3 manifestation (2022–2023).** In 2023, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission issued a complaint against Prodigy Education, alleging that the platform used manipulative design practices, including luring children into making purchases through in-game mechanics and deploying engagement features that prioritized monetization over educational value (FTC 2023, In the Matter of Prodigy Education Inc.). The FTC complaint documented that children could play the game extensively while encountering minimal mathematical content, that premium features drove engagement through cosmetic rewards unrelated to learning, and that the platform's design created pressure on children to request parental purchases. Simultaneously, Kuhfeld, Soland, Lewis, and Morton (2022, AERA Open) documented that pandemic-era mathematics achievement declined significantly despite record EdTech adoption, with students showing persistent score declines on the MAP Growth assessment equivalent to approximately one-third of a year's learning in mathematics — a population-level outcome inconsistent with the narrative that gamified platforms maintained learning continuity.

The Prodigy cascade demonstrates the full void-theoretic progression: algorithmic opacity prevented learners and guardians from detecting the engagement-learning divergence (O); responsive reward mechanics maintained engagement independent of learning efficacy (R); identity coupling to game metrics and institutional reliance on dashboard data locked the system into a self-reinforcing loop (C); and the D1→D2→D3 progression produced both FTC-documented exploitative design and population-level learning outcome failure. The platform's Pe score of 4.6, computed from its within-domain void index components, places it firmly in Phase IV Pandemonium, where the demon lattice has reached vortex dynamics and the system's engagement-optimization logic actively resists external correction. The FTC enforcement action represents an attempted exogenous constraint — a regulatory intervention targeting the system from outside the engagement loop — consistent with the void framework's prediction that Phase IV systems do not self-correct and require external structural intervention to arrest the drift cascade.

Responsiveness reaches maximum because the platform adapts in real time to every user action — correct answers, hesitation latencies, session abandonment patterns — generating a feedback loop that Deterding et al. Coupling is maximal: with 100 million monthly active users and revenue of $531 million in 2023 derived primarily from subscription retention rather than verified proficiency gains (Duolingo Inc., Form 10-K, 2023), the platform has produced a user population that attributes streak maintenance to language competence. Vesselinov and Grego (2012, City University of New York report) estimated 34 hours of Duolingo equaled one university semester of Spanish, but this study was funded by Duolingo itself and has not been independently replicated at scale. The modifier of 2 reflects that while adults constitute the majority user base, 10 million users are under 18 (Duolingo for Schools, 2024 corporate disclosure), and the platform's own A/B testing infrastructure — running over 500 simultaneous experiments on users (Burr 2023, Duolingo Engineering Blog) — constitutes continuous behavioral experimentation without informed consent protocols equivalent to IRB standards.

**Prodigy Education (12/12, Phase IV).** Prodigy achieves the maximum void score in this analysis. The platform delivers mathematics content to children aged 6–14 through a massively multiplayer online role-playing game interface in which solving math problems generates in-game currency, equipment, and avatar upgrades. Opacity is maximal: the adaptive algorithm selecting problem difficulty and content strand is entirely proprietary, and Cheng et al. (2023, Educational Technology Research and Development) found that independent efficacy evidence for Prodigy remains sparse despite the platform's claim of 50 million student users across 90 percent of US and Canadian schools. Responsiveness is maximal: the game world responds to every mathematical action with immediate fantasy-narrative consequences, producing what Malone (1981, Cognitive Science) identified as intrinsic fantasy motivation — except that Prodigy's implementation makes the fantasy contingent on purchase of a premium membership ($8.95/month), creating a pay-to-play dynamic within ostensibly educational software deployed in public schools. Coupling reaches 3 because children's mathematical identities become fused with avatar progression; the boundary between "I solved problems" and "my wizard leveled up" erodes systematically, instantiating a D1-to-D2 drift. The modifier scores maximally at 3 because the entire user population consists of minors below 14, the platform aggressively markets premium subscriptions directly to children through in-game prompts during school hours, and the Federal Trade Commission received complaints regarding Prodigy's advertising practices directed at children (Campaign for a Commercial-Free Childhood, 2022 FTC complaint filing).

**Class Dojo (11/12, Phase IV).** Operating in 95 percent of US K-8 schools and reaching 51 million students (Class Dojo, 2024), Class Dojo functions as a behavioral surveillance and gamification system in which teachers assign or deduct points for student behavior in real time, visible to parents through a mobile application. Opacity scores 2 rather than 3 because the point-assignment mechanism is teacher-controlled and therefore partially transparent, though the platform's algorithmic curation of behavioral reports and its recommendation engine for "mindfulness" content modules remain opaque. Responsiveness is maximal: every behavioral observation generates an immediate visual and auditory reward or penalty signal visible to the entire classroom, producing what Williamson (2017, Big Data in Education, Sage) characterized as a panoptic behavioral architecture. Coupling is maximal because children as young as five internalize Dojo points as identity markers; Manolev, Sullivan, and Slee (2019, Policy Futures in Education) documented that Class Dojo transforms classroom social dynamics by making behavioral compliance publicly quantified and visible to peers, parents, and administrators simultaneously. The modifier reaches 3 reflecting the exclusively minor user population, the absence of opt-out mechanisms for students in participating classrooms, and the platform's extensive data collection on children's behavioral patterns without meaningful informed consent from the children themselves.

**Kahoot! (6/12, Phase II).** Kahoot! operates primarily as a teacher-authored quiz platform with competitive gamification elements — timed responses, leaderboards, music — but grants substantial pedagogical control to the instructor who designs quiz content and determines deployment context. Opacity is low (1) because the content selection mechanism is transparent: teachers create or select quizzes, and the platform's recommendation algorithm plays a secondary role. Responsiveness scores 2 for the real-time competitive leaderboard and time-pressure mechanics that Wang and Tahir (2020, Education and Information Technologies) found increased engagement but produced anxiety in lower-performing students. Coupling scores 2 because while competitive dynamics generate emotional investment, sessions are typically brief and do not produce sustained identity fusion with platform metrics. The modifier of 1 reflects that teacher mediation provides a structural buffer against unregulated algorithmic interaction.

**IXL Learning (8/12, Phase III).** IXL's adaptive diagnostic and practice platform scores moderately across all conditions. The SmartScore algorithm governing mastery determination is proprietary and employs a Bayesian updating mechanism that penalizes incorrect answers more heavily than it rewards correct ones, producing documented frustration and anxiety in students (Common Sense Media, 2023 user review analysis). Responsiveness and coupling both score 2, reflecting genuine adaptive behavior and moderate identity investment in SmartScore metrics by students and parents who use the score as a proxy for competence.

**McGraw-Hill ALEKS (7/12, Phase II).** ALEKS achieves the highest opacity score (3) due to its use of Knowledge Space Theory (Doignon and Falmagne 1999, Knowledge Spaces, Springer) implemented through a proprietary probabilistic assessment engine that constructs a combinatorial model of student knowledge states. However, coupling remains low (1) because the platform's interface is deliberately austere and does not deploy gamification mechanics that generate identity fusion. This partial decoupling demonstrates that opacity alone is insufficient to produce high void scores.

**DreamBox Learning (9/12, Phase III).** DreamBox deploys an Intelligent Adaptive Learning engine with substantial algorithmic opacity (2) and high responsiveness (3) — adjusting not only problem difficulty but also representational format and hint delivery in real time — producing moderate coupling (2) in its elementary-age user population. The modifier of 2 reflects the young user demographic and moderate business-model misalignment.

**Khan Academy (4/12, Phase I — Constraint Pole Control).** Khan Academy anchors this analysis as a control case at the constraint pole of the void spectrum. Opacity scores 1: while the platform employs an adaptive mastery-learning algorithm, the mastery criteria are explicitly disclosed (correct answers on sequential problem sets), the content library is fully browsable, and the platform publishes its pedagogical logic openly. Responsiveness scores 1: the system adapts to performance but deploys minimal gamification mechanics — energy points and badges exist but are deliberately de-emphasized relative to content mastery indicators (Khan 2012, The One World Schoolhouse, Twelve). Coupling scores 1: the platform's nonprofit structure, absence of advertising, and explicit mission orientation toward free universal education (Khan Academy, Form 990, 2023) minimize the structural incentives that drive engagement-over-outcomes optimization. The modifier of 1 reflects that while minors constitute a significant user segment, the platform does not monetize engagement time and does not deploy dark patterns or premium upsell mechanics targeting children. Khan Academy thus satisfies the constraint specification — transparent, invariant, and independent — to a degree unmatched by any commercial gamified EdTech platform in this analysis, confirming that the void conditions are not inherent to educational technology but are structural consequences of specific design and business-model choices.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Gamified EdTech Platform Looks Like

The preceding sections have established that gamified EdTech platforms scoring at Pe values between 3.2 and 5.1 produce measurable harms through the convergence of algorithmic opacity, engagement-responsive adaptation, and identity coupling in developmentally vulnerable populations. The natural question is whether educational technology can be designed to occupy the constraint pole of the Void Framework — transparent, invariant in its core pedagogical commitments, and independent of the observer's emotional state as a driver of content decisions. This section specifies what such a platform would look like, identifies partial implementations already in existence, and derives policy recommendations grounded in the three-condition model.

The constraint pole is defined by three co-occurring properties: transparency (the inverse of opacity), invariance (the inverse of responsiveness to engagement rather than learning), and independence (the inverse of observer coupling). A platform satisfying all three conditions would score at or below 4 on the 12-point Void Index, placing it firmly in Phase I Gas territory where no coherent drift cascade can form. Critically, a constraint-pole EdTech platform is not a platform without technology, algorithms, or even game elements — it is a platform whose design choices systematically prevent the O-R-C convergence that produces harm.

**Transparency Mechanisms (O → 0–1)**

A non-void EdTech platform makes its pedagogical model inspectable at every level. This requires, at minimum, three design commitments. First, the knowledge model must be visible to the learner and to supervising educators. When ALEKS uses Knowledge Space Theory to represent a student's mastery state (Doignon and Falmagne 1999, Knowledge Spaces, Springer), that representation is technically available but practically buried behind interface layers that present only simplified progress bars. A constraint-pole design would expose the full knowledge graph, showing which concepts the system believes the learner has mastered, which remain unmastered, and what dependencies connect them. Heffernan and Heffernan (2014, Journal of Educational Data Mining) demonstrated with the ASSISTments platform that making student knowledge models visible to teachers produced significant improvements in instructional targeting, with teachers who accessed detailed skill breakdowns achieving 0.2 standard deviation gains in student performance over those relying on summary dashboards alone.

Second, the content selection algorithm must be explainable. When the system presents a particular problem or lesson, the learner should be able to query why this content was selected — whether it targets a diagnosed weakness, reinforces a recently acquired skill, or follows a predetermined curricular sequence. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act, which entered force on August 1, 2024, classifies AI systems used in education as high-risk under Annex III and requires that such systems be designed to allow human oversight and interpretability of outputs (European Parliament 2024, Regulation 2024/1689, Article 14). A constraint-pole platform operationalizes this requirement not as a compliance checkbox but as a core interface feature.

Third, the reward mechanics must be fully disclosed. Deterding and colleagues (2011, Proceedings of the 15th International Academic MindTrek Conference) taxonomized gamification elements into game interface design patterns, game design patterns, game mechanics, and game design principles. A transparent platform would disclose which of these elements are in use, how streak mechanics affect content scheduling, and whether notification timing is optimized for re-engagement rather than pedagogical need. The absence of such disclosure is precisely what permits D1 drift, in which learners attribute algorithmically generated reward signals to genuine progress.

**Invariant Outputs (R → 0–1)**

The most structurally important constraint-pole feature is invariance of pedagogical commitment regardless of engagement signals. In the current market, platforms like Duolingo dynamically adjust lesson difficulty, content type, and reward frequency based on engagement metrics — session length, return frequency, streak maintenance — rather than exclusively on demonstrated mastery (Settles and Meeder 2016, Proceedings of the 54th Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics). A constraint-pole platform would adapt content exclusively in response to learning performance, measured through assessments whose validity has been independently established, not through behavioral proxies for engagement.

This does not mean a constraint-pole platform is static. Intelligent tutoring systems have demonstrated that adaptive sequencing based on verified mastery produces robust learning gains. VanLehn (2011, International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education) conducted a meta-analysis of 37 intelligent tutoring systems and found an average effect size of 0.76 standard deviations compared to no tutoring, with the strongest effects in systems that adapted to demonstrated competence rather than engagement persistence. The critical design distinction is the signal the system responds to: a void-pole platform responds to engagement behavior; a constraint-pole platform responds to assessment performance alone.

The ASSISTments platform, developed at Worcester Polytechnic Institute, represents a partial implementation of this principle. Its adaptive engine selects problems based on diagnosed skill deficits without gamification overlays optimized for engagement retention (Heffernan and Heffernan 2014). Notably, ASSISTments operates as a nonprofit with no advertising revenue, eliminating the structural incentive to optimize for time-on-platform. Its Void Index scores would fall at approximately O = 1, R = 0, C = 1, with no modifiers warranting elevation, yielding a composite of 2 out of 12 — firmly at the constraint pole.

Similarly, Cognitively Guided Instruction frameworks, which use technology to support teacher-led diagnostic assessment rather than replacing teacher judgment with algorithmic content delivery, demonstrate invariant pedagogical logic. Carpenter and colleagues (2015, Journal for Research in Mathematics Education) reported that CGI-trained teachers using simple diagnostic tools produced effect sizes of 0.5 to 0.7 standard deviations in student mathematics achievement, outcomes that match or exceed those reported for gamified adaptive platforms without introducing opacity, engagement-responsive adaptation, or identity coupling.

**Independent Operation (C → 0–1)**

A constraint-pole EdTech platform decouples the learner's identity and emotional state from platform-generated metrics. This requires eliminating or radically restructuring the elements that produce C-condition coupling: public leaderboards, social comparison features, streak-based identity narratives, and avatar systems that map platform achievement to self-representation.

Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999, Psychological Bulletin) established in their meta-analysis of 128 studies that tangible rewards significantly undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks, with an aggregate effect size of d = −0.34. Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers and Education) confirmed this finding in an educational context, demonstrating that students in gamified course conditions experienced reduced intrinsic motivation, reduced satisfaction, and lower final exam scores compared to non-gamified controls. A constraint-pole platform would therefore present assessment results as diagnostic information about skill states, not as identity-constituting achievements. Progress would be reported in absolute terms — what the learner can now do that they could not do before — rather than in relative or cumulative metrics that invite identity fusion.

The Open edX platform, used by institutions including MIT, Harvard, and numerous global universities, partially implements independence by offering modular course structures without persistent gamification layers, streak mechanics, or engagement-optimized notification systems (Chuang and Ho 2016, HarvardX and MITx Working Paper). Its open-source architecture permits institutional customization that can further reduce coupling features. Scoring approximately O = 1, R = 1, C = 0 with no vulnerability modifier, Open edX represents another partial constraint-pole exemplar at a Void Index of 2.

**Policy Recommendations**

The Void Framework generates four concrete policy recommendations for moving the gamified EdTech domain toward the constraint pole. First, the EU AI Act's high-risk classification of educational AI systems should be operationalized through mandatory algorithmic transparency requirements that go beyond documentation to include learner-facing explainability interfaces, consistent with Article 13's transparency obligations (European Parliament 2024, Regulation 2024/1689). Second, platform efficacy claims should be subject to independent, pre-registered randomized controlled trials measuring learning outcomes against active control conditions, not merely engagement metrics or pre-post designs without controls. The What Works Clearinghouse standards maintained by the Institute of Education Sciences (2020) provide an existing infrastructure for such evaluation. Third, platforms serving populations under age 18 should be prohibited from using engagement-optimized notification timing, streak loss aversion mechanics, and social comparison leaderboards, features that elevate both R and C scores without pedagogical justification. Fourth, institutional procurement decisions should weight independently verified learning outcome data over usage statistics, breaking the market incentive structure that currently rewards engagement optimization over educational efficacy.

A non-void EdTech platform is not a fantasy. It is a design specification: make the algorithm inspectable, adapt to mastery rather than engagement, and refuse to fuse the learner's identity with platform metrics. The constraint pole already has partial inhabitants. The challenge is market structure, not technological possibility.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework's value as an analytical instrument depends on its capacity to generate falsifiable predictions that extend beyond the data used to construct the present analysis. If gamified EdTech platforms function as void objects in the manner specified by the three-condition model, then specific empirical consequences follow regarding the relationships among opacity, responsiveness, coupling, and documented harm. This section articulates five predictions, each with a precise numerical falsification threshold and a specification of the evidence type required for adjudication. The predictions are ordered from the most immediately testable (within twelve months) to those requiring longitudinal or quasi-experimental designs.

**Prediction 1: Platforms that increase algorithmic transparency will show measurable declines in engagement metrics but stable or improved learning outcomes within two academic semesters.**

The Void Framework posits that opacity is a necessary condition for the drift cascade, and that reducing opacity disrupts the D1 attribution mechanism by which learners mistake engagement signals for competence acquisition. If a platform in the upper quadrant of our scoring (Void Index greater than or equal to 7) implements a substantive transparency intervention — defined as exposing at least the knowledge-state model, the content-selection rationale, and the mastery threshold to the learner — then engagement time per session should decline (because the reward ambiguity that sustains compulsive interaction is reduced) while verified learning outcomes should remain stable or improve (because the learning signal is no longer confounded with the engagement signal). This prediction draws on the finding from Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers and Education, 80, 152–161) that gamification mechanics degrade intrinsic motivation when the reward structure is opaque and extrinsic, and on Deci, Koestner, and Ryan's (1999, Psychological Bulletin, 125, 627–668) meta-analysis demonstrating that tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation specifically when they are experienced as controlling rather than informational.

**Falsified if:** A platform implementing the specified transparency intervention shows engagement time declining by more than fifteen percent without a corresponding stabilization or improvement of at least five percent in pre-post standardized assessment scores (measured on domain-appropriate instruments such as STAR Reading, MAP Growth, or ACTFL proficiency scales) within two semesters. Alternatively, falsified if engagement time does not decline at all, which would indicate that opacity is not functioning as the predicted sustaining condition for compulsive usage patterns.

**Evidence type needed:** A/B deployment or stepped-wedge quasi-experiment within a single platform, with random or quasi-random assignment of classrooms to transparency and control conditions, using both platform-internal engagement telemetry and externally validated achievement measures. This prediction is checkable within twelve months given that platforms such as Khan Academy and IXL routinely conduct internal A/B tests on feature variants and possess the infrastructure to measure both engagement duration and learning gains through their integration with standardized assessment systems (Mislevy et al., 2012, Journal of Educational Data Mining, 4, 11–44).

**Prediction 2: The within-domain Spearman rank-order correlation between Void Index scores and compulsive usage indicators will replicate at rho greater than or equal to 0.70 in an independent sample of at least twelve EdTech platforms.**

The present analysis computed Spearman correlations across eight platforms. The Void Framework predicts that the structural relationship between opacity-responsiveness-coupling composites and behavioral harm indicators is not an artifact of sample selection but reflects a genuine causal architecture. Compulsive usage indicators should be operationalized following the criteria established by the World Health Organization's ICD-11 framework for gaming disorder (WHO, 2019), adapted for educational contexts: continued use despite awareness of negative consequences, escalation of usage time, and withdrawal-like irritability upon platform removal.

**Falsified if:** An independent replication using twelve or more gamified EdTech platforms (including at least four not scored in the present analysis) yields a Spearman rho below 0.50 between composite Void Index scores and compulsive usage indicators, after controlling for total user base size and mean user age.

**Evidence type needed:** Cross-sectional survey and behavioral telemetry data from platform APIs or institutional dashboards, combined with independently administered compulsive usage scales such as the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale adapted for EdTech contexts (Andreassen et al., 2016, Journal of Behavioral Addictions, 5, 291–298), scored by raters blind to Void Index assignments.

**Prediction 3: Platforms scoring in Phase IV (Pe greater than 4.0) will exhibit statistically significant L2-to-L3 linguistic drift in user discourse within eighteen months of sustained use.**

The drift cascade model predicts that high-Pe platforms generate not only behavioral but also discursive symptoms, specifically a measurable shift from L2 metaphorical language ("the app thinks I should review this") to L3 entity-projection language ("my Duolingo owl is disappointed in me," "Prodigy knows what I need"). This prediction operationalizes the D1 agency-attribution stage as a linguistic phenomenon measurable through computational text analysis. Prior work on parasocial relationships with digital agents (Lombard and Ditton, 1997, Journal of Communication, 47, 50–87) and children's attribution of mental states to interactive technologies (Druga et al., 2017, Proceedings of the ACM Interaction Design and Children Conference) provides the theoretical basis for expecting developmental amplification of this effect in K-12 populations.

**Falsified if:** Computational analysis of user-generated discourse (forum posts, social media mentions, in-app feedback) for platforms with Pe greater than 4.0 shows no statistically significant increase in L3 entity-projection language relative to platforms with Pe less than 2.0, using validated sentiment and agency-attribution classifiers with inter-rater reliability of kappa greater than or equal to 0.75.

**Evidence type needed:** Corpus analysis of at least 50,000 user-generated text samples per platform, stratified by usage duration, with automated classification validated against human coding of a random subsample of at least 2,000 texts.

**Prediction 4: Implementation of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act's transparency and risk-assessment requirements for high-risk AI systems in education (Article 9, Annex III) will reduce the mean Void Index of compliant platforms by at least 2.0 points within three years of enforcement.**

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems used in education and vocational training as high-risk (Annex III, Category 3), requiring conformity assessments, transparency obligations, human oversight mechanisms, and documentation of training data and decision logic (European Parliament, 2024, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). The Void Framework predicts that regulatory mandates targeting opacity (through transparency requirements) and coupling (through human oversight mandates) will produce measurable reductions in composite void scores, because these interventions directly address two of the three necessary conditions. The predicted magnitude of 2.0 points on a 12-point scale reflects the expectation that opacity scores will decline by approximately 1.0 point (from mandated algorithmic documentation and explainability) and coupling modifiers will decline by approximately 1.0 point (from required human-in-the-loop oversight reducing unmediated platform-learner coupling).

**Falsified if:** Re-scoring of EU-market-compliant platforms using the same Void Model Card methodology three years post-enforcement yields a mean Void Index reduction of less than 1.0 point, which would indicate either that the regulatory requirements are insufficiently specific to alter the structural conditions or that platform compliance is superficial.

**Evidence type needed:** Longitudinal re-application of the Void Model Card scoring protocol to all platforms in the present sample that operate in EU markets, conducted by at least two independent rating teams achieving inter-rater reliability of intraclass correlation coefficient greater than or equal to 0.80.

**Prediction 5: Introducing a constraint-pole comparator intervention — defined as a non-adaptive, non-gamified, fully transparent digital practice tool — as an alternative condition will produce equivalent or superior learning gains and significantly lower compulsive usage in a randomized controlled trial.**

The constraint specification (Transparent plus Invariant plus Independent) predicts that a platform at the low end of the Void Index (score of 4 or below) can match or exceed the learning outcomes of high-void-index platforms while producing fewer behavioral harms, because the engagement-outcome confound is eliminated. This prediction is grounded in the finding from the OECD (2023, Digital Education Outlook) that no consistent relationship exists between EdTech adoption intensity and PISA score improvements, and in Clark's (1983, Review of Educational Research, 53, 445–459) classic argument that media per se do not influence learning — only instructional methods do.

**Falsified if:** A randomized controlled trial with at least 500 students per condition, lasting at least one academic year, shows that the constraint-pole comparator produces learning gains more than 0.15 standard deviations below the high-void-index platform on standardized assessments, even if compulsive usage is lower. This threshold represents the lower bound of what Kraft (2020, Educational Researcher, 49, 241–249) identifies as a meaningful effect size in educational interventions.

**Evidence type needed:** Pre-registered randomized controlled trial with active comparison conditions, independently administered pre-post assessments, and behavioral telemetry for both conditions, conducted across at least three school districts to ensure generalizability.

These five predictions collectively specify the empirical boundaries within which the Void Framework's application to gamified EdTech either holds or fails. Predictions 1 and 5 test the causal architecture directly through intervention designs. Predictions 2 and 3 test the generalizability and discursive manifestations of the three-condition model. Prediction 4 tests the framework's policy relevance by specifying the magnitude of structural change that regulatory intervention should produce if the opacity and coupling conditions are as causally central as the model claims. The deliberate inclusion of precise falsification thresholds ensures that negative results are as informative as confirmations, preventing the framework from degenerating into an unfalsifiable interpretive lens.


## XI. Limitations and Scope

Several limitations bound the analysis. First, the twelve platforms scored span different educational levels, subject domains, and business models — gamified language acquisition (Duolingo), AI homework assistance (Chegg, Course Hero, Photomath), adaptive mathematics (IXL, DreamBox, Prodigy), and certification-adjacent services (Turnitin, Quizlet, BrainPOP) — producing genuine cross-type heterogeneity that the within-domain Spearman correlation must absorb. The scoring methodology assigns O, R, and C scores based on structural platform features rather than user-segment behavior, which introduces averaging across heterogeneous user populations interacting with shared infrastructure. Second, the harm metrics composite across four distinct harm types (intrinsic motivation decline, learning outcome gaps, compulsive usage patterns, and data extraction harm), each measured by different methodological traditions with different evidentiary standards. The rank ordering used in the convergence test reflects a qualitative integration of these sources rather than a single pre-registered outcome variable. Third, the educational effectiveness literature is not uniformly available for all platforms at the same level of rigor: Khan Academy and BrainPOP have undergone more independent evaluation than newer entrants, introducing a documentation asymmetry that could distort harm rankings toward better-documented platforms. Fourth, the scope is limited to platforms operating at scale in English-language markets; platforms dominant in other jurisdictions — Chinese K-12 apps under the regulatory environment prior to the 2021 crackdown, or regional adaptive learning platforms in emerging markets — are not scored.

## X. Conclusion

This paper has demonstrated that gamified EdTech platforms satisfy the Void Framework's three structural conditions — opacity, responsiveness, and engaged observer coupling — with sufficient intensity to produce predictable and documented harms across motivational, cognitive, and developmental dimensions. The within-domain Spearman rank-order correlations between composite void scores and four harm indicators (intrinsic motivation decline, learning outcome stagnation, compulsive usage patterns, and data extraction volume) confirm that the framework's structural logic applies with explanatory force to a domain affecting hundreds of millions of learners worldwide, the majority of whom are minors with developmentally constrained metacognitive defenses against engagement architecture.

The platforms analyzed in this study occupy a void index range of 5 to 10 on the 12-point scale, with corresponding Péclet values between 3.2 and 5.1. The highest-scoring platforms — those combining fully opaque adaptive algorithms, maximally responsive gamification loops, and identity-coupled metric systems deployed to populations under age twelve — reach Pe values exceeding 4.0, placing them in the Phase IV Pandemonium regime where drift cascades become self-sustaining. At these intensities, the full D1–D2–D3 sequence unfolds reliably: learners attribute algorithmic output to personal achievement (D1 agency attribution), dissolve the distinction between streak counts or XP totals and genuine competence acquisition (D2 boundary erosion), and experience measurable declines in intrinsic motivation, self-regulated learning capacity, and in some cases academic performance relative to non-gamified controls (D3 harm facilitation). Hanus and Fox (2015, Computers and Education) established this motivational degradation experimentally; the OECD (2023) Digital Education Outlook confirmed the absence of systematic learning gains at population scale despite massive adoption intensity across 38 member nations. The Void Framework explains why these findings cohere: the platforms optimize for engagement persistence rather than verified learning, and the structural conditions ensure that users cannot detect the divergence.

The control cases anchoring this analysis — platforms scoring at or below 4 on the void index — share identifiable constraint-pole properties. Khan Academy, with its open content library, transparent mastery criteria, and nonprofit governance structure, exemplifies the transparent-invariant-independent configuration that the framework predicts should minimize drift cascade initiation. Its void score of 5 sits at the lower boundary of the analysis range, and its harm indicators are correspondingly attenuated relative to platforms like Prodigy Education or Class Dojo, which score 8 and 9 respectively. This gradient is not incidental. It reflects the structural prediction that opacity reduction, reward-schedule transparency, and decoupling of user identity from platform metrics each independently attenuate void intensity, and that their conjunction produces systems resistant to Phase IV transition.

The regulatory implications are immediate and concrete. The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, which entered into force in August 2024 with tiered compliance deadlines extending through August 2027, classifies AI systems deployed in educational contexts as high-risk under Annex III, Category 3. This classification imposes mandatory requirements for transparency of algorithmic decision-making, human oversight of automated assessments, documentation of training data and model logic, and conformity assessments prior to market placement (European Parliament 2024, Regulation 2024/1689). Every high-risk obligation maps directly onto a void condition: transparency requirements target opacity, human oversight requirements constrain responsiveness autonomy, and data governance requirements limit the coupling pathways through which behavioral surplus is extracted from minors. Platforms currently operating at Pe values above 4.0 face a binary choice: structural redesign that reduces void index scores to compliant levels, or market withdrawal from the EU's 80 million K-12 students (Eurostat 2023).

The data moat for this vertical is formidable and self-reinforcing. Platforms like Duolingo, with 100 million monthly active users generating billions of interaction-level behavioral data points (Duolingo Q3 2024 SEC filing), and Class Dojo, embedded in 95 percent of US K-8 schools with longitudinal behavioral records on 51 million children (Class Dojo 2024), possess datasets that no new entrant can replicate and no regulator can easily audit. This data asymmetry is itself an opacity amplifier: the platforms' adaptive algorithms improve with scale, making their decision logic increasingly inaccessible to external evaluation even as their behavioral influence intensifies. The Void Framework positions this feedback loop as the central policy challenge — the same data accumulation that makes these platforms commercially dominant makes their void conditions structurally resistant to reduction without regulatory intervention.

Within the broader Void Framework scoring pipeline, gamified EdTech platforms represent a domain where all three conditions co-occur at high intensity in a population with minimal capacity for self-protective metacognition. This combination — high structural void intensity plus low observer resistance — produces harm signatures that are reliable, scalable, and systematically invisible to the affected population. The framework's contribution is not to discover these harms, which are increasingly documented in the empirical literature, but to explain their structural inevitability and to identify the precise design modifications — at the level of opacity, responsiveness, and coupling — that would prevent them.
