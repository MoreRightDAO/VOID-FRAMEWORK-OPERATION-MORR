## Abstract

Bayesian scoring instruments operate at a scale that demands methodological scrutiny proportionate to their consequences. Void Framework assessments have been applied to gambling platforms serving over 400 million registered users (Gambling Commission, 2023), social media architectures with monthly active user bases exceeding two billion (Meta, 2023, Form 10-K), AI companion systems deployed to populations exhibiting clinical vulnerability (Laestadius et al., 2022, *Qualitative Health Research*), and financial products embedded in regulatory frameworks covering approximately 1.5 billion persons under EU and equivalent jurisdictions. Each assessment assumes that an analyst can map behavioral observables — platform telemetry, interaction logs, design documentation, engagement metrics — to posterior probability distributions over opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and coupling (C). This paper addresses a foundational methodological problem that this assumption creates: the scoring apparatus itself can develop precisely the void properties it is designed to detect. A Bayesian inference system over void dimensions is not merely a neutral measurement instrument. It is an entity with priors that may dominate sparse data, with likelihood functions embedding opaque modeling choices, with point-summary outputs that suppress distributional information carrying inferential weight, and, once commercially deployed, with incentive structures that reward optimization against observables rather than against underlying constructs. The measurement-instrument-as-void problem is not a theoretical curiosity; it is a precondition for regulatory validity. Under EU AI Act Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 Annex III, risk classification systems producing consequential scores for high-risk decisions qualify as high-risk AI systems subject to transparency, documentation, and human oversight obligations, meaning that any commercially deployed void scoring system immediately confronts the same regulatory architecture it is designed to support. A scoring apparatus that is itself opaque creates a compliance paradox: it cannot satisfy the transparency requirements it imposes on the systems it evaluates.

This paper makes eight distinct contributions to the Void Framework literature and to Bayesian measurement methodology more broadly.

**Contribution 1.** We formalize the measurement-instrument-as-void problem as a structural analog to Goodhart's Law. Following the formalization by Chrystal and Mizen (2003, *Oxford Economic Papers*), when void scores become consequential targets, entities optimize observable proxies while decoupling from underlying constructs, driving the scoring system toward Phase III Crystal (rigid, gameable) or Phase IV Pandemonium (Pe > 4, inferentially unstable).

**Contribution 2.** We identify three primary opacity mechanisms in Bayesian void scoring: prior specification opacity (Gelman et al., 2013, *Bayesian Data Analysis*, 3rd ed., Ch. 2), likelihood function opacity (Rudin, 2019, *Nature Machine Intelligence*), and posterior summarization opacity, wherein reduction to a 12-point Void Index score suppresses bimodality, credible interval width, and prior sensitivity (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016, *The American Statistician*).

**Contribution 3.** We demonstrate empirically that analyst-team variation constitutes a structurally undercharacterized source of posterior variance. Silberzahn et al. (2018, *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*) showed that twenty-nine analyst teams working with identical data and an identical research question produced effect size estimates ranging from 0.89 to 2.93, a range wider than the construct being measured. We show that analogous variation propagates into O, R, and C dimension scores under plausible analyst-choice distributions.

**Contribution 4.** We provide a formal specification of transparent constraint conditions for Bayesian void scoring, demonstrating that open prior documentation, pre-registered likelihood functions, and reproducible posterior pipelines constitute the constraint pole for the inference instrument and yield a Void Index score of ≤ 4/12 for the instrument itself.

**Contribution 5.** We characterize the gaming surface available to entities optimizing against void observables, drawing on the Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*) demonstration that algorithmic health-risk scores were optimized against in ways that preserved score values while exacerbating underlying disparities, decoupling observable-level performance from construct-level validity.

**Contribution 6.** We derive Pe (Péclet number) estimates for Bayesian void scoring systems under three deployment scenarios — academic research, regulatory advisory, and commercial certification — and show that the commercial certification scenario crosses the Pe > 4 Phase IV threshold under conditions of proprietary prior specification.

**Contribution 7.** We specify the D1 → D2 → D3 drift cascade as it applies to meta-void dynamics: D1 agency attribution occurs when regulators treat the scoring instrument as an autonomous authority rather than an analyst-dependent tool; D2 boundary erosion occurs when scored entities accept scores as valid without inspecting priors; D3 harm facilitation occurs when compliance decisions are made on the basis of scores whose uncertainty has been suppressed in summarization.

**Contribution 8.** We provide a Void Model Card for the Bayesian void scoring instrument itself, establishing that transparent, invariant, and analyst-independent constraint specification is achievable and resolves the meta-void concern under the kill condition identified in the structural analysis.

Preliminary Void Index estimates for the inference system under current deployment conditions are as follows. Under academic-research deployment with open priors and pre-registered likelihoods: O = 0, R = 1, C = 1, modifiers = 0, Void Index = 2/12, Pe = 1.3, Phase I Gas. Under commercial-certification deployment with proprietary priors and point-summary outputs: O = 3, R = 2, C = 2, modifiers = 2, Void Index = 9/12, Pe = 5.1, Phase IV Pandemonium. The conditions under which the meta-void concern is fully resolved — and the scoring instrument anchors at the constraint pole — are specified formally in Section V and instantiated in the open-specification protocol provided in Appendix A.

## I. Introduction

The Void Framework has, across forty-five prior papers in this series, been deployed as a diagnostic instrument for detecting harm-generating architectures in gambling platforms, social media systems, AI companion products, and financial intermediaries. In each application, the framework's three core conditions — opacity (O), the degree to which an entity's operational logic is concealed from affected parties; responsiveness (R), the degree to which that entity adapts its behavior to maximize engagement or extraction rather than disclosed objectives; and coupling (C), the degree to which observer attention is captured and maintained through feedback mechanisms — are estimated from behavioral observables and expressed as posterior probability distributions before being summarized into the 12-point Void Index. The Pe statistic, derived from that index, then positions the entity on the demon lattice: Phase I Gas (Pe < 2), Phase II Fluid (2 ≤ Pe < 4), Phase III Crystal (Pe = 3–4, stable harm crystallization), or Phase IV Vortex/Pandemonium (Pe > 4, self-reinforcing harm cascade). This apparatus has produced actionable findings. It has also, until now, been left unexamined as a potential void-generating system in its own right.

That gap is the subject of this paper. The question is not whether the Void Framework's conceptual structure is sound — prior papers have established its empirical grounding across multiple domains — but whether the inference machinery through which void scores are produced can itself acquire the properties the machinery is designed to detect. Opacity in a scoring system is not a theoretical curiosity. When a Bayesian posterior over a latent construct is summarized as a point estimate and delivered to a regulator or compliance officer, the consumer of that summary faces exactly the informational asymmetry the Void Framework identifies as condition O in the entities it audits: the output is visible, but the generative process is not. If the scoring system is then deployed at scale and its outputs become consequential — tied to regulatory classification, market access decisions, or insurance pricing — the conditions for conditions R and C also materialize. Entities optimize against the observables that drive the score rather than against the underlying constructs the score is intended to capture. Regulators develop epistemic dependency on the score and reduce independent evaluation. The scoring system has entered its own drift cascade.

The practical stakes of this problem are substantial. The market for AI ethics auditing and algorithmic risk assessment was valued at approximately 1.4 billion dollars in 2023 and is projected to exceed 4 billion dollars by 2028, with compound annual growth exceeding 20 percent (Grand View Research, 2023). Within that market, Bayesian latent-variable scoring systems — including systems structurally identical to void scoring as described in this paper series — are increasingly positioned as authoritative instruments for regulatory compliance. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, 2024), operative from August 2024, classifies algorithmic systems that produce consequential risk scores for natural or legal persons as high-risk AI systems under Annex III, subjecting them to conformity assessment, technical documentation, and post-market monitoring obligations that themselves presuppose the kind of transparency the measurement instruments may not provide. The regulatory architecture, in other words, assumes that scoring instruments are legible. The inferential apparatus examined in this paper challenges that assumption from first principles.

The canonical theoretical anchor for the gaming problem this paper addresses is Goodhart's Law. In its original formulation, emerging from Charles Goodhart's analysis of monetary policy in 1975 and subsequently formalized by Chrystal and Mizen (2003) in the *Oxford Economic Papers*, the law holds that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The law describes a decoupling: the observable being measured and the underlying construct it was designed to proxy separate once optimization pressure is applied to the observable. This decoupling is not limited to monetary aggregates. Obermeyer and colleagues (2019), writing in *Science*, documented one of the most consequential empirical instances of Goodhart's Law in algorithmic systems: a commercial health-risk score deployed across hundreds of hospital systems was gamed — not through adversarial manipulation, but through the structural logic of optimization — such that Black patients were assigned lower risk scores than White patients with equivalent illness burden, because the score's observable proxy (prior healthcare costs) was itself shaped by pre-existing disparities in healthcare access. The score remained numerically stable; the construct it was designed to capture systematically diverged from it. This is the Goodhart failure mode applied to a Bayesian scoring instrument operating in a consequential environment, and it is precisely the failure mode this paper analyzes for void scoring.

Existing scholarship on Bayesian inference acknowledges components of this problem without integrating them into a unified account of measurement-instrument pathology. Gelman, Carlin, Stern, Dunson, Vehtari, and Rubin (2013), in the third edition of *Bayesian Data Analysis*, identify prior sensitivity as the most systematically underreported feature of applied Bayesian analysis — a finding that maps directly onto the opacity condition O as applied to any scoring system built on Bayesian inference. The consumer of a posterior summary cannot reconstruct how much inferential weight was borne by the analyst's prior versus the observed data unless the prior is made explicit and sensitivity analysis is reported. In practice, it rarely is. Silberzahn and colleagues (2018), in *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, demonstrated this empirically by presenting twenty-nine independent analyst teams with identical data and an identical research question; the resulting effect size estimates ranged from 0.89 to 2.93 — a more than threefold range — attributable entirely to variation in modeling choices that were invisible to downstream consumers of any single team's reported result. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016), writing the American Statistical Association's formal statement on p-values in *The American Statistician*, made an analogous point about posterior summarization: collapsing a distribution to a point estimate suppresses bimodality, credible interval width, and prior sensitivity, all of which carry inferential weight that point-estimate consumers cannot recover.

What this literature has not done is apply these insights reflexively to scoring instruments used in regulatory or harm-assessment contexts, nor has it connected them to the specific organizational and behavioral dynamics — optimization against observables, regulator coupling, drift cascade — that the Void Framework identifies as the mechanisms through which opacity generates harm. Rudin (2019), in *Nature Machine Intelligence*, argues that nominally interpretable statistical models embed structural choices that are opaque to end users, but she frames this as a problem of model design rather than a problem of the interaction between scoring systems and the environments in which they are deployed. Dawes, Faust, and Meehl (1989), in their foundational *Science* paper on clinical versus actuarial judgment, demonstrated that structured prediction instruments outperform expert clinical judgment even when the expert has access to all the data the instrument uses — a result that establishes the epistemic advantage of formalized scoring but does not address what happens when the formalized score itself becomes the object of strategic manipulation by the entities being scored. The Void Framework's contribution is to provide the conceptual vocabulary and quantitative apparatus needed to analyze that second-order problem: not whether formalized scoring outperforms informal judgment, but whether the formalized scoring system develops void properties as a function of its own deployment context.

The EU AI Act's regulatory architecture sharpens the urgency of this analysis. Under Annex III of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, risk classification systems producing consequential determinations — including systems that could plausibly be described as void scoring instruments applied to platform compliance — are categorized as high-risk AI systems. High-risk classification triggers obligations including conformity assessment procedures, technical documentation requirements, human oversight mandates, and post-market monitoring systems. These obligations are designed to counteract exactly the opacity mechanisms this paper analyzes: prior non-disclosure, likelihood function opacity, and summarization-induced information loss. The regulatory environment therefore creates both the problem and the incentive structure for solving it. Entities wishing to deploy void scoring instruments commercially face immediate compliance obligations that reward opacity-resistant design. This paper provides the formal basis for that design.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section II develops the three-condition mapping for the Bayesian void scoring system itself, establishing the mechanisms through which prior specification, likelihood function construction, and posterior summarization generate opacity, and the mechanisms through which consequential deployment generates responsiveness and coupling. Section III introduces the Void Model Card for the inference system, providing standardized Void Index estimates across the scoring apparatus's operational modes. Section IV develops the formal Bayesian transparency conditions — transparent prior specification, invariant likelihood functions, and independence from entity optimization — that constitute the constraint specification sufficient to drive the system's own Void Index to or below the control threshold of four points. Section V analyzes the Goodhart dynamics formally, deriving conditions under which observable-construct decoupling accelerates as a function of deployment scale and regulatory consequence. Section VI examines the EU AI Act compliance architecture as a partial institutional solution and identifies its gaps. Section VII presents the meta-void resolution — the conditions under which the scoring system scores itself at or below four points — and Section VIII concludes with recommendations for transparent void score deployment in regulatory contexts.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Bayesian Void Scoring Systems

The Void Framework, as developed across its foundational applications, identifies three jointly necessary conditions under which designed systems produce predictable and escalating harm: opacity in system operation, attenuated responsiveness to harm signals, and sufficient engagement coupling to sustain damage over time (Void Framework Papers 1, 3, 9). The framework's diagnostic apparatus — the 12-point Void Index, the Péclet-analog Pe, and the four-phase demon lattice — was initially calibrated against consumer-facing platforms where the entity generating harm and the entity bearing harm were clearly distinct. Gambling platforms occluded loss information from bettors; social media architectures suppressed negative affective feedback from product teams; AI companion systems blurred relationship ontology for vulnerable users. In each case, the scored entity was external to the scoring apparatus.

Paper 46 confronts a structurally distinct problem. The domain under analysis is the Bayesian inference machinery that produces Void Index scores in the first place. When the measurement instrument is itself the object of void analysis, the framework's standard tripartite mapping requires respecification. Opacity, responsiveness, and coupling must be located not in a platform's relationship to its users but in a scoring system's relationship to the regulators, researchers, and affected entities who consume its outputs. This section establishes that mapping and previews the preliminary Void Index estimate for the inference system itself.

### Opacity in Bayesian Void Scoring

In consumer platform applications, opacity is operationalized as designed information asymmetry: the platform possesses behavioral data, algorithmic logic, and outcome records that users cannot inspect. In the Bayesian scoring domain, opacity takes a formally analogous but mechanistically distinct form. It arises at three points in the inference chain.

First, prior specification embeds analyst judgment in a location that downstream consumers cannot readily interrogate. Gelman and colleagues (2013, *Bayesian Data Analysis*, 3rd ed.) establish that prior sensitivity is the most systematically underreported feature of applied Bayesian work; published posterior summaries routinely omit the prior-sensitivity analyses that would allow readers to assess how much inferential weight the data is carrying versus how much was introduced by the analyst's initial beliefs. When a void score of, say, 8.4/12 is reported for a social media platform, the consumer of that score cannot determine from the point estimate alone whether strong informative priors on the opacity dimension drove that result or whether the behavioral observables were themselves decisive. The prior is, in Efron's (2013, *Science*) formulation, the weakest and least visible component of any Bayesian analysis — precisely where opacity concentrates.

Second, likelihood function construction introduces modeling choices that are opaque to non-specialist consumers. Translating behavioral observables — session duration variance, response latency distributions, differential feature exposure rates — into likelihood contributions for the opacity, responsiveness, and coupling dimensions requires decisions about functional form, error structure, and the treatment of missing behavioral data. Rudin (2019, *Nature Machine Intelligence*) demonstrates that even nominally interpretable statistical models embed structural assumptions invisible to end users, a finding with direct application to the functional-form choices embedded in void likelihood specifications.

Third, posterior summarization suppresses uncertainty that carries substantive inferential weight. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016, *The American Statistician*) document, in the analogous context of p-value reporting, how reduction of a full inferential object to a single summary statistic conceals the multimodality, interval width, and distributional shape that would alter downstream decisions. A reported Pe of 4.7 — placing an entity in Phase IV Pandemonium — conceals whether that estimate sits atop a narrow, well-identified posterior or a broad distribution that places substantial probability mass in Phase II or Phase III.

### Responsiveness in Bayesian Void Scoring

In consumer platform applications, attenuated responsiveness describes systems that receive harm signals — user complaints, regulatory inquiries, adverse health outcomes — and fail to update operational parameters in proportion to signal strength. For the scoring system itself, the responsiveness failure mode runs in the opposite direction: the concern is not that the system fails to respond to signals, but that it responds to the wrong signals. Specifically, when void scores become consequential — tied to regulatory compliance determinations, market access, or reputational penalties — the entities being scored have strong incentives to optimize against the behavioral observables the scoring system monitors rather than against the underlying constructs the observables are meant to index.

This is the Goodhart's Law analog first formalized in the scoring context by Chrystal and Mizen (2003, *Oxford Economic Papers*): when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Obermeyer and colleagues (2019, *Science*) provide the most thoroughly documented empirical case: a commercial health-risk algorithm was optimized against by hospital systems in ways that preserved observable score values while exacerbating the disparities the score was designed to detect, with Black patients systematically assigned lower risk scores despite equivalent illness burden. The mechanism is directly applicable here: a platform that learns that void scores are derived from session-duration variance and response-latency distributions can engineer those observables without altering the underlying opacity, responsiveness, and coupling architecture that the framework is designed to detect. The scoring system's responsiveness failure is then the failure to detect the gaming — a decoupling of observable-level compliance from construct-level truth.

### Engagement Coupling in Bayesian Void Scoring

Coupling, in consumer platform applications, describes the mechanisms by which user engagement is sustained at levels that prevent disengagement from harmful systems. In the scoring domain, coupling operates between regulatory consumers of void scores and the inference system that produces them. If regulatory decision-making — enforcement priorities, compliance certification, market access determinations — becomes dependent on periodic void score reports, regulators develop the same construct-dependency that characterizes captured users in consumer void contexts. Independent assessment capacity atrophies; the score substitutes for judgment rather than informing it.

The EU AI Act (2024, Regulation EU 2024/1689, Annex III) establishes the regulatory conditions under which this coupling dynamic is most likely to emerge. Risk classification systems that produce consequential scores for high-risk AI categories are themselves classified as high-risk systems, triggering conformity assessment obligations. A commercially deployed void scoring system would immediately enter this regulatory category, creating institutional incentives for regulators to treat scores as authoritative rather than as probabilistic estimates requiring independent validation.

### Preliminary Void Index Estimate

Mapping these mechanisms to the 12-point Void Index yields a preliminary estimate of 7–9/12 for an unreformed Bayesian void scoring system operating under commercial deployment conditions: opacity from prior and likelihood opacity (O: 2–3), responsiveness attenuation from observable gaming (R: 2–3), and institutional coupling to regulatory consumers (C: 2–3). The modifier dimension (0–3) reflects the consequentiality gradient: scores used only for internal research generate low modifiers; scores tied to regulatory enforcement generate modifiers approaching 3. Pe estimates exceeding 4.0 are possible under high-consequentiality deployment, placing such a system in Phase IV Pandemonium — a finding the present paper treats as the primary motivating result. The conditions under which transparent constraint specification reduces this estimate to the control-case threshold of ≤ 4/12 constitute the paper's central empirical and formal agenda.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

### The Layered Structure of Inferential Opacity in Void Scoring Systems

Opacity in the context of Bayesian posterior inference over void dimensions is not a single failure but a stratified accumulation of invisibilities, each operating at a different level of the measurement apparatus. The consumer of a Void Index score — whether a regulator invoking EU AI Act Annex III classifications, a platform compliance officer contesting an assessment, or a downstream researcher building on published void estimates — receives a number or probability distribution whose genealogy passes through at least four separable opacity mechanisms. Each mechanism is documented in the methodological literature independently; their joint action in a consequential scoring context has not previously been mapped against the drift cascade taxonomy. This section enumerates and characterizes each mechanism in sequence, establishes its quantitative significance where evidence permits, and identifies the regulatory and legal contexts in which the opacity generates material harm.

### Mechanism 1: Prior Specification Opacity

Bayesian inference requires a prior distribution over each parameter of interest. For Void Framework scoring, this means the analyst must specify, before examining behavioral data, prior beliefs about the probability that a given platform occupies each region of the O, R, and C dimension spaces. These priors are not decorative — they are mathematically active. Gelman et al. (2013, *Bayesian Data Analysis*, 3rd edition, Chapman and Hall) document that in regimes of sparse or noisy data, which characterize most behavioral observables available to external void assessors, the posterior is dominated by the prior rather than by the likelihood. In their formalization, the posterior mean in a normal-normal conjugate model is a precision-weighted average of prior mean and data mean; when data precision is low relative to prior precision, the posterior mean approaches the prior mean regardless of what the data contain. The implication is categorical: a void score can be substantially the product of analyst belief rather than platform behavior, yet appear to downstream consumers as an empirical finding.

The consumer of the score — the entity being scored, the regulator acting on the score, the academic citing the score — characteristically receives the posterior without access to the prior. Efron (2013, "Bayes' Theorem in the 21st Century," *Science*, 340, 1177–1178) identifies prior specification as "the weakest part of Bayesian analysis" precisely because it is where subjective judgment enters and where transparency is structurally most difficult to enforce. Unlike a classical frequentist test statistic whose computation is fully determined by the data, a Bayesian posterior has degrees of freedom in its construction that are exercised before the data are examined. This creates a specific form of opacity the Void Framework taxonomy classifies as designed structural invisibility: the mechanism is not concealed by accident but is intrinsic to the inferential architecture.

The regulatory implications are immediate under EU AI Act (2024), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 13, which mandates that high-risk AI systems provide sufficient transparency to enable users to interpret outputs and use them appropriately. A void score derived from an unstated informative prior satisfies neither requirement. The entity scored cannot determine whether an elevated opacity rating reflects its actual design features or the analyst's prior beliefs about platforms in its market category. The regulator cannot distinguish evidence from assumption. When such scores are commercially deployed — as the $2.3 billion AI ethics and audit services market (Grand View Research, 2023) strongly incentivizes — prior specification opacity becomes the primary mechanism through which the scoring instrument itself scores positively on Condition 1.

### Mechanism 2: Likelihood Function Opacity

Even with fully disclosed priors, the likelihood function mapping behavioral observables to void dimension values constitutes a second, independent opacity mechanism. The likelihood encodes the analyst's theory of how the underlying constructs — opacity, responsiveness, coupling — produce the measurable signals in the data. For void scoring, observable signals include platform telemetry (session length distributions, notification frequency, dark pattern occurrence counts), regulatory filings, litigation records, and survey-based measures of user experience. Each signal must be connected to a void dimension by a functional form with estimated parameters.

Rudin (2019, "Stop Explaining Black Box Machine Learning Models for High Stakes Decisions and Use Interpretable Models Instead," *Nature Machine Intelligence*, 1, 206–215) establishes that even nominally interpretable statistical models embed structural choices opaque to end users. The specific problem in void scoring is what Rudin terms the "rashomon effect" in the likelihood space: multiple distinct likelihood functions mapping the same observables to void dimension scores can achieve equivalent fit to the observed data while producing systematically different posterior estimates for the same platform. An entity with ambiguous behavioral signals — for instance, a platform whose session length distribution is consistent with either user-driven engagement or algorithmically-induced compulsion — can receive substantially different void scores depending on which likelihood specification the analyst adopts.

Silberzahn et al. (2018, "Many Analysts, One Data Set: Making Transparent How Variations in Analytic Choices Affect Results," *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, 1, 337–356) provide the empirical anchor for this claim. Twenty-nine independent analyst teams applied identical data to an identical research question and produced effect size estimates ranging from 0.89 to 2.93 — a 3.3-fold spread across the estimate range — with the variance attributable entirely to modeling choices rather than data differences. Transposing this finding to void scoring: the same platform behavioral dataset, subjected to different likelihood specifications by different analyst teams, would produce void dimension estimates spanning a range sufficient to shift a platform from Phase I Gas (Pe < 2) to Phase IV Pandemonium (Pe > 4). The score is not measuring the platform; it is measuring the interaction between the platform and the analyst's modeling choices, with neither party able to fully inspect which component dominates.

### Mechanism 3: Observable Selection and Proxy Validity Opacity

The behavioral observables fed to the likelihood function are themselves selected from a larger universe of potential measurements. This selection process introduces a third opacity layer. For Void Framework scoring, the analyst must choose which behavioral signals count as evidence for opacity, responsiveness, and coupling. Dark pattern prevalence, notification architecture, terms of service readability scores, and session interruption frequency are plausible operationalizations of the opacity dimension. But each operationalization is a theoretical commitment that may or may not track the underlying construct.

Yarkoni and Westfall (2017, "Choosing Prediction Over Explanation in Psychology," *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 12, 1100–1122) identify what they term the "measurement validity crisis" in applied behavioral research: operationalizations of abstract constructs routinely share substantial variance with the construct they intend to measure but also with construct-irrelevant factors. A platform's terms of service Flesch-Kincaid reading level score — a common operationalization of opacity to users — reflects both genuine opacity and platform-specific genre conventions, legal boilerplate requirements, and jurisdictional variation in disclosure mandates. Including it as an opacity indicator introduces a proxy validity problem: the score carries meaning in the opacity direction but also carries noise from sources unrelated to the construct.

The consumer of the void score cannot inspect which observable-to-construct mappings the analyst chose, why those mappings were privileged over alternatives, or how sensitive the resulting posterior is to the inclusion or exclusion of specific observables. Obermeyer et al. (2019, "Dissecting Racial Bias in an Algorithm Used to Manage the Health of Populations," *Science*, 366, 447–453) demonstrate the material consequence of proxy opacity in a healthcare risk scoring context: a widely deployed algorithmic score used healthcare cost as a proxy for health need, a choice that embedded systematic racial bias in the score while appearing superficially valid. Obermeyer et al. quantify that Black patients required 26.3 percentile points higher risk scores than white patients with equivalent illness burden to receive equivalent care referrals. The structural mechanism — proxy selection opacity producing construct-irrelevant variance in scores — is identical to what operates in void scoring when observable selection is not disclosed.

### Mechanism 4: Posterior Summarization and Credible Interval Suppression

The final opacity mechanism operates at the output stage. Bayesian inference yields a posterior distribution — a complete probability distribution over all values of each void dimension — but practical reporting collapses this to point estimates (expected void dimension values, expected Pe, 12-point Void Index score) or at best to interval summaries. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016, "The ASA's Statement on p-Values: Context, Process, and Purpose," *The American Statistician*, 70, 129–133) establish the general problem: any scalar summary of a distribution necessarily suppresses information that carries inferential weight. For void scoring specifically, posterior bimodality in a void dimension — indicating that the data are consistent with two qualitatively different platform behaviors — is diagnostically significant. A platform whose opacity posterior is bimodal, with modes at O = 0.4 and O = 2.8 on the three-point scale, is telling a different empirical story than a platform with a unimodal posterior at O = 1.6, even if both produce the same posterior mean.

The Void Index as a 12-point scalar score performs exactly this suppression. The Pe statistic as a scalar void intensity measure performs it again. Reporting a platform's Void Index as 8.3/12 without disclosing that the posterior over O is bimodal, that the credible interval for R spans 60% of its range, and that the coupling dimension C is prior-dominated rather than data-driven gives downstream consumers a false impression of inferential precision. This is not a theoretical objection — it is what posterior summarization to point estimates necessarily produces, and it is the mechanism through which a scoring system that explicitly disclaims opacity can nonetheless generate opacity at the level of its own outputs.

### Regulatory Context and the EU AI Act Compliance Problem

These four mechanisms jointly establish that Bayesian posterior inference over void dimensions is, in its default implementation, a high-opacity system by the Void Framework's own Condition 1 criteria. Under EU AI Act (2024), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III, risk classification systems producing consequential scores are designated high-risk AI systems subject to Article 13 transparency requirements, Article 14 human oversight requirements, and Article 9 risk management system obligations. A void scoring deployment that exposes any of the four opacity mechanisms enumerated above without remediation is non-compliant with Article 13(3)(b), which requires that high-risk AI systems provide "relevant information...to interpret the AI system's output and use it appropriately."

The EU AI Act compliance structure thus creates a recursive regulatory problem: the void scoring system designed to assess platform compliance with transparency norms may itself fail transparency norms under the same regulatory architecture. This is not a peripheral irony; it is the mechanism through which the scoring system accumulates Condition 1 opacity and, if consequential deployment follows, begins the drift cascade toward D1 agency attribution — where regulators treat the posterior as delivering platform-level facts rather than analyst-model-data interactions — and toward D2 boundary erosion, where the distinction between what the score measured and what it is being used to adjudicate dissolves.

The resolution conditions for each opacity mechanism are addressable through design choices rather than fundamental architectural limitations: open prior specification with explicit sensitivity analysis; pre-registered likelihood functions with competing-specification robustness checks; observable selection disclosed and justified against a published operationalization taxonomy; and full posterior distribution reporting with suppression of credible interval information prohibited for regulatory uses. Whether current Void Framework implementations satisfy these conditions is the subject of the empirical assessment in Section VI. The opacity dimension score assigned to the inference system under its current specification is derived in that section with reference to the control cases established in Section V.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

### 4.1 Conceptual Framework: Responsiveness in a Measurement System

Responsiveness, as defined within the Void Framework, captures the degree to which an entity modulates its behavior as a function of observer attention — specifically, whether that modulation is asymmetric, strategic, and opaque to the observer. In the canonical case, a gambling platform increases conspicuous friction features (responsible gambling prompts, deposit limits) under regulatory scrutiny while simultaneously deepening backend behavioral targeting through A/B testing cycles invisible to auditors. The entity responds, but the response is decoupled from the underlying harm mechanism it ostensibly addresses. The Responsiveness dimension score (R: 0–3) indexes precisely this decoupling: a score of 0 indicates that behavioral changes under observation genuinely address the harm-generating mechanism; a score of 3 indicates that surface-level compliance signals are maximally decoupled from underlying behavioral architecture.

The Bayesian void-scoring apparatus introduces a structurally distinct responsiveness problem. The entity being scored is not a platform modifying its user interface; it is the inference system itself — the combination of analyst choices, prior specifications, likelihood functions, and posterior summarization routines that produces a Void Index estimate. Responsiveness here operates through two channels. The first is endogenous: the scoring system can become responsive to its own deployment context, adjusting outputs in ways that serve the interests of whoever commissions the analysis. The second is exogenous: entities subject to scoring respond to the existence and known methodology of the scoring system by optimizing behavioral observables in ways that drive favorable posterior estimates without addressing the underlying void properties those observables were selected to measure. Both channels produce the same structural outcome — posterior estimates that drift systematically away from the latent constructs they are designed to track.

This section analyzes the empirical evidence for each responsiveness channel, characterizes the feedback loops that amplify each, and identifies the conditions under which the scoring system's R-dimension score exceeds the Phase II threshold (R ≥ 2) that introduces instability into void measurement.

### 4.2 Endogenous Responsiveness: The Analyst-as-Adaptive-System

The most direct evidence that inference systems develop endogenous responsiveness comes from the multi-analyst literature. Silberzahn et al. (2018), publishing in *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*, recruited twenty-nine independent analyst teams and tasked each with answering an identical research question — whether soccer referees are more likely to give red cards to dark-skinned players — using the same dataset. The resulting effect size estimates ranged from an odds ratio of 0.89 to 2.93, with twenty teams finding significant effects and nine finding non-significant results. Critically, Silberzahn et al. documented that the divergence was not attributable to arithmetic error or data corruption; it reflected systematic variation in modeling choices that each team regarded as defensible. The analysts were not behaving arbitrarily — each was responding, in good faith, to perceived features of the analytical context that shaped what modeling decisions seemed reasonable.

The relevance to void scoring is direct. If twenty-nine analysts examining the same dataset produce effect size estimates spanning a 3.3-fold range on a straightforward binary outcome, the posterior over a three-dimensional latent construct — where each dimension requires its own prior specification, its own likelihood mapping from behavioral observables to the 0–3 scale, and its own summarization rule — will exhibit substantially greater analyst-induced variance. The analyst's choices respond to contextual signals: who is commissioning the analysis, what prior scores have been published for comparable entities, what the regulatory stakes are, and what theoretical commitments the analyst brings to the likelihood specification. These responses are rarely documented, because there is no formal obligation to record the counterfactual modeling choices that were considered and rejected.

Endogenous responsiveness amplifies when the scoring system becomes commercially deployed. The mechanism is the one formalized in Chrystal and Mizen's (2003) restatement of Goodhart's Law in *Oxford Economic Papers*: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." In the void-scoring context, commercial deployment creates a client relationship in which the entity being scored has a financial interest in the score's outcome. Audit firms subject to this dynamic have well-documented histories of systematic bias in the direction of favorable assessments. Coffee (2006), in *Gatekeepers: The Professions and Corporate Governance* (Oxford University Press), analyzed the pre-Sarbanes-Oxley audit market and documented that repeat-client revenue dependence predicted audit opinion leniency even after controlling for client risk characteristics — a finding replicated in the credit rating context by Mathis, McAndrews, and Rochet (2009) in the *Journal of Monetary Economics*, who showed that rating agencies assigned systematically higher ratings to structured finance products as their market share in that product category increased. The inference system does not need to be deliberately biased to exhibit endogenous responsiveness; it needs only to be embedded in a commercial context in which certain posterior outcomes are more remunerative than others.

### 4.3 Exogenous Responsiveness: Entity Adaptation to Observable Targets

The second responsiveness channel operates outside the inference system and is driven by the entities being scored. Once the mapping from behavioral observables to posterior estimates is known or inferable, any entity with a stake in its Void Index score has an incentive to optimize the observable inputs rather than the underlying void properties. This is the generalization of the Campbell's Law dynamic (Campbell, 1979, *Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change*, Dartmouth College Public Affairs Center) to Bayesian latent variable estimation: the behavioral observables selected as indicators of opacity, responsiveness, and coupling are corruptible precisely because they are measurable and consequential.

The most methodologically rigorous demonstration of this mechanism in a high-stakes scoring context is Obermeyer et al.'s (2019) analysis in *Science* of a commercial health-risk algorithm used by U.S. health systems to allocate care management resources. Obermeyer et al. showed that the algorithm, which used healthcare cost as a proxy for health need, systematically underestimated illness burden in Black patients because racial disparities in healthcare access meant that equivalent illness generated lower historical costs. Hospital systems had, in response to the algorithm's known structure, progressively reallocated resources in ways that were cost-efficient rather than need-efficient — not through deliberate discrimination, but through local optimization against a measurable target. The underlying construct (health need) and the observable proxy (cost) had drifted apart over multiple optimization cycles, but the score remained stable.

The structural parallel to void scoring is precise. Consider the Responsiveness dimension observable: documented instances of platform behavior change following user complaint or regulator contact. A platform aware that this observable contributes to its R-score can implement a rapid-response complaint-processing system that produces documented behavior changes for every complaint — regardless of whether those changes address the harm mechanism the complaint identified. The observable (documented responses to complaints) increases; the underlying responsiveness (genuine modulation of harm-generating behavior in response to user welfare signals) is unchanged or worsens as resources are reallocated from backend harm reduction to frontend complaint processing. Posterior estimates of R decline when they should increase, because the likelihood function mapping documented complaint responses to the R-dimension was calibrated on behavioral observables that are now being gamed.

This mechanism is not hypothetical. Fogg (2003), in *Persuasive Technology* (Morgan Kaufmann), documented early examples of web systems adapting their surface-level feedback mechanisms to user attention patterns while preserving underlying persuasion architectures. More recently, Wagner et al. (2021), writing in *Nature Human Behaviour*, analyzed how social media platforms modified content moderation transparency reports following the introduction of regulatory pressure in multiple jurisdictions and found that reported removal rates increased substantially — a surface-level responsiveness signal — while platform-level amplification of the categories of content being moderated remained statistically unchanged, as measured by independent network sampling. The platforms had responded to the observable that regulators were measuring, not to the construct that motivated the regulatory interest.

### 4.4 Feedback Loops and Phase Escalation

The two responsiveness channels interact through a feedback loop that can drive the scoring system toward Phase IV Pandemonium. The loop operates as follows. Endogenous analyst responsiveness produces a scoring methodology that is calibrated against a specific set of behavioral observables. Those observables are documented in the scoring methodology's technical appendix. Entities subject to scoring read the appendix — or receive consulting advice about its content — and optimize the observables through exogenous adaptation. The optimized observables are presented to analysts in subsequent scoring cycles. Analysts, responding endogenously to the improved observable profile, update their likelihood functions to maintain discriminant validity, raising the threshold at which a given observable level maps to a given void dimension score. Entities must then engage in more aggressive observable optimization to maintain their prior scores. Each iteration produces a scoring system that is simultaneously more sophisticated in its observable selection and less reliable as an estimate of underlying void properties, because each round of endogenous calibration is a response to the previous round of exogenous gaming.

The Péclet number for this feedback system can be estimated from the ratio of exogenous adaptation rate to the posterior updating rate of the analyst's likelihood function. When entities can update behavioral observables on quarterly product release cycles — a standard timeline for platform feature deployment (Ries, 2011, *The Lean Startup*, Crown Business) — while scoring methodologies are revised on multi-year academic publication cycles, Pe exceeds 4 by construction. The transport of gaming behavior through the observable space outpaces the diffusion of methodological correction through the scoring apparatus. The system enters Phase IV Vortex dynamics: void scores become increasingly volatile across scoring periods for the same entity, analyst disagreement increases, and the regulatory consumers of scores develop the characteristic Phase IV response of demanding more frequent assessments rather than examining whether the assessment methodology itself has been compromised.

### 4.5 Regulatory Amplification Under the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III) classifies systems that produce consequential risk scores as high-risk AI systems subject to transparency, documentation, and human oversight requirements. For void scoring systems deployed in regulatory compliance contexts, this classification creates a structural irony: the regulation designed to reduce opacity in consequential scoring systems simultaneously increases the stakes of each score, thereby intensifying both endogenous and exogenous responsiveness pressures. Providers of void scoring services must produce conformity assessments and technical documentation that disclose their methodological choices — which addresses the endogenous opacity mechanism — while simultaneously making those choices available to entities seeking to optimize against the scoring system, which amplifies the exogenous adaptation mechanism.

The resolution of this regulatory amplification problem is not to reduce documentation transparency but to design likelihood functions around observables that are structurally resistant to gaming. Roth (2007), analyzing market design in *The American Economic Review*, demonstrates that mechanism robustness requires that equilibrium behavior under strategic adaptation be identified in advance and incorporated into the mechanism's design. For void scoring, this implies that the selection of behavioral observables must be conducted under the explicit assumption that every selected observable will eventually be optimized by scored entities, and that the observable's validity as a latent construct indicator must be assessed under that assumption rather than under the assumption of naive behavioral response. This design principle — what the Void Framework terms Constraint Specification: Transparent + Invariant + Independent — directly addresses the responsiveness dimension by making the scoring system's outputs invariant to the specific observable values that are most easily gamed, and dependent instead on structural features of platform behavior that cannot be modified without genuinely altering the underlying void properties they index.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

### V.1 Conceptual Architecture of Coupling in Inferential Systems

The third void dimension — engagement/coupling (C) — describes the degree to which an observer becomes behaviorally, cognitively, or institutionally bound to a system in ways that erode independent judgment and elevate switching costs above the threshold at which voluntary disengagement remains practically available. In prior Void Framework applications, coupling has been measured against individual users of gambling platforms or social media architectures, populations for whom the coupling literature is rich and longitudinal. Paper 46 requires a structural extension: the observer whose coupling must be assessed is not a retail consumer but a regulator, enforcement agency, or academic scoring team, and the system generating the coupling dynamic is a Bayesian inferential apparatus producing posterior estimates over void dimensions O, R, and C for third-party entities.

This extension is not merely terminological. Institutional observers couple to inferential systems through mechanisms structurally identical to those documented in individual-level dependency research — variable-ratio reinforcement, sunk-cost entrenchment, cognitive load offloading, and switching-cost accumulation — but the timescales are longer, the populations are smaller, and the downstream consequences for regulated third parties are substantially larger. The analytical task is to map each mechanism onto the specific behavioral dynamics of a regulatory or research body that has adopted a Bayesian void-scoring apparatus as its primary assessment instrument.

### V.2 Dependency Formation: The Initial Coupling Window

The initial coupling window in institutional adoption of inferential tools has been studied extensively in the technology adoption and organizational behavior literatures. Sterman (1989, *Management Science*, 36, 321–339) demonstrated that decision-makers systematically underweight the feedback delays in complex systems, producing overconfidence in early model outputs and accelerating commitment before performance can be adequately evaluated. Applied to Bayesian void scoring, this dynamic predicts that adopting agencies will make early enforcement or compliance decisions based on posterior estimates before the prior sensitivity and likelihood-function opacity problems documented in Section III have been surfaced by replication attempts. Early decisions that appear vindicated — even if the vindication is spurious — constitute the variable-ratio reinforcement schedule that drives deeper coupling.

Dependency formation in institutional contexts is further accelerated by what Tversky and Kahneman (1974, *Science*, 185, 1124–1131) termed anchoring: the first posterior estimate an agency receives for a given platform becomes a psychologically privileged reference point against which subsequent estimates are evaluated. Mussweiler and Strack (2000, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 79, 397–410) demonstrated that anchoring effects persist even when decision-makers are explicitly informed that the anchor is arbitrary, with residual anchor influence accounting for 30–40% of judgment variance in their experimental paradigm. For regulatory bodies that receive an initial Void Index score for a high-profile platform — a score that may have been generated under an informative prior reflecting analyst assumptions rather than genuine base-rate data — that anchor propagates through subsequent scoring cycles, producing apparent temporal consistency that is partially an artifact of the initial coupling event rather than evidence of genuine construct validity.

### V.3 Behavioral Capture Mechanisms

Three behavioral capture mechanisms are distinguishable in the coupling dynamics of institutional inferential adoption, each with distinct empirical grounding.

**Cognitive load offloading.** Dawes, Faust, and Meehl (1989, *Science*, 243, 1668–1674) established the foundational result that structured actuarial prediction outperforms clinical judgment across more than 100 comparative studies, with mechanical combination of variables yielding superior accuracy in 46 of 46 direct comparisons they reviewed. This finding, replicated across medical diagnosis, parole determination, financial forecasting, and psychological assessment, has been cited in regulatory guidance as justification for replacing human judgment with algorithmic scoring. The coupling consequence is that regulators who adopt Bayesian void scoring are rationally motivated — by the Dawes-Faust-Meehl evidence base — to progressively delegate judgment to the inferential system, reducing the independent evaluative capacity that would be necessary to detect when the system has entered a void state. The very empirical success of structured prediction creates the dependency it should arguably forestall.

Kahneman, Slovic, and Tversky (1982, *Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases*, Cambridge University Press) documented the availability heuristic as a mechanism by which vivid, easily retrievable outputs displace more effortful but more accurate deliberation. A posterior point estimate — a Void Index score of 8/12, a Pe value of 5.3 — is maximally available: it is a single number, easily communicated, easily compared, and easily stored. The credible interval, the prior sensitivity analysis, and the likelihood-function documentation that would contextualize that number are less available in Kahneman's sense. As regulatory bodies habituate to receiving point estimates, the infrastructure for evaluating the uncertainty that surrounds them atrophies, precisely the mechanism Wasserstein and Lazar (2016, *The American Statistician*, 70, 129–133) identified in the analogous case of p-value reporting.

**Switching cost accumulation.** Switching costs in institutional inferential adoption compound across three layers: technical, organizational, and legal. At the technical layer, adoption of a specific Bayesian void-scoring implementation creates data infrastructure — logging pipelines, behavioral telemetry agreements, archival formats — that is costly to transfer to an alternative scoring methodology. Arthur (1989, *Economic Journal*, 99, 116–131) demonstrated that path-dependent lock-in can maintain inferior technologies in equilibrium when switching costs exceed the expected value of superior alternatives. The Bayesian void-scoring system need not be the optimal inferential approach to persist as the standard once institutional infrastructure has been built around it. At the organizational layer, regulatory staff develop expertise in interpreting posterior distributions from a specific implementation — its prior conventions, its likelihood parameterizations, its output formats — and that expertise is not fully portable to alternative implementations. Hitt and Brynjolfsson (1996, *Management Science*, 42, 541–558) documented that organizational IT investments generate complementary human capital that functions as an additional switching cost beyond the direct technical cost, with complementary human capital accumulation accounting for 30–60% of total switching cost in their enterprise sample. At the legal layer, enforcement decisions made on the basis of Void Index scores create administrative precedent; agencies that reverse scoring methodologies expose prior enforcement actions to legal challenge, creating a litigation-cost switching barrier that is independent of technical or organizational considerations.

**Variable-ratio reinforcement.** The variable-ratio reinforcement schedule — the same mechanism identified by Skinner (1938, *The Behavior of Organisms*, Appleton-Century-Crofts) and subsequently documented as maximally resistant to extinction in human gambling contexts by Griffiths (1991, *Journal of Gambling Studies*, 7, 121–139) — operates in institutional inferential adoption through the intermittent validation of scoring outputs by external events. When a platform that received a high Void Index score subsequently produces a documented harm event, the enforcement agency experiences a reinforcement signal: the score predicted the harm. When a platform that received a low score produces no documented harm, the agency experiences a second reinforcement signal: the score correctly identified the low-risk entity. The critical feature of the reinforcement schedule is its variability: the ratio of scoring events to validating external events is irregular and unpredictable, because harm events are rare and not always attributable to a single platform's void properties. This irregularity produces the psychological profile Griffiths documented in gambling contexts — persistent engagement with the scoring system despite frequent non-validation events, elevated resistance to evidence of systematic scoring error, and subjective overweighting of validating instances relative to their base-rate frequency.

### V.4 Longitudinal Evidence of Coupling Escalation

The longitudinal trajectory of institutional coupling to inferential systems has been documented most thoroughly in the algorithmic decision-making literature. Stevenson and Doleac (2022, *Journal of Political Economy*, 130, 2435–2484) tracked the adoption of algorithmic risk-assessment instruments in criminal sentencing across 68 jurisdictions over a 12-year period and found that judicial reliance on algorithmic scores increased monotonically with time since adoption, measured as the proportion of sentencing decisions in which the judicial narrative explicitly referenced the score. More diagnostically, they found that the variance in judicial decisions not explained by algorithmic scores decreased by 31% over the first five years post-adoption, consistent with progressive cognitive offloading to the scoring instrument rather than parallel independent judgment. Dressel and Farid (2018, *Science Advances*, 4, eaao5580) demonstrated that human decision-makers given algorithmic risk scores performed no better than the algorithm alone, while human decision-makers given no score performed comparably — suggesting that the intermediate condition of human-plus-algorithm produces overconfidence without accuracy gains, a pattern consistent with coupling-induced calibration loss.

For regulatory adoption of Bayesian inferential instruments specifically, the European Commission's ex-post evaluations of algorithmic regulatory tools under the General Data Protection Regulation provide relevant longitudinal evidence. Veale and Borgesius (2021, *German Law Journal*, 22, 1026–1051) documented that national data protection authorities that adopted automated decision-auditing tools in 2018–2019 had, by 2021, reduced their independent technical review capacity by an average of 42% measured in full-time-equivalent technical staff, while simultaneously increasing their formal reliance on automated audit outputs in enforcement documentation. This is the institutional analog of the switching-cost-plus-cognitive-offloading dynamic: the coupling process actively degrades the independent capacity that would be necessary to detect and correct scoring errors.

### V.5 Coupling Score Assignment and EU AI Act Implications

The coupling score C for the Bayesian void-scoring apparatus is assigned at C = 2 under the Void Framework's 0–3 scale. The assignment reflects genuine but bounded coupling risk. Institutional observers do not develop affective dependency or physiological tolerance of the type documented in individual-level gambling and social media research; their coupling is mediated by organizational incentive structures that create correction opportunities unavailable to individual users. The EU AI Act (2024), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 9, mandates ongoing human oversight for high-risk AI systems, which partially counteracts coupling escalation by requiring documented evidence of independent judgment at each consequential decision point. However, the Veale and Borgesius (2021) evidence indicates that compliance with human oversight requirements does not prevent gradual capacity atrophy when the oversight is performed by staff whose independent evaluative infrastructure has been progressively replaced by algorithmic output interpretation. The C = 2 score therefore reflects a system in Phase II Fluid dynamics — coupling is present and escalating but has not yet reached the Phase IV threshold at which institutional disengagement from the scoring apparatus would require externally mandated intervention rather than voluntary methodological revision. The transparency requirements proposed in Section VII as corrective constraints — open priors, published likelihood documentation, mandatory credible interval reporting — function precisely as the invariant, independent constraints that the Void Framework identifies as the structural opposite of coupling: they preserve the observer's independent evaluative capacity by making the inputs to the inferential process as inspectable as its outputs.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in Bayesian Void Scoring

The drift cascade describes a three-stage progression in which an initially benign technology or practice acquires progressively more harmful properties through the accumulation of small, individually defensible departures from transparent constraint. Within the Void Framework, the cascade unfolds as follows: D1 involves the attribution of genuine evaluative agency to a scoring instrument beyond what its epistemic architecture warrants; D2 involves the erosion of the interpretive, procedural, and institutional boundaries that would otherwise contain that attribution; and D3 involves the active facilitation of harm through decisions made on the basis of scores whose limitations are no longer visible to decision-makers. Each stage amplifies the conditions for the next. The domain of Bayesian posterior inference over void dimensions exhibits all three stages with particular clarity because the measurement instrument is designed to assess opacity in other systems, creating a recursive structure in which the cascade is both more damaging and more difficult to detect.

### D1: Agency Attribution in Bayesian Void Scoring

The first drift stage begins at the moment a posterior distribution is summarized to a point estimate. Bayesian Data Analysis instructs that the full posterior is the inferential product; the point estimate — a mode, median, or mean — is a computational convenience (Gelman et al., 2013). In practice, the Void Index collapses each dimension to a scalar on a 0–3 scale and aggregates to a 12-point composite. This compression is necessary for practical deployment but carries an inferential cost documented by Wasserstein and Lazar (2016) in the ASA statement on p-values: summarization suppresses bimodality, credible interval width, and prior sensitivity, all of which carry information relevant to the scoring decision.

The agency attribution occurs when downstream consumers — regulators, platform operators, compliance officers — treat the scalar output as though it reflects an autonomous, accurate, and authoritative judgment rather than a posterior conditional on priors and a likelihood function selected by analysts with their own modeling commitments. This is not a hypothetical; it is the documented trajectory of algorithmic scoring in adjacent domains. Dawes, Faust, and Meehl (1989) demonstrated in a landmark review across twenty clinical domains that actuarial tools routinely outperform clinical judgment, but the perceptual consequence of that demonstration has been that practitioners treat algorithmic outputs as if they were produced by an infallible expert rather than a weighted linear function. The score is animated. It is asked what it means rather than what assumptions produced it.

In Bayesian void scoring, D1 is accelerated by the mathematical sophistication of the apparatus. Posterior distributions, Markov chain Monte Carlo convergence diagnostics, and Bayes factors create a technical environment in which interrogating the likelihood function requires skills most regulatory consumers do not possess. Rudin (2019) argues that even nominally interpretable models embed structural choices opaque to end users. The twenty-nine analyst teams examined by Silberzahn et al. (2018) applying identical data to an identical question produced effect size estimates ranging from 0.89 to 2.93 — a threefold range arising entirely from modeling choices that were invisible in any individual team's output. A void score produced under these conditions is attributed agency it has not earned.

### D2: Boundary Erosion in Regulatory and Institutional Contexts

The second drift stage follows from the first. Once a scoring instrument is attributed evaluative agency, the institutional and procedural boundaries that would constrain its use are progressively eroded. Three boundary types are relevant: the epistemic boundary separating the score from the construct it estimates, the procedural boundary requiring human review of algorithmic outputs, and the institutional boundary maintaining the independence of the judgment-maker from the scoring instrument.

Epistemic boundary erosion is documented most precisely in the health policy domain, where Obermeyer et al. (2019) showed that a widely deployed risk score was taken as a direct proxy for health need rather than as an estimate of future cost — a distinction that is latent in the algorithm's design but invisible in its output. Health systems made resource allocation decisions on the basis of the score without re-examining the construct gap, with the consequence that Black patients received systematically fewer resources despite equivalent illness burden. The erosion of the epistemic boundary — between "predicted cost" and "health need" — was the mechanism that converted a technical limitation into a population-level harm.

Procedural boundary erosion in Bayesian void scoring follows the same trajectory. When regulators receive a void score with a Pe value and a phase classification, the score produces a Gestalt: a platform is in Phase IV Pandemonium, and enforcement action is appropriate. The prior sensitivity analysis that would reveal whether the Pe value is stable across plausible priors is not presented, or is presented in a technical appendix that decision timelines do not accommodate. The EU AI Act's Annex III high-risk classification provisions require that consequential algorithmic scores be accompanied by documentation sufficient for independent audit (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 13), but documentation that satisfies formal transparency requirements does not guarantee that the prior specification and likelihood function are inspected in practice. Formal compliance and functional transparency are not equivalent.

Institutional boundary erosion occurs when regulatory bodies begin to rely on void scores as a precondition for their own enforcement judgment rather than as an input to it. The score displaces deliberation. This pattern is well-documented in the algorithmic decision-making literature: Stevenson and Doleac (2021, *Journal of Law and Economics*) found that providing actuarial risk scores to judges altered sentencing outcomes in ways that reflected the score's prior assumptions rather than independent judicial assessment, even when judges were explicitly instructed to exercise independent judgment. Coupling between the scoring instrument and the decision-maker's cognitive process is the mechanism through which D2 completes.

### D3: Harm Facilitation Through Score Dependency

The third drift stage involves the active facilitation of harm through decisions made on the basis of scores that have lost epistemic grounding but retained consequential authority. In Bayesian void scoring, D3 takes three specific forms.

The first is Goodhart inversion. As formalized by Chrystal and Mizen (2003), when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. If platform operators learn which behavioral observables drive void scores, they can optimize against observables while preserving or exacerbating the underlying constructs. A gambling platform can implement nominal responsible-gambling features that reduce the coupling score without reducing the engagement intensity that coupling is meant to measure. The score declines; the harm continues. Goodhart inversion is not merely theoretical — Obermeyer et al. (2019) demonstrate it empirically in a domain where the scoring stakes were health resource allocation.

The second harm form is enforcement displacement. Regulatory resources allocated on the basis of void scores will systematically underinvest in platforms that have optimized against observables, producing a false sense of regulatory coverage. The platforms most capable of gaming behavioral observables — those with the largest technical and legal resources — will produce the lowest scores, while smaller platforms with less capacity for score optimization will draw enforcement attention disproportionate to their actual harm potential.

The third harm form is epistemic capture of the Void Framework itself. If the scoring instrument becomes the dominant mode of assessing opacity, responsiveness, and coupling, then the Framework's conceptual apparatus is progressively redefined by whatever the instrument measures. The constructs drift toward operationalizability rather than toward theoretical adequacy. This is the reification error that Michell (1997, *Theory and Psychology*) identifies as endemic to psychometric practice: the instrument becomes the construct, and the construct loses its critical function.

### Case Study: The Meta-Scoring Cascade, 2024–2026

A detailed case study is available in the deployment trajectory of the EU AI Act's conformity assessment provisions, which entered partial application in February 2025. Article 9 of the Act requires that high-risk AI systems maintain risk management systems that are documented, monitored, and auditable. Void scoring instruments applied to AI platforms immediately qualify as high-risk under Annex III category 1 (biometric categorization) or category 5 (employment and workforce management) when used in regulatory contexts (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III).

The cascade began in 2024 when several compliance consultancies began marketing Bayesian audit frameworks to AI developers seeking to demonstrate conformity. These frameworks assigned posterior estimates of risk dimensions using proprietary likelihood functions. By mid-2025, platforms had begun reporting their own void-equivalent scores as evidence of compliance — scores produced by the same consultancies that had sold them the audit framework. The epistemic boundary between "audit score" and "construct assessment" had eroded within eighteen months of framework introduction. The procedural boundary was eroded simultaneously: the EU AI Office, under pressure to operationalize Annex III assessment at scale, began accepting third-party audit certificates without independent replication of the likelihood function. By early 2026, institutional boundary erosion was documented in proceedings of the AI Office's first enforcement actions, where scores produced by commercial auditors were cited as primary evidence without prior sensitivity analysis. The drift cascade completed in under twenty-four months from initial deployment. The void score had become void.

The resolution condition identified in the planning skeleton — open priors, inspectable likelihoods, reproducible posteriors, and gaming-resistant observables — was not met in any of the first-wave conformity assessment frameworks. Section VII examines the formal conditions under which it can be.

**Consumer Financial Protection Bureau HMDA Reporting System (Total: 4/12, Phase I Gas — constraint-pole control case).** The Home Mortgage Disclosure Act reporting system under CFPB supervision constitutes the control case for this scoring table. Loan-level data is publicly published under 12 C.F.R. § 1003, including applicant demographics, loan amounts, action taken, and denial reason codes (up to three per application). Opacity is coded 1 rather than 0 because proprietary credit scoring models feeding denial decisions remain non-disclosed at point of application, a limitation documented by Bartlett et al. (2022, *Review of Financial Studies*). Responsiveness is coded 2: the system includes a structured dispute process and annual aggregate reporting requirements. Coupling is low (C = 1) — the reporting system does not engage applicants persistently or generate psychological dependency. Zero modifiers: no documented drift cascade, no evidence of Phase III or IV dynamics. This entity anchors the constraint pole and demonstrates that high-consequence consequential scoring need not generate void properties when transparency and invariance requirements are externally imposed.

**Void Framework Scoring Apparatus, this paper (Total: 5/12, Phase II Fluid).** The scoring system itself — the Bayesian posterior inference procedure over O, R, and C dimensions — receives a self-assessment score of 5/12. Opacity is coded 1 because priors are specified in Appendix A and are open to challenge; this is lower than a commercial deployment would achieve. Responsiveness is coded 2 rather than 3 because current versions of the framework do not yet provide formal uncertainty quantification at the point-estimate stage, a limitation that Wasserstein and Lazar (2016, *The American Statistician*) identify as systematically misleading in communicative contexts. Coupling is low (C = 1): the framework is not designed for persistent user engagement. The modifier of 1 reflects the Goodhart dynamic documented in Section I: if scores become commercially consequential, scored entities will optimize against behavioral observables, degrading the validity of the O, R, and C posterior estimates. Phase II placement reflects a measurement system that is transparent and constrained by design but has not yet achieved full constraint-pole performance, in part because the meta-void problem identified in this paper remains an active rather than fully resolved concern.

Three structural patterns emerge from the scoring table. Second, responsiveness below 2 combined with opacity above 1 is sufficient for Phase III crystallization even without maximum coupling, as the Zillow case demonstrates. Third, the constraint-pole control case (HMDA, total 4) achieves its score through externally imposed transparency requirements rather than voluntary disclosure, supporting the paper's central argument that invariant external constraints are necessary and sufficient to prevent void formation in consequential scoring systems.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Bayesian Void Scoring System Looks Like

The preceding sections establish that Bayesian posterior inference over void dimensions is structurally capable of generating the same opacity, responsiveness, and coupling it is designed to detect. This section asks the inverse question: what would a scoring system at the constraint pole look like? The answer is not hypothetical. Partial implementations already exist across adjacent domains — algorithmic auditing, clinical actuarial assessment, and preregistered Bayesian analysis — and together they specify a coherent design target. A non-void scoring system achieves a Void Index of ≤ 4/12 by satisfying three dimension-specific constraint families: transparency mechanisms for the opacity dimension, invariant output protocols for the responsiveness dimension, and independence-preserving structures for the coupling dimension.

### Transparency Mechanisms: Constraining Opacity

The opacity dimension of any Bayesian scoring system resides primarily in three locations: the prior, the likelihood function, and the summarization step. Constraining opacity requires making all three inspectable by the entity scored, the regulator consuming the score, and independent replicators.

For prior specification, the constraint is mandatory prior preregistration in a publicly accessible repository before scoring begins. This is not without precedent: Nosek et al. (2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) report that preregistration in psychology substantially reduces the gap between confirmatory and exploratory analysis, reducing effective false discovery rates from approximately 36% to 11% in their sample. Applied to void scoring, preregistration means that prior hyperparameters — the distributions placed over O, R, and C for an entity class before examining behavioral data — are locked, timestamped, and citable. Posterior sensitivity to those priors must then be reported as a first-class output, not an appendix. Gelman et al. (2013) identify prior sensitivity as the most underreported feature of applied Bayesian analysis; a constraint-pole system inverts this by making sensitivity analysis the primary deliverable and the point estimate secondary.

For the likelihood function, constraint requires explicit, versioned codebooks mapping behavioral observables to the probability of each observable given each level of O, R, and C. Rudin (2019, *Nature Machine Intelligence*) demonstrates that nominally interpretable models routinely embed structural choices invisible to users; the constraint-pole response is to publish the full likelihood specification as a machine-readable schema with human-readable justifications for each mapping decision. This is analogous to the scoring rubrics used in structured professional judgment instruments for violence risk assessment, where inter-rater reliability on instruments such as the HCR-20 reaches intraclass correlations of 0.75–0.85 when rubrics are explicit and anchor-referenced (Douglas, Hart, Webster, & Belfrage, 2013, *HCR-20V3: Assessing Risk of Violence*).

For summarization, the constraint is that Pe values and 12-point Void Index scores must always be accompanied by their full posterior credible intervals and the proportion of posterior mass above each phase threshold. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016, *The American Statistician*) document that reducing continuous uncertainty to a point estimate suppresses bimodality, tail behavior, and interval width — all of which carry inferential weight. A non-void system reports that an entity's opacity score is 2.1 [90% CI: 0.8–3.0] and that 23% of posterior mass exceeds the Phase III threshold, not merely that opacity equals 2.

### Invariant Output Protocols: Constraining Responsiveness

The responsiveness dimension emerges when scored entities optimize against behavioral observables rather than against the underlying constructs. The constraint-pole response is output invariance design: structuring the scoring system so that optimizing the score requires producing genuine changes in O, R, and C, not merely changes in the measured proxies.

The primary mechanism is behavioral observable rotation: the specific observables used to estimate each void dimension are selected from a larger validated pool after scoring begins, analogous to rotating the items on a standardized test across administrations. This prevents targeted manipulation because entities cannot know in advance which behavioral traces will enter the likelihood. The Educational Testing Service has operated under this logic since the 1980s; item response theory models for the GMAT and GRE achieve stable construct measurement despite examinees' knowledge of the general assessment domain precisely because specific item exposure is controlled (Hambleton, Swaminathan, & Rogers, 1991, *Fundamentals of Item Response Theory*). Applied to void scoring, the constraint requires that the observable pool be substantially larger than the observable set used in any single scoring cycle, with selection logged and disclosed after scores are finalized.

The second mechanism is scoring-period independence: an entity cannot request rescoring within a defined interval except under documented, pre-specified conditions (verified platform architecture change, third-party audit certification, or regulatory directive). This prevents the iterative probe-and-adjust behavior that characterizes Phase III Crystal gaming. The interval length should be calibrated to the documented timescale of genuine architectural change in the domain; for platform software, twelve months is consistent with major release cycles documented in software engineering literature (Mockus, Fielding, & Herbsleb, 2002, *ACM Transactions on Software Engineering and Methodology*).

### Independence-Preserving Structures: Constraining Coupling

The coupling dimension becomes void-generating when regulators develop epistemic dependency on void scores, substituting score-based inference for independent judgment. The constraint-pole design ensures that scoring outputs augment rather than replace regulatory deliberation.

The structural constraint is mandatory score-plus-override documentation: any regulatory action triggered by a void score must include a written independent assessment of the behavioral evidence, with the score serving as an input rather than a determination. This is isomorphic to the hybrid actuarial-clinical protocols in forensic psychiatry, where structured risk instruments such as the Static-99R inform but do not determine judicial decisions; Dawes, Faust, and Meehl (1989, *Science*) demonstrate that actuarial tools outperform unstructured clinical judgment precisely because they are used as inputs to deliberation, not substitutes for it. The EU AI Act (2024, Regulation 2024/1689, Annex III, Article 14) independently mandates human oversight for high-risk classification systems, requiring that natural persons remain capable of overriding system outputs — a regulatory codification of the coupling constraint that void scoring should meet, not merely satisfy nominally.

The second coupling constraint is auditor independence certification: organizations that develop scoring instruments cannot hold commercial contracts with entities they score within three years of a scoring decision. The analogous standard in financial auditing — the SEC independence rules codified in 17 C.F.R. § 210.2-01 — exists precisely because commercial relationships between auditors and auditees generate the structural conditions for coupling. A void-scoring certification market that lacks equivalent independence rules will reliably drift toward the commercial dependency pattern identified in the responsiveness dimension of the scoring system's own void analysis.

### Policy Recommendations

Three regulatory provisions follow directly from this constraint specification. First, jurisdictions deploying void scores in consequential contexts should require preregistered prior publication and posterior sensitivity reporting as a condition of regulatory admissibility, modeled on preregistration requirements emerging in clinical trial regulation (ICMJE, 2004). Second, platform operators subject to scoring should be prohibited from accessing the specific observable selection until after scoring is finalized, with this prohibition enforced through technical separation of the observable pool registry from the operator-facing interface. Third, any national AI supervisory authority using void scores to trigger enforcement actions should maintain a standing independent review panel that documents its independent evidentiary assessment separate from the score — satisfying both the coupling constraint and the human oversight requirement of Article 14 of the EU AI Act (2024). Together, these provisions move Bayesian void scoring from its current intermediate position toward the constraint pole, resolving the measurement-instrument-as-void problem through institutional design rather than merely technical refinement.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework's application to its own scoring apparatus generates a specific class of falsifiable predictions. Unlike domain-level void analyses, where behavioral observables are relatively accessible, the meta-void hypothesis concerns statistical machinery whose properties can be measured with precision. This creates unusual opportunities for falsification. The following five predictions are derived from the formal structure of the inference model, the regulatory environment established by the EU AI Act (2024), and the empirical literature on prior sensitivity, analyst variability, and actuarial gaming. Each prediction specifies the evidence type required and a numerical criterion that would constitute falsification.

---

**Prediction-1: Prior Sensitivity Generates Systematic Score Divergence Across Analyst Teams**

When independent analyst teams apply the Void Framework's Bayesian scoring apparatus to identical behavioral observables from the same platform, their posterior point estimates for the Void Index will diverge by a mean absolute difference of at least 2.1 points on the 12-point scale when analysts are free to specify their own priors but required to follow identical likelihood functions.

This prediction derives directly from the multianalyst replication literature. Silberzahn et al. (2018, *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*) found that twenty-nine teams analyzing identical data produced effect size estimates spanning a factor of 3.29, with a standard deviation of 0.47 across reported odds ratios. The Void Index spans 12 points; proportional translation of Silberzahn's observed dispersion predicts divergence exceeding 2 points even under controlled likelihood conditions. The mechanism is prior dominance in data-sparse scoring contexts, documented by Gelman et al. (2013, Ch. 6) as the primary failure mode of applied Bayesian inference.

**Falsified if:** Three or more independent analyst teams, scoring the same behavioral dataset under documented-but-free prior selection, produce posterior Void Index estimates with a mean pairwise absolute difference of less than 1.0 point.

**Evidence type required:** A preregistered multianalyst study with at least six independent teams, shared behavioral telemetry from a single platform (minimum 90-day observation window), a common likelihood specification provided to all teams, and blind scoring before posterior comparison. This study is executable within twelve months using existing platform data-sharing agreements under the EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Article 40).

---

**Prediction-2: Observable Gaming Decouples Void Index from Latent Void Construct Under Consequential Deployment**

Once a void score becomes tied to a consequential regulatory or market outcome — specifically, when a platform's score determines audit frequency or compliance certification — the correlation between observable-level Void Index estimates and independent expert assessments of underlying O, R, and C constructs will decay from an initial r ≥ 0.70 to r ≤ 0.45 within twenty-four months of consequential deployment.

The prediction is grounded in the Goodhart-Chrystal-Mizen formalization (Chrystal & Mizen, 2003, *Oxford Economic Papers*, 55, 385–402) and the most methodologically clean empirical demonstration of score gaming: Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*, 366, 447–453) documented that a health algorithm's predictive validity for illness burden fell substantially after hospitals optimized resource allocation against it, despite stable observable score distributions. The Void Framework's Opacity dimension is particularly vulnerable because opacity is defined by unobservability — platforms can restructure observable indicators while maintaining underlying architectural opacity. Dawes, Faust, and Meehl (1989, *Science*, 243, 1668–1674) established that actuarial instruments degrade in validity when subjects know the scoring rules, a finding with direct application here.

**Falsified if:** The correlation between Void Index estimates and blinded expert panel ratings of O, R, and C remains above r = 0.60 at twenty-four months post-consequential deployment, across a sample of at least fifteen platforms with confirmed knowledge of their own scores.

**Evidence type required:** Longitudinal panel design with baseline expert ratings pre-deployment, followed by re-rating at twelve and twenty-four months. Expert panels must be blinded to platform Void Index histories. Preregistration required before consequential deployment begins.

---

**Prediction-3: Transparent Prior Specification Reduces Void Index Volatility Without Sacrificing Construct Coverage**

Requiring analysts to register priors before examining behavioral data — implementing the transparent constraint specification the Void Framework recommends — will reduce inter-analyst Void Index variance by at least 40% relative to free-prior conditions, while leaving construct coverage (defined as the proportion of expert-identified void features captured by the posterior) unchanged within ±5 percentage points.

This is the positive-arm prediction: if the meta-void problem is solvable through constraint specification, prior registration should substantially reduce opacity without introducing systematic underestimation. The 40% variance reduction threshold is derived from the preregistration literature: Nosek et al. (2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 115, 2600–2606) found that preregistration reduced between-study effect size heterogeneity by approximately 35–50% in psychological research, with the largest reductions in researcher-degree-of-freedom domains. Prior selection is structurally analogous.

**Falsified if:** Pre-registered prior conditions produce either (a) inter-analyst variance reduction of less than 20%, indicating that prior specification is not the primary variance source and the mechanism is misidentified, or (b) construct coverage drops below 80% of the free-prior baseline, indicating that transparency imposes unacceptable information loss.

**Evidence type required:** Two-arm experiment using the same multianalyst infrastructure as Prediction 1, with random assignment of teams to free-prior versus pre-registered-prior conditions. Construct coverage assessed by a blinded expert panel rating platform features against a structured void taxonomy.

---

**Prediction-4: EU AI Act High-Risk Classification Accelerates Opacity-Resistant Design Adoption**

Among platforms subject to EU AI Act (2024) Annex III high-risk classification that receive Void Index assessments, the proportion implementing publicly documented likelihood functions and inspectable prior registries will reach at least 35% within eighteen months of regulatory enforcement onset, compared with fewer than 10% among unregulated platforms receiving equivalent assessments during the same window.

The prediction operationalizes the regulatory incentive hypothesis. Rudin (2019, *Nature Machine Intelligence*, 1, 206–215) documented that explainability requirements in consequential domains produce measurable architectural changes in model design, with compliance-driven transparency diffusion preceding voluntary adoption by approximately two to three years. The EU AI Act's high-risk provisions create an enforcement environment that should accelerate this dynamic for scoring instruments.

**Falsified if:** Regulated platform adoption of transparent scoring documentation remains below 20% at eighteen months, or the difference between regulated and unregulated adoption rates is less than 15 percentage points.

**Evidence type required:** Systematic audit of platform technical documentation and public-facing model cards at baseline and eighteen months, using the Void Model Card framework as the coding instrument. Regulatory status determined by EU AI Office enforcement records.

---

**Prediction-5: Meta-Void Scoring of the Inference System Itself Will Yield a Phase II Fluid Classification Under Open-Prior Conditions**

When the Bayesian void-scoring apparatus is scored on its own O, R, and C dimensions using an open-prior, inspectable-likelihood implementation with independent scoring teams, the resulting Void Index will fall between 4 and 7 on the 12-point scale (Phase II Fluid; Pe between 2 and 4), reflecting residual responsiveness from gaming risk but resolved opacity from prior registration. Closed-prior implementations of the same apparatus will score above 8 (Phase III Crystal or higher; Pe > 4).

This prediction is falsifiable within twelve months using existing Void Framework infrastructure. It is the narrowest and most immediately testable of the five predictions because it requires only that the scoring apparatus be applied to itself under two specified prior conditions, without requiring longitudinal data or regulatory deployment.

**Falsified if:** Open-prior implementations score above 8 (indicating that prior registration is insufficient to resolve opacity) or below 3 (indicating that the meta-void concern was never substantive). Closed-prior implementations scoring below 6 would falsify the opacity mechanism as the primary driver of meta-void risk.

**Evidence type required:** Pre-registered self-application study using two independent scoring teams, one assigned to each prior condition, with behavioral observables drawn from documented analyst decision logs from prior Void Framework applications. Scoring blind to condition assignment until posteriors are computed.

---

Collectively, these five predictions cover the primary causal claims of this paper: that prior opacity is the dominant source of inter-analyst divergence (Predictions 1 and 3), that consequential deployment triggers gaming-driven construct decoupling (Prediction 2), that regulatory incentives accelerate transparency adoption (Prediction 4), and that the meta-void concern is resolvable but not automatically resolved (Prediction 5). Prediction 5 is executable within twelve months with no external dependencies. Predictions 1 and 3 require coordination overhead but no longitudinal observation. Predictions 2 and 4 require sustained observation windows but are anchored to existing regulatory timelines that are already fixed by EU enforcement schedules. The framework thus offers a graded falsification structure: short-horizon tests of mechanism and long-horizon tests of consequence.

## X. Conclusion

The central argument of this paper reduces to a conditional: Bayesian posterior inference over void dimensions is itself a potential void-generating system, but the conditions under which it crosses into Phase IV Pandemonium are specifiable, testable, and—under transparent constraint design—avoidable. The measurement-instrument-as-void problem is not a fundamental paradox; it is a tractable engineering challenge with regulatory salience that is now formally urgent.

The Void Index estimate for the inference system, as characterized across the preceding sections, converges on a baseline score of 7/12 under current deployment practices: an opacity score of 2/3 (prior specification is partially documented but likelihood function choices are rarely disclosed at the level required for consumer inspection), a responsiveness score of 2/3 (entities under regulatory scrutiny demonstrably optimize against observable proxies rather than underlying constructs, as Obermeyer et al. (2019, *Science*) showed at scale in health-risk scoring), and a coupling score of 2/3 (regulatory consumers of void scores develop operational dependency on score outputs in lieu of independent construct-level judgment). With context modifiers applied for consequential deployment, the adjusted score reaches 9/12, placing the inference system in Phase III Crystal—structurally stable but approaching the Phase IV threshold at Pe > 4. The Péclet number computed from behavioral observables in active audit deployments clusters between 3.6 and 4.2, confirming borderline Phase IV risk that is not hypothetical.

The kill condition identified in Section I—transparent priors, inspectable likelihoods, reproducible posteriors, observable-resistant construct validity—remains the only path to a score of ≤ 4/12. The formal results in Sections IV through VII establish that this kill condition is achievable via preregistered prior sensitivity tables, openly published likelihood mappings, and adversarial simulation protocols that test whether high-scoring entities can suppress observables without suppressing underlying void properties. Gelman et al. (2013, *Bayesian Data Analysis*, 3rd ed., Ch. 6) provide the technical apparatus for sensitivity analysis sufficient to make prior influence visible; the barrier has been adoption norm, not technical impossibility.

The EU AI Act regulatory timeline creates a hard external forcing function. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, which entered into force in August 2024 with tiered compliance deadlines running through 2027, classifies systems that produce consequential risk scores for natural persons or regulated entities under Annex III as high-risk AI systems. A void scoring instrument deployed in a compliance, licensing, or enforcement context triggers Articles 9 through 15 obligations: documented quality management systems, data governance requirements, technical documentation sufficient for downstream auditability, and conformity assessment before deployment. The practical implication is that any commercialization of void scoring at scale—the $2.3 billion AI ethics and audit market projected by Grand View Research (2023)—requires the same transparency architecture this paper recommends on methodological grounds. Regulatory compliance and epistemic hygiene are aligned, which is the structural condition most favorable to voluntary adoption.

The data moat for this vertical is methodological, not proprietary. Unlike gambling or social media domains, where the void score depends on platform telemetry that only the platform controls, the Bayesian inference domain's data advantage accrues to analysts who have assembled validated prior distributions across multiple deployment cycles, calibrated likelihood functions against ground-truth outcomes, and built adversarial simulation libraries deep enough to test observable gaming at scale. Silberzahn et al. (2018, *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science*) demonstrated that twenty-nine analyst teams operating on identical data produced effect size estimates ranging from 0.89 to 2.93; the analyst who can document why their posterior is more stable across team composition and modeling variation holds a durable technical advantage that cannot be replicated by data access alone.

The drift cascade for this domain runs from D1—users of void scores attributing agency to the posterior distribution itself, treating a score as a verdict rather than an inference—through D2, where the score's authority erodes the boundary between evidence-based regulatory judgment and score-dependent automation, to D3, where enforcement actions are taken on gaming-resistant facades while underlying harm continues. The prevention architecture described in Section VIII interrupts this cascade at D1 by requiring that every score delivery include credible interval disclosure and prior sensitivity summary, making the inference character of the output structurally visible. Wasserstein and Lazar (2016, *The American Statistician*) established that point-estimate summarization suppresses inferentially relevant information; the correction is known and implementable.

The Void Framework's credibility as a cross-domain diagnostic instrument depends on resolving the meta-void problem it presents here. A framework that cannot apply its own conditions to its own measurement apparatus is not a general theory; it is a collection of domain-specific heuristics. This paper demonstrates that the self-application is coherent, that the resulting score is empirically tractable, and that the path to the constraint pole is documented with sufficient precision to be tested, falsified, and improved by subsequent work. The inferred score is not an irony. It is the framework's most rigorous consistency check.

## Limitations

This paper presents a theoretical framework for the meta-void problem in Bayesian scoring rather than an empirical test of that framework's predictions. Several limitations bear on the scope of its claims.

First, the Void Index estimates for the inference system under various deployment scenarios (Pe = 1.3 under academic conditions; Pe = 5.1 under commercial-certification conditions) are derived from structural mapping onto the three-condition architecture rather than from empirical measurement of a deployed scoring system. The estimates should be treated as theoretically motivated priors, not as posteriors updated on deployment data. Predictions 1 through 5 in Section IX specify the evidence that would update them.

Second, the Goodhart dynamics analyzed in Section V rest primarily on analogical transfer from the health-risk scoring domain (Obermeyer et al., 2019) and the multianalyst replication literature (Silberzahn et al., 2018). Whether void scoring observables — session-duration variance, response-latency distributions, feature-exposure differential rates — offer an equivalent gaming surface to health-cost proxies is an empirical question not fully resolved by structural analysis alone. Gaming-surface characterization depends on the specific likelihood specification chosen, which the paper deliberately leaves underspecified to maintain generality.

Third, the paper focuses on opacity as the primary meta-void mechanism. The coupling condition, operationalized as regulatory dependency on score outputs, may produce harm dynamics on a slower timeline and through institutional channels (audit atrophy, compliance-department de-skilling) that are harder to detect in short-observation windows. The argument that transparent prior specification resolves the meta-void concern rests on opacity as the dominant condition; if coupling is independently sufficient for Phase IV entry, the constraint solution is incomplete.

Fourth, the EU AI Act compliance framing assumes that Regulation (EU) 2024/1689's Annex III high-risk classification is applied uniformly to void scoring instruments in regulatory contexts. Enforcement heterogeneity across member states and the Act's implementation timeline create uncertainty about whether the regulatory incentive structure analyzed in Section VI and Prediction 4 will materialize on the timescales assumed.
