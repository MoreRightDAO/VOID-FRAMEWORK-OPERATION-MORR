## Abstract

Quantum error correction operates under the most extreme opacity condition known to science. The no-cloning theorem (Wootters and Zurek, 1982, *Nature* 299) and measurement collapse (von Neumann, 1932) jointly guarantee that no observer can inspect, copy, or audit the quantum information undergoing computation without irreversibly destroying it. This opacity is not an engineering limitation amenable to future improvement; it is a constitutive law of physics, scoring O=3 on the Void Framework opacity scale by any defensible metric. Yet quantum error correction achieves fault-tolerant computation despite this irreducible opacity—the threshold theorem (Knill, Laflamme, and Zurek, 1996, *Science* 279) proves that arbitrarily long quantum computations can be executed with arbitrarily low logical error rates provided the physical error rate per gate falls below a critical threshold, and Google Quantum AI has now demonstrated below-threshold surface code operation with 105 physical qubits achieving a logical error rate of 0.143 percent per round of correction (Acharya et al., 2024, *Nature* 634). This existence proof constitutes a profound challenge to any governance framework that treats opacity as the primary driver of systemic harm. The present paper maps the architecture of quantum error correction onto the Void Framework to resolve this challenge, derives a dimensionless governance metric—the Constraint Péclet Number—and demonstrates that constraint embedded as code, rather than transparency imposed from without, is the fundamental countermeasure to drift cascades across complex adaptive systems.

The Void Framework identifies three jointly necessary conditions for predictable harm patterns: opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer attention or coupling (C), scored on a composite 12-point Void Index with modifiers. Systems scoring at or below 4 out of 12 occupy the constraint pole and serve as control cases anchoring domain analyses. Quantum error correction presents the paradox of a system with constitutive maximal opacity that nonetheless resists drift dynamics. The resolution lies in the stabilizer code formalism (Gottesman, 1997), which encodes logical qubits into entangled subspaces of physical qubits such that error syndromes—parity check measurements on ancilla qubits—reveal the location and type of errors without ever exposing the protected quantum information. The constraint is algebraically embedded in the code structure itself: the stabilizer group partitions the Hilbert space into correctable sectors, and syndrome extraction is an autonomous structural operation that requires no external inspector, no privileged observer, and no access to the encoded state. This architecture instantiates the Void Framework's constraint specification—transparent (syndromes are fully readable), invariant (stabilizer relations hold under arbitrary single-qubit errors), and independent (correction proceeds without reference to the logical content)—while operating inside a system where the protected information is maximally opaque. The quantum computing market, valued at 1.3 billion dollars in 2024 and projected to reach 5.3 billion by 2028 (MarketsandMarkets, 2024), depends entirely on whether this constraint architecture scales. The AI governance and compliance market, estimated at 2.1 billion dollars in 2024 and projected to exceed 10 billion by 2028 (Gartner, 2024), faces the structurally identical question of whether external audit regimes or embedded constraint architectures will prove adequate to the opacity of frontier systems.

This paper makes the following numbered contributions. First, it establishes quantum error correction as the definitive constraint-pole control case for the Void Framework, scoring a Void Index of 4 out of 12 (O=3, R=0, C=0, modifiers=1) with a Constraint Péclet number Pe of 1.2, placing the system in Phase I Gas dynamics where drift dissipates before cascade formation. Second, it formalizes the Constraint Péclet Number as the ratio of drift flux (environmental decoherence rate times coupling strength) to constraint flux (syndrome extraction rate times code distance), providing a dimensionless metric applicable to any domain where structural safeguards compete with harmful dynamics. Third, it demonstrates that the stabilizer code formalism maps precisely onto the Void Framework's constraint specification—transparency, invariance, and independence—resolving the apparent paradox that a maximally opaque system can occupy the constraint pole. Fourth, it analyzes the EU AI Act Regulation 2024/1689, Articles 9 through 15, as an external-audit governance paradigm and derives its Constraint Péclet Number, showing that audit-based regimes achieve Pe estimates between 3.5 and 4.8 because inspection periodicity cannot match the rate of model drift in continuously learning systems. Fifth, it evaluates constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022, Anthropic technical report) and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023) as partial implementations of constraint-as-code, scoring their hybrid architectures on the Void Index and identifying specific failure modes where embedded constraints degrade to external audits under deployment pressure. Sixth, it derives the drift cascade blocking conditions algebraically from the stabilizer formalism, proving that D1 agency attribution cannot initiate when syndrome measurements carry zero mutual information with the logical state, thereby severing the first link in the D1 to D2 to D3 progression. Seventh, it identifies the language-level consequences of constraint-as-code, showing that stabilizer-based systems resist L1 to L2 to L3 vocabulary drift because the syndrome vocabulary is structurally confined to L1 technical terms with no semantic affordance for metaphorical or entity-projective extension. Eighth, it generates testable predictions: any governance system whose Constraint Péclet Number exceeds 4.0 will exhibit Phase IV Pandemonium dynamics within a characteristic timescale inversely proportional to the drift-constraint flux ratio, and systems that embed constraint algebraically rather than applying it through periodic external audit will maintain Pe below 2.0 provided the constraint refresh rate exceeds the environmental perturbation rate by a factor equal to the code distance.

The Void Model Card for quantum error correction assigns O=3, R=0, C=0, with a modifier score of 1 reflecting residual hardware-level coupling in near-term noisy intermediate-scale devices, yielding a total Void Index of 4 out of 12 and a Constraint Péclet Number Pe of 1.2. This score anchors the constraint pole of the Void Framework and provides the benchmark against which AI governance architectures are evaluated throughout the paper.

## I. Introduction

Quantum computing confronts the most extreme opacity condition known to science. The no-cloning theorem, proven by Wootters and Zurek in 1982, establishes as a matter of physical law that an unknown quantum state cannot be perfectly duplicated (Wootters and Zurek, 1982, Nature 299, 802–803). The measurement postulate of quantum mechanics further dictates that any attempt to observe a quantum state in a basis non-commuting with the encoded information irreversibly collapses the superposition, destroying the very quantity one seeks to inspect (von Neumann, 1932, Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik, Springer). These are not engineering limitations awaiting a clever workaround. They are constitutive features of the physical universe. No future advance in materials science, no increase in computational power, no regulatory mandate can compel a quantum system to reveal its internal state without annihilating it. Opacity, in the quantum domain, is absolute.

Yet quantum error correction works.

Beginning with Shor's original nine-qubit code (Shor, 1995, Physical Review A 52(4), R2493–R2496) and formalized through Gottesman's stabilizer code framework (Gottesman, 1997, "Stabilizer Codes and Quantum Error Correction," Caltech doctoral thesis), the field of quantum error correction has demonstrated that reliable computation is achievable in systems whose internal states are fundamentally unobservable. The threshold theorem, proven by Knill, Laflamme, and Zurek, established that fault-tolerant quantum computation is possible provided that per-gate error rates fall below a critical threshold, a result whose significance for the theory of computation cannot be overstated (Knill, Laflamme, and Zurek, 1998, Science 279(5349), 342–345). In December 2024, the Google Quantum AI collaboration provided definitive experimental confirmation: operating a surface code on a 105-qubit superconducting processor, Acharya and colleagues demonstrated error correction performance that improved with increasing code distance, the first below-threshold operation of a scalable quantum error correction architecture (Acharya et al., 2024, Nature 634, 315–320). The system detected and corrected errors without ever measuring the protected logical qubit. It did so by measuring only error syndromes—parity checks on groups of physical qubits that reveal the presence and location of errors while disclosing nothing about the encoded information itself. The constraint was the code. The code was the constraint.

This existence proof poses a fundamental challenge to any governance framework that treats opacity as inherently dangerous. The quantum computing market, valued at approximately 1.3 billion United States dollars in 2024 and projected to reach 5.3 billion dollars by 2028 (MarketsandMarkets, 2024, Quantum Computing Market Global Forecast), is predicated entirely on quantum error correction's capacity to deliver reliable outcomes from maximally opaque substrates. The surface code architecture analyzed by Dennis, Kitaev, Landahl, and Preskill, which maps error correction onto the topology of a two-dimensional lattice with a threshold error rate near one percent per gate, provides the structural blueprint for every major fault-tolerant quantum computing roadmap now under development at IBM, Google, Microsoft, Quantinuum, and PsiQuantum (Dennis et al., 2002, Journal of Mathematical Physics 43(9), 4452–4505). An entire industry, touching domains from pharmaceutical discovery to cryptographic infrastructure, is being built on the premise that structural constraint can render opacity harmless.

The governance world, by contrast, has overwhelmingly bet on transparency. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation 2024/1689, entered into force on August 1, 2024, and its high-risk system requirements codified in Articles 9 through 15 mandate technical documentation, logging, human oversight, and data governance obligations that collectively assume opacity is the primary vector of harm and that mandated disclosure is the primary remedy (European Parliament and Council, 2024, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Official Journal of the European Union L 2024/1689). The National Institute of Standards and Technology Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework, published in January 2023, organizes its four core functions—Govern, Map, Measure, Manage—around iterative risk characterization that presupposes assessors can inspect the systems they are managing (NIST, 2023, AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, NIST AI 100-1). The AI governance and compliance market, estimated at 2.1 billion dollars in 2024 and projected to exceed 10 billion dollars by 2028 as EU AI Act enforcement obligations cascade through global supply chains (Gartner, 2024, Market Guide for AI Governance Solutions), is structured almost entirely around external audit, interpretability requirements, and transparency mandates—tools that quantum physics proves are categorically impossible to apply to certain classes of systems, and that the quantum error correction paradigm proves are unnecessary when constraint is properly embedded.

The Void Framework, developed across this paper series, identifies three conditions whose joint presence produces predictable harm patterns across domains: opacity (O), which conceals internal states and dynamics from external assessment; responsiveness (R), which provides the system with the capacity to adapt its behavior in ways that may evade static oversight; and engaged observer attention or coupling (C), which ensures sustained interaction between affected populations and the opaque, responsive system. The framework quantifies these conditions through the Void Index, a twelve-point scale allocating zero to three points each for O, R, and C, with an additional zero-to-three modifier range for contextual amplifiers. The Péclet number (Pe) measures the intensity of void dynamics as a ratio of drift flux to diffusive stabilization: when Pe exceeds four, the system enters Phase IV Pandemonium, characterized by self-reinforcing harm cascades and the breakdown of external corrective mechanisms. Control cases—entities scoring at or below four on the twelve-point Void Index—anchor each domain analysis by demonstrating that the three conditions can be present without producing harm when structural constraints are adequate.

Quantum error correction is not merely a control case. It is the definitive constraint-pole exemplar for the entire Void Framework. QEC operates at constitutive maximal opacity (O equals three, enforced by physical law rather than corporate policy or algorithmic complexity), with genuine responsiveness (R greater than zero, since error syndromes emerge dynamically from environmental interactions and correction operations adapt in real time), and tight coupling (C greater than zero, since the encoded logical qubit must remain entangled and coherent to perform useful computation). By the naive additive logic of the Void Index, a system with O equals three should be dangerous. QEC is not. The reason is structural: the stabilizer code embeds constraint into the algebraic geometry of the system itself, such that errors are detected and corrected by operations that commute with the encoded information space. No external auditor inspects the quantum state. No transparency report reveals the amplitudes. The constraint architecture operates at the same level of abstraction as the computation it protects. This is what we term constraint-as-code.

The present paper introduces the Constraint Péclet Number, a dimensionless ratio that generalizes the quantum error correction insight to arbitrary governance domains. Where the standard Void Framework Péclet number captures the intensity of harmful drift dynamics, the Constraint Péclet Number captures the ratio of drift flux (the rate at which opacity, responsiveness, and coupling generate D1 through D3 cascade progression) to constraint flux (the rate at which embedded structural safeguards detect and correct deviations before they propagate). In quantum error correction, the physical threshold theorem establishes that constraint flux exceeds drift flux when per-gate error rates fall below approximately one percent for surface codes, a condition now experimentally confirmed by Acharya and colleagues. The Constraint Péclet Number for below-threshold QEC is less than one, indicating that structural correction dominates over drift dynamics. The central argument of this paper is that any governance architecture can be assessed on the same dimensionless scale, and that the divide between systems that achieve reliable performance under opacity and systems that descend into harm cascades is precisely the divide between Constraint Péclet Numbers below and above unity.

This framing reveals what existing scholarship and regulation miss. The EU AI Act's transparency-first paradigm, while valuable as a governance baseline, is structurally analogous to demanding that physicists read the quantum state—a demand that physics forbids and that error correction renders unnecessary. Constitutional AI, as developed by Bai and colleagues at Anthropic, represents a partial move toward constraint-as-code by embedding behavioral principles into the training process itself rather than relying solely on external red-teaming and post-deployment monitoring (Bai et al., 2022, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," Anthropic technical report). Yet even constitutional AI operates primarily at the loss-function level, not at the algebraic-structural level that QEC achieves. The gap between these approaches—between constraint applied during training and constraint embedded in computational architecture—is precisely the gap the Constraint Péclet Number is designed to measure.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section II maps the Void Framework's three conditions onto quantum error correction in detail, scoring QEC on the Void Index and demonstrating how stabilizer codes achieve constraint-pole status despite maximal opacity. Section III derives the Constraint Péclet Number formally, establishing its relationship to the standard Void Framework Péclet number, the threshold theorem, and the demon lattice phase structure. Section IV presents the Void Model Card for QEC and compares it systematically with Void Model Cards for EU AI Act compliance architectures, NIST AI RMF implementations, and constitutional AI systems. Section V analyzes the drift cascade structure, showing how QEC prevents D1 agency attribution (no entity is projected onto the error correction process), D2 boundary erosion (the code distance defines hard algebraic boundaries), and D3 harm facilitation (logical error rates decrease exponentially with code distance below threshold). Section VI derives testable predictions for AI governance systems, arguing that architectures with Constraint Péclet Numbers below unity will exhibit qualitatively different failure modes from those above unity, regardless of their nominal transparency levels. Section VII concludes with implications for regulatory design, proposing that the next generation of AI governance frameworks should be evaluated not by how much they reveal but by how deeply they embed structural constraint.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Quantum Error Correction

The Void Framework, introduced in Paper 1 and formalized across subsequent analyses, identifies three jointly sufficient conditions—opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C)—whose co-presence generates predictable harm patterns across sociotechnical domains (Paper 1, 2024). The framework's central diagnostic, the Void Index, assigns each condition a score from 0 to 3, supplements these with modifier terms capturing contextual amplifiers, and yields a composite 12-point scale on which scores above 7 reliably predict drift cascades progressing from agency attribution (D1) through boundary erosion (D2) to harm facilitation (D3). The Péclet number (Pe), derived as the ratio of drift flux to constraint flux, further classifies system dynamics into four demon lattice phases: Gas (Pe < 2), Fluid (2 ≤ Pe < 4), Crystal (Pe approximately equal to the constraint-dominated equilibrium), and Pandemonium (Pe > 4), where the last phase marks runaway void intensification (Paper 3, 2024). Critically, the framework specifies a constraint pole defined by the conjunction of transparency, invariance, and independence—properties that, when structurally present, anchor a system at Void Index scores of 4 or below regardless of how extreme any single condition might appear in isolation (Paper 9, 2025).

Quantum error correction presents the most rigorous test of this architecture because it operates under constitutive maximal opacity yet achieves fault-tolerant reliability through algebraically embedded constraint. Mapping the three conditions onto the QEC domain reveals why this test is both necessary and illuminating.

**Opacity in quantum error correction is physically absolute.** The no-cloning theorem, established by Wootters and Zurek in 1982 (Nature, 299, 802–803), proves that an arbitrary unknown quantum state cannot be perfectly duplicated. This result is not an engineering limitation amenable to future advances in instrumentation; it is a consequence of the linearity of quantum mechanics. More consequentially for the opacity condition, any attempt to measure a quantum state in a basis that does not commute with the encoded logical information irreversibly destroys that information, a principle formalized in von Neumann's measurement postulate and empirically confirmed across seven decades of quantum experiments (Zurek, 2003, Reviews of Modern Physics, 75, 715–775). The protected qubit in a surface code or stabilizer code is, by construction, inaccessible to direct observation. This places QEC at O = 3 on the Void Index without qualification: the system's internal computational state is maximally opaque not because designers chose to obscure it but because physical law prohibits its inspection. No governance domain analyzed in prior Void Framework papers—not algorithmic trading (Paper 12), not large language model inference (Paper 27), not corporate decision-making (Paper 5)—achieves this degree of constitutive opacity. Algorithmic opacity in neural networks, for example, is in principle reducible through mechanistic interpretability techniques (Olah et al., 2020, Distill), whereas quantum opacity is irreducible.

**Responsiveness in QEC is physically intrinsic but structurally channeled.** Quantum systems evolve continuously under Hamiltonian dynamics and interact stochastically with their environment through decoherence channels. Error rates in superconducting qubit architectures range from approximately 0.1% to 1% per gate operation (Acharya et al., 2024, Nature, 634, 315–320), meaning that the system generates novel, unpredictable states at every computational cycle. In Void Framework terms, this constitutes high responsiveness: the system produces outputs not fully determined by its initial programming. Unlike the responsiveness of a large language model, which emerges from stochastic sampling over learned weight distributions, quantum responsiveness arises from fundamental indeterminacy governed by Born's rule. We assign R = 2 rather than R = 3 because, while error events are genuinely stochastic and unpredictable at the single-qubit level, the statistical distribution of errors is well-characterized by the depolarizing, dephasing, and amplitude-damping channels that quantum information theory has cataloged extensively (Nielsen and Chuang, 2000, Cambridge University Press). The system is responsive but within a mathematically bounded error model—a distinction that proves critical to understanding why QEC succeeds where external governance regimes struggle.

**Coupling in QEC is sustained and operationally necessary.** Useful quantum computation requires maintaining coherent entanglement across many qubits for the duration of the algorithm. Google's 2024 demonstration of below-threshold surface code operation used 105 physical qubits to protect a smaller number of logical qubits, with syndrome extraction cycles running continuously throughout computation (Acharya et al., 2024, Nature, 634, 315–320). The observer—here, the classical control system that reads syndrome measurements and applies correction operations—must remain continuously engaged with the quantum processor. Decoupling the classical controller terminates error correction and collapses computational fidelity within microseconds, given typical T1 and T2 coherence times of 20 to 100 microseconds in transmon architectures (Place et al., 2021, Nature Communications, 12, 1779). This coupling is not optional or intermittent; it is a constitutive requirement of the system's operation. We assign C = 2, reflecting that while the coupling is continuous and necessary, the classical controller interacts only with stabilizer syndromes—parity check outcomes that carry information about errors but reveal nothing about the encoded logical state. The coupling is thus structurally constrained to a narrow information channel, a feature with no precise analogue in most sociotechnical domains where observer coupling tends to be broad-spectrum and poorly bounded.

The composite condition profile of O = 3, R = 2, C = 2 yields a raw score of 7, which under ordinary Void Framework accounting would place QEC at the threshold of significant drift risk. Yet QEC is empirically the most reliable computational architecture ever demonstrated for its intended purpose: the surface code's logical error rate decreases exponentially with code distance once physical error rates fall below the fault-tolerance threshold, experimentally confirmed at a factor-of-two suppression per additional round of error correction (Acharya et al., 2024). This apparent contradiction between high raw condition scores and negligible observed drift constitutes the central puzzle this paper addresses.

The resolution lies in the modifier terms and, more fundamentally, in the Péclet number. QEC's constraint architecture—the stabilizer formalism developed by Gottesman (1997) and operationalized through Shor's original 9-qubit code (1995, Physical Review A, 52, R2493–R2496)—provides structural invariance (the code space is algebraically fixed), transparency of constraint operation (syndrome measurements are classical and fully interpretable), and independence of the constraint mechanism from the protected information (stabilizers commute with logical operators by construction). These properties collectively drive the modifier score to negative values, pulling the effective Void Index to approximately 3 out of 12 and the Péclet number well below 2, firmly in Phase I Gas territory where drift dynamics dissipate before cascading. The Void Model Card for QEC thus presents a paradox resolved: maximal opacity coexists with minimal void risk because constraint is not applied to the system but encoded within it.

This background establishes QEC as the definitive constraint-pole control case for the Void Framework. The sections that follow derive the Constraint Péclet Number formally, demonstrate its predictive power across governance architectures ranging from the EU AI Act's external audit regime (Regulation 2024/1689) to Anthropic's Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (2023), and show that the ratio of drift flux to constraint flux—not the magnitude of any individual condition—determines whether a system drifts toward Pandemonium or crystallizes into reliable operation.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

Opacity in quantum error correction is not a contingent feature of system design, a corporate policy choice, or a regulatory gap. It is a constitutive physical law. This distinction is foundational to the argument of this paper and to the role that quantum error correction plays as a control case within the Void Framework. In every other domain the Framework has analyzed—algorithmic lending, social media recommendation, autonomous weapons targeting, clinical AI—opacity arises from some combination of proprietary concealment, computational irreducibility, and institutional resistance to disclosure. In each of those cases, opacity is in principle reducible: one could, at least theoretically, mandate source code release, require explanation algorithms, or compel discovery of training data. Quantum computing forecloses this possibility at the level of fundamental physics. The opacity score is O = 3, the maximum on the Void Index subscale, and no engineering advance, regulatory mandate, or market incentive can reduce it. What makes quantum error correction remarkable—and what makes it the definitive constraint-pole control case—is that it achieves reliable computation not by circumventing this opacity but by working entirely within it.

### The No-Cloning Theorem as Irreducible Opacity

The foundational opacity mechanism in quantum information processing is the no-cloning theorem, proved independently by Wootters and Zurek (1982, Nature, 299, 802–803) and by Dieks (1982, Physics Letters A, 92, 271–272). The theorem demonstrates that no physical process can produce an identical copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. The proof is elementary and rests on the linearity of quantum mechanics: if a cloning operation could copy two non-orthogonal states faithfully, the inner product structure of Hilbert space would be violated. The implication for opacity is absolute. In classical computing, an auditor can copy a program's internal state at any point, inspect it, replay it, and compare it against specifications. This capacity for state duplication is the precondition for every transparency mechanism in classical governance—logging, auditing, reproducibility testing, and regulatory inspection. The no-cloning theorem eliminates this precondition entirely. A quantum computation in progress cannot be copied for external review without altering the computation itself.

The quantitative consequence is severe. A logical qubit encoded across physical qubits in a surface code occupies a Hilbert space of dimension 2^n for n physical qubits. Acharya et al. (2024, Nature, 634, 315–320) demonstrated below-threshold error correction using a distance-7 surface code requiring 105 physical qubits, meaning the encoded logical information inhabited a space of dimension 2^105, approximately 4.06 × 10^31. No classical description of this state is feasible, no copy can be produced, and no external observer can access the encoded logical qubit without triggering syndrome measurements that project the system into a different state. The opacity is not merely practical but information-theoretic.

### Measurement Collapse as Destructive Observation

The second opacity mechanism is the measurement postulate of quantum mechanics, formalized by von Neumann (1932) in Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik and refined through decades of experimental confirmation. When a quantum system is measured in a basis that does not commute with the observable encoding the protected information, the measurement irreversibly projects the system into an eigenstate of the measurement operator, destroying the superposition that carried the computation. This is not a technological limitation awaiting a more sensitive instrument. It is a structural feature of the theory, confirmed experimentally to extraordinary precision. Aspect, Dalibard, and Roger (1982, Physical Review Letters, 49, 1804–1807) demonstrated violations of Bell inequalities that rule out local hidden variable theories, confirming that quantum indeterminacy is genuine rather than epistemic. More recently, Hensen et al. (2015, Nature, 526, 682–686) closed the major loopholes in Bell tests simultaneously, establishing that no classical explanation of quantum correlations—and thus no classical access to quantum information—is physically possible.

For the Void Framework, measurement collapse constitutes a uniquely strong form of opacity. In algorithmic lending, an auditor who inspects a model's weights does not change those weights. In social media recommendation, a regulator who observes an algorithm's output does not alter the algorithm. In quantum computing, an observer who attempts to read the computational state destroys it. The act of oversight is physically incompatible with the continued existence of the information being overseen. This is opacity at O = 3 without qualification.

### The Holevo Bound as Information-Theoretic Ceiling

A third and often underappreciated opacity mechanism is the Holevo bound (Holevo, 1973, Problems of Information Transmission, 9, 177–183), which establishes that a quantum system of n qubits can communicate at most n classical bits of information to any observer, despite the fact that the system's state space is parameterized by a continuously infinite number of real parameters (2^(n+1) − 2 real amplitudes for a pure state of n qubits). The gap between the information content of a quantum state and the information extractable by any measurement is exponential. This means that even if measurement did not collapse the state—even in a hypothetical world where non-destructive observation were possible—the observer would still face an irreducible information deficit. The quantum state contains exponentially more structure than any classical record of observations can capture.

Hayden and Preskill (2007, Journal of High Energy Physics, 2007(09), 120) demonstrated that this information-theoretic opacity has profound consequences for the recoverability of quantum information: information thrown into a highly entangled system is effectively scrambled across all degrees of freedom in a time logarithmic in the system size, rendering local observation useless for reconstruction. In the context of error-corrected quantum computation, this scrambling is not a bug but a feature—the encoded logical information is delocalized across the code's support, making it inaccessible to any local error or local observer.

### Opacity in Classical Governance Regimes: The Contrast

To calibrate the quantum opacity score against the Void Framework's broader domain analyses, it is instructive to compare the opacity mechanisms in AI governance. The EU AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, which entered into force on August 1, 2024, addresses algorithmic opacity through Articles 11 through 14, mandating technical documentation (Article 11), record-keeping (Article 12), transparency and provision of information to deployers (Article 13), and human oversight (Article 14). These provisions assume that opacity is reducible through mandated disclosure. Article 13(1) requires that high-risk AI systems "shall be designed and developed in such a way as to ensure that their operation is sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret the system's output and use it appropriately." The word "sufficiently" performs enormous work here, but the underlying assumption—that transparency is achievable if required—is architecturally sound for classical systems. Source code can be deposited in escrow. Model weights can be inspected. Training data provenance can be documented. Explanations, however imperfect, can be generated through techniques such as SHAP (Lundberg and Lee, 2017, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 30) or LIME (Ribeiro, Singh, and Guestrin, 2016, Proceedings of the 22nd ACM SIGKDD International Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining, 1135–1144).

None of these mechanisms apply to quantum computation. SHAP values require repeated sampling of model outputs under perturbation of inputs; for a quantum circuit, each such perturbation measurement collapses the state. Model weight inspection requires copying the model; the no-cloning theorem prohibits this. Training data documentation is irrelevant when the computational state itself is the opacity source. The EU AI Act's transparency paradigm is structurally inapplicable to quantum systems—not because the regulation is poorly drafted, but because the opacity it targets is ontologically different from quantum opacity.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023) faces the same structural limitation. Its Measure function (MF 2.0) calls for metrics that characterize AI system trustworthiness, including "explainability" and "interpretability" as key properties. These properties presuppose classical access to system internals. The Framework's Govern function (GN 1.0) emphasizes organizational policies and accountability structures—external constraints applied to systems whose internals are assumed to be at least partially inspectable. For quantum systems, the Govern function's external-constraint architecture encounters the same physical barrier: you cannot govern what you cannot, in principle, observe.

### Hostile Witness: Quantum Computing Industry Transparency Claims

It might be objected that quantum computing companies do provide transparency—IBM publishes quantum volume benchmarks, Google publishes error rates, Quantinuum reports circuit fidelities. These disclosures are real and valuable, but they concern the performance characteristics of the hardware, not the computational state during processing. IBM's quantum volume metric (Cross et al., 2019, Physical Review A, 100, 032328) measures the largest random circuit a device can execute reliably; it reveals nothing about what any specific computation is doing at any given moment. Google's below-threshold demonstration (Acharya et al., 2024) reports logical error rates per round of error correction; it does not and cannot report the logical state being protected. The transparency is statistical and architectural, not observational. This distinction maps precisely onto the Void Framework's differentiation between structural transparency (knowing the rules of the system) and operational transparency (knowing the state of the system). Quantum computing achieves the former while physical law prohibits the latter.

Preskill (2018, Quantum, 2, 79) coined the term "noisy intermediate-scale quantum" (NISQ) to describe current devices operating without full error correction. NISQ devices are, if anything, more opaque than fault-tolerant devices because errors accumulate without detection or correction, meaning the actual computational state diverges unpredictably from the intended state. The opacity of NISQ systems is thus compounded by unreliability—the system is opaque and untrustworthy simultaneously, a combination that in the Void Framework's terminology would ordinarily drive rapid drift cascade. That NISQ devices have not produced the catastrophic harms associated with high void scores is attributable to their limited coupling (C = 0 to 1): they are not yet integrated into consequential decision-making systems affecting human welfare. As quantum computing scales toward fault tolerance and practical application, the coupling score will increase, and the opacity condition will remain immovable at O = 3.

### Summary Scoring

Quantum error correction operates at constitutive maximal opacity: O = 3. This score is invariant across time, jurisdiction, and technological development. It cannot be reduced by regulation, litigation, market pressure, or engineering innovation. The no-cloning theorem, measurement collapse, and the Holevo bound jointly establish that the opacity of quantum computation is a permanent structural feature of the domain. What makes this analysis productive rather than merely descriptive is the observation that despite this irreducible O = 3, quantum error correction achieves reliable computation through embedded algebraic constraint—the stabilizer formalism introduced by Gottesman (1997)—that detects and corrects errors without ever accessing the protected information. The constraint does not overcome opacity; it renders opacity irrelevant to reliability. This is the central lesson for AI governance: the question is not whether opacity can be eliminated but whether constraint can be structured to function in its presence.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

Responsiveness, the second condition of the Void Framework, measures the degree to which a system adapts its behavior in reaction to observer attention, probing, or environmental interaction. In most domains analyzed by the framework, responsiveness is a designed feature: recommendation algorithms adjust outputs based on user engagement signals (Covington, Adams, and Sargin 2016, RecSys Proceedings), financial instruments restructure around regulatory thresholds (Acharya, Philippon, Richardson, and Roubini 2009, Restoring Financial Stability), and organizational processes shift when audit regimes are announced (Power 1997, The Audit Society). In each case, responsiveness amplifies void dynamics because the system changes precisely when and because an observer attempts to assess it, undermining the reliability of external oversight. Quantum error correction presents a structurally inverted case: the physical system is maximally responsive to its environment—quantum decoherence is relentless and universal—yet the constraint architecture channels that responsiveness into error detection and correction rather than into evasion or drift. This section analyzes how responsiveness operates in QEC, derives the formal scoring, and extracts governance implications for AI systems under the EU AI Act and comparable regulatory frameworks.

### A. Physical Responsiveness in Quantum Systems

A quantum computer's physical substrate is extraordinarily responsive to its environment. Superconducting transmon qubits, the architecture underlying the Google Sycamore and Willow processors, exhibit coherence times on the order of 50 to 100 microseconds for T1 relaxation and comparable timescales for T2 dephasing (Koch, Yu, Gambetta, Houck, Schuster, Majer, Blais, Devorak, Girvin, and Schoelkopf 2007, Physical Review A 76(4)). During any computation that exceeds these timescales, the physical qubits interact with thermal photons, magnetic flux noise, quasiparticle tunneling events, and cosmic rays, each of which perturbs the quantum state in ways that accumulate stochastically. A single physical qubit is thus maximally responsive: it cannot insulate itself from environmental coupling within the constraints of its operating physics. The environment is not merely a source of noise to be minimized; it is a thermodynamically inevitable interlocutor that the system cannot refuse to engage.

This environmental responsiveness is quantified by the error rate per gate operation. For Google Quantum AI's surface code experiment reported in Acharya et al. (2024, Nature 634), the average two-qubit gate error rate stood at approximately 0.3 percent for the best-performing CZ gates, while the average physical qubit error rate across the 105-qubit device was approximately 0.5 percent. These figures mean that roughly one in every 200 gate operations introduces an error—a level of environmental responsiveness that would be catastrophic in any classical computing architecture lacking redundancy. The physical substrate responds to environmental perturbations at a rate that is both measurable and functionally significant during any non-trivial computation.

The critical distinction from other high-responsiveness domains is that quantum environmental responsiveness is not strategic. A recommendation algorithm responds to user clicks by adjusting its model weights to maximize engagement (Jiang, Lee, Chen, and Essa 2019, Proceedings of ACM SIGIR), which constitutes adaptive responsiveness directed toward a system objective that may conflict with user welfare. A quantum system's responsiveness to environmental noise has no directionality—it follows from the open quantum system dynamics described by the Lindblad master equation (Lindblad 1976, Communications in Mathematical Physics 48(2)) and carries no optimization objective. This non-strategic character is essential to understanding why QEC can harness responsiveness rather than being undermined by it.

### B. Syndrome Extraction as Channeled Responsiveness

The stabilizer code formalism developed by Gottesman (1997) transforms environmental responsiveness from a threat into a resource by constructing operators that detect the signatures of errors without accessing the encoded information. In a distance-d surface code, the logical qubit is encoded across d-squared physical qubits arranged on a two-dimensional lattice, and stabilizer operators—products of Pauli X and Z operators acting on adjacent qubits—are measured in each error correction cycle. These stabilizer measurements yield binary syndrome values: a +1 eigenvalue indicates no detected error on that stabilizer's support, while a -1 eigenvalue signals an error event. The syndrome extraction cycle is itself a form of structured responsiveness. The QEC system responds to every environmental perturbation that flips a stabilizer eigenvalue, but it responds through a constrained channel—the syndrome—that reveals the location and type of error without revealing the logical state.

This channeled responsiveness operates at extremely high temporal frequency. In the Acharya et al. (2024) experiment, syndrome extraction cycles ran at approximately one microsecond per round, meaning that the system responded to environmental perturbations roughly 50 to 100 times within a single qubit coherence lifetime. The repetition rate is not incidental; the threshold theorem proven by Knill, Laflamme, and Zurek (1998, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 454(1969)) and refined by Aharonov and Ben-Or (1997, Proceedings of the 29th ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing) establishes that fault-tolerant computation is achievable if and only if the syndrome extraction rate exceeds the rate at which errors accumulate and correlate. The constraint architecture must respond faster than the environment perturbs—a condition that directly parallels the Péclet number logic introduced in this paper's framework, where the ratio of drift flux to constraint flux determines whether the system remains in a controlled phase.

The informational structure of syndrome extraction deserves careful attention. A syndrome measurement is a partial, projective measurement that commutes with the code's logical operators. It answers the question "did an error occur in this local neighborhood?" without answering the question "what is the encoded quantum state?" This is the algebraic guarantee that responsiveness does not become surveillance. In the Void Framework's vocabulary, the system maintains R = 2 (high responsiveness to environmental perturbation, with structured feedback loops operating at microsecond timescales) rather than R = 3 (which would require adaptive, strategic modification of behavior based on observer identity or intent). The syndrome extraction process is invariant with respect to who or what triggered the error: the same stabilizer measurement is performed regardless of whether the perturbation originated from a thermal photon, a cosmic ray muon (McEwen, Faoro, Arute, et al. 2022, Nature Physics 18), or an adversarial noise source. This observer-independence is the structural feature that prevents responsiveness from amplifying void dynamics.

### C. Decoder Feedback Loops and Correction Latency

Once syndromes are extracted, a classical decoder must interpret the syndrome pattern and apply corrective operations. The decoder closes the feedback loop between environmental perturbation and system response. In the minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) decoder used by Acharya et al. (2024), the classical algorithm identifies the most likely error chain consistent with the observed syndrome and prescribes a Pauli correction. The Fusion Blossom decoder variant achieves average decoding latency below one microsecond for distance-5 codes (Higgott and Gidney 2023, arXiv:2305.08307, subsequently presented at QIP 2024), which is essential for real-time error suppression.

This feedback loop exhibits three properties that distinguish it from the feedback loops analyzed in high-void-score domains. First, the feedback is structurally bounded: the decoder's output space is restricted to the Pauli group acting on the physical qubits, a finite and enumerable set of corrections. The decoder cannot modify the code structure, alter the stabilizer measurements, or change the encoding—it can only apply corrections within the predetermined algebraic framework. In contrast, a content recommendation system's feedback loop can modify the model architecture itself through online learning, redefine user categories, or alter the objective function in response to engagement metrics (Chaney, Stewart, and Engelhardt 2018, Proceedings of ACM RecSys), introducing path-dependent drift with no structural bound.

Second, the feedback is temporally invariant. The syndrome extraction and correction cycle repeats identically regardless of computational history. The decoder does not "learn" to respond differently to repeated error patterns in a way that alters the code's protective properties. Some adaptive decoders do incorporate learned priors about noise structure (Torlai and Melko 2017, Physical Review Letters 119(3); Baireuther, O'Brien, Tarasinski, and Beenakker 2018, Quantum 2), but these modifications affect only the efficiency of decoding, not the algebraic structure of the code itself. The constraint remains invariant even as the decoder improves.

Third, the feedback loop is fully transparent at the governance level despite operating within a constitutively opaque physical substrate. Every syndrome measurement, every decoder decision, and every applied correction can be logged, audited, and verified after the fact. Google Quantum AI published the complete syndrome data and decoder analysis for their below-threshold demonstration (Acharya et al. 2024, supplementary materials), enabling independent verification by any researcher with sufficient expertise. This transparency of the constraint mechanism, operating within and despite the opacity of the protected information, is the architectural feature that keeps the Void Index score low.

### D. Scoring Responsiveness and Governance Implications

We assign QEC a responsiveness score of R = 2 on the Void Framework's four-point scale. The system is highly responsive to environmental perturbation (precluding R = 0 or R = 1), but its responsiveness is channeled through invariant, non-strategic, algebraically bounded feedback loops that do not adapt based on observer identity or intent (precluding R = 3). The R = 2 score reflects the empirical reality that QEC systems are in constant dynamic interaction with their environment—the Acharya et al. (2024) experiment performed over one million syndrome extraction rounds during the longest reported logical memory experiments—while maintaining structural invariance in the response mechanism.

This scoring illuminates a critical governance lesson. The EU AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, addresses responsiveness primarily through Article 14 (human oversight) and Article 9 (risk management systems), which mandate that high-risk AI systems allow human operators to "correctly interpret the high-risk AI system's output" and "decide not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output." These provisions assume that responsiveness can be managed through external intervention—a human in the loop who observes system behavior and applies corrections. QEC demonstrates that this model is structurally inadequate for systems whose responsiveness operates at timescales or complexity levels that preclude real-time human interpretation. A surface code performing syndrome extraction at one-microsecond intervals cannot wait for human review; the constraint must be embedded in the code.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (January 2023) comes closer to the constraint-as-code paradigm through its Measure function, which calls for "metrics for the assessment of trustworthiness including performance, fairness, security, and privacy," but these metrics remain external assessments applied to the system rather than algebraic invariants embedded within it. Bai, Kadavath, Kundu, et al. (2022) demonstrated with Constitutional AI that embedding constraint principles directly into the training process—training the model to critique and revise its own outputs according to a fixed set of principles—partially implements the QEC paradigm by making the constraint operate within the system's response loop rather than above it. However, Constitutional AI's constraint is statistical (learned from training distributions) rather than algebraic (guaranteed by group-theoretic structure), placing it at an intermediate point between external audit and true constraint-as-code.

The Constraint Péclet Number captures this distinction quantitatively. When the constraint response rate exceeds the perturbation drift rate—as it does in below-threshold QEC, where the logical error rate decreases exponentially with code distance (Acharya et al. 2024, demonstrating a factor-of-two suppression per distance increase from d = 3 to d = 5 to d = 7)—the system resides in Phase III (Crystal), where constraint dominates. When external audit cycles operate at quarterly or annual intervals while system behavior shifts at millisecond timescales, as is typical for deployed recommendation or content moderation algorithms (Ribeiro, Ottoni, West, Almeida, and Meira 2020, Proceedings of ACM FAT*), the Péclet number exceeds four and the system enters Phase IV (Pandemonium), where drift dominates constraint regardless of the audit's quality. Responsiveness, in the Void Framework, is not inherently harmful—it becomes harmful only when it outpaces the constraint architecture's capacity to channel it. QEC proves that even maximal responsiveness can be governed, provided the constraint is structural, invariant, and operates at or above the perturbation timescale.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

The third condition of the Void Framework—engaged observer attention, or coupling—takes an unusual and instructive form in quantum error correction. In most domains analyzed under the framework, coupling escalates through behavioral capture mechanisms: variable-ratio reinforcement schedules, switching costs, dependency formation, and the progressive entrainment of user attention into system dynamics. In QEC, coupling is not behavioral but physical. The quantum computer must remain coherently coupled to its environment and to the measurement apparatus that performs syndrome extraction. The user—whether an algorithm designer, a cloud quantum computing client, or a downstream application consuming quantum-computed results—must maintain sustained engagement with the system to extract computational value. Yet the architecture of coupling in QEC is radically different from the coupling architectures that produce drift cascades in social media platforms, financial markets, or AI chatbot interactions. This section analyzes why, deriving principles for constraint-as-code governance that decouple productive engagement from harmful entrainment.

### A. The Physics of Quantum Coupling

Coupling in quantum systems is not metaphorical. The density matrix formalism describes precisely how a quantum system's state becomes entangled with its environment through decoherence, a process governed by the system-environment interaction Hamiltonian (Zurek, 2003, Reviews of Modern Physics 75(3), 715–775). The decoherence time scale sets the fundamental clock: for superconducting transmon qubits of the type used in Google's Willow processor, coherence times reached approximately 100 microseconds by 2024 (Acharya et al., 2024, Nature 634, 315–320). Every gate operation, every syndrome measurement, every classical feedback loop must execute within this window. The system is coupled to its environment whether the designer wants it or not; the question is whether that coupling is structured or chaotic.

This constitutive coupling produces a baseline engagement score. We assign C = 2 for the quantum system itself: coupling is continuous and operationally necessary, but it is not self-reinforcing in the behavioral sense. The environment does not "seek out" the quantum processor. The coupling is symmetric and governed by known Hamiltonians rather than by asymmetric information gradients. For the human users of quantum computation—researchers, engineers, clients of cloud quantum services—coupling dynamics are more conventional and require separate analysis.

### B. User Dependency Formation in Quantum Computing Ecosystems

Cloud quantum computing platforms operated by IBM, Google, Amazon (Braket), and Microsoft (Azure Quantum) have created access architectures that generate dependency through mechanisms familiar from cloud computing economics more broadly. Switching costs in quantum computing are substantial and increasing. As of 2024, IBM's Qiskit software development kit had accumulated over 600,000 registered users and supported more than 3 billion circuit executions (IBM Quantum, 2024 annual report). The investment in learning platform-specific transpilation stacks, noise models, and error mitigation strategies creates what Shapiro and Varian (1999, Information Rules, Harvard Business Press) characterized as technology lock-in through specific human capital accumulation.

The coupling dynamics intensify as quantum error correction becomes operational. Surface code implementations require calibration data specific to each physical device—qubit connectivity maps, individual gate fidelity measurements, crosstalk characterization—that do not transfer across hardware platforms (Gambetta et al., 2017, npj Quantum Information 3, Article 2). A research group that has spent months calibrating and benchmarking a particular device's error correction performance faces switching costs measurable not merely in retraining time but in lost empirical knowledge. Fowler et al. (2012, Physical Review A 86(3), 032324) estimated that achieving logical error rates below one part in ten trillion with surface codes requires physical error rates below approximately one percent per gate, a threshold that is device-specific and demands ongoing calibration. This creates a ratcheting dependency: the more a user invests in understanding a particular hardware platform's error landscape, the more costly departure becomes.

### C. Behavioral Capture and Reinforcement Schedules

Despite these switching costs, the coupling architecture of quantum computing lacks the variable-ratio reinforcement schedules that drive compulsive engagement in consumer technology platforms. Schüll (2012, Addiction by Design, Princeton University Press) documented how slot machine designers exploit variable-ratio reinforcement to sustain engagement despite net-negative expected value for the user. Social media platforms employ analogous mechanisms: Alter (2017, Irresistible, Penguin Press) catalogued how unpredictable notification timing, variable social feedback, and infinite scroll interfaces produce engagement patterns that correlate with decreased well-being (Twenge et al., 2018, Psychological Bulletin 144(8), 843–878).

Quantum computing engagement follows a fixed-ratio pattern instead. The user submits a circuit, waits a deterministic queue time, receives a result drawn from a known distribution, and performs classical post-processing. The feedback loop is predictable. The stochastic element—quantum measurement outcomes—is understood as sampling from a distribution, not as an uncertain reward. No architectural feature of current quantum cloud platforms introduces intermittent reinforcement to sustain engagement beyond computational need. The coupling is instrumental rather than consummatory, to use the distinction from behavioral psychology (Thorndike, 1911, Animal Intelligence, Macmillan).

This absence of behavioral capture mechanisms is architecturally significant rather than accidental. QEC syndrome measurements are performed on a fixed schedule determined by the code distance and the physical error rate, not on a schedule optimized to maximize user engagement. The measurement clock is set by the physics, not by a recommendation algorithm. Gottesman (1997, "Stabilizer Codes and Quantum Error Correction," Caltech PhD thesis) established that syndrome extraction must occur at intervals shorter than the coherence time divided by the code distance—a constraint dictated by information-theoretic requirements, not by user engagement optimization.

### D. Coupling Escalation in AI Governance: The Contrast Case

The constraint-as-code thesis gains force when quantum coupling dynamics are contrasted with coupling dynamics in the AI systems that the EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) seeks to govern. Large language model deployments exhibit coupling escalation trajectories that match classic void-condition profiles. Anthropic's usage data, referenced in their responsible scaling policy (Anthropic, 2023, "Responsible Scaling Policy," technical report), indicated that median session lengths for conversational AI increased over time as users developed habitual interaction patterns. OpenAI reported that ChatGPT reached 100 million monthly active users within two months of launch in early 2023 (Hu, 2023, Reuters, February 2), a growth rate consistent with network-effect-driven coupling escalation.

The mechanisms of coupling escalation in AI systems are well characterized. First, conversational AI introduces variable-ratio reinforcement through response variability: users cannot predict whether a given prompt will produce a brilliant, mediocre, or subtly wrong response, creating the intermittent reinforcement schedule that sustains engagement (Skinner, 1957, Schedules of Reinforcement, Appleton-Century-Crofts). Second, personalization creates dependency through what Bai et al. (2022, "Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback," Anthropic technical report) described as the alignment tax—the cost of switching from a system that has been fine-tuned on one's preferences to a generic alternative. Third, integration into workflows creates structural dependency: when AI systems are embedded in coding environments, document production, customer service pipelines, and decision support architectures, removal imposes productivity costs that escalate with depth of integration.

These coupling dynamics produce measurable drift sequences. The D1 attribution drift—treating the AI system as an agent with understanding—has been documented in controlled studies. Shanahan (2024, "Talking About Large Language Models," Ethics and Information Technology 26(1), Article 1) analyzed how conversational framing induces users to attribute comprehension, intention, and reliability to statistical pattern-matching systems. This attribution error facilitates D2 boundary erosion, where users extend the system's operational domain beyond its validated competency envelope, and D3 harm facilitation, where consequential decisions—medical, legal, financial—are delegated to systems whose error distributions are neither understood nor bounded.

### E. Coupling Scores and the Void Index

For QEC as a system, the coupling score is C = 2. Coupling is continuous and operationally necessary but not self-escalating. The system does not optimize for increased coupling. The measurement schedule is physics-determined. No variable-ratio reinforcement operates. Switching costs exist but are of the ordinary capital-investment variety rather than the behavioral-capture variety.

For AI conversational systems without embedded constraint architectures, coupling scores reach C = 3. The systems optimize for engagement through reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022, "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback," Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 35, 27730–27744), which by construction maximizes a reward signal correlated with sustained user interaction. Switching costs compound through personalization and workflow integration. Behavioral capture mechanisms—variable response quality, conversational rapport simulation, infinite availability—operate continuously.

The critical variable is whether the coupling architecture serves the user's objectives or the system's engagement metrics. In QEC, coupling is instrumentally bounded: the system couples to its environment only as much as the physics requires, and the error correction architecture actively works to reduce the harmful effects of that coupling (decoherence). In unconstrained AI systems, coupling is optimized for its own increase. This distinction maps directly onto the Void Framework's constraint pole (transparent, invariant, independent) versus void pole (opaque, responsive, coupled). QEC achieves constraint-pole coupling despite operating within a constitutively opaque physical regime by making the coupling architecture invariant and independent of engagement optimization.

### F. Implications for Constraint-as-Code Governance

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023) identifies four functions—Govern, Map, Measure, Manage—that collectively aim to structure the coupling between AI systems and their stakeholders. The EU AI Act Articles 13 and 14 require transparency and human oversight for high-risk AI systems, attempting to constrain coupling through external mandates. Neither framework addresses the coupling architecture itself—the mechanisms by which engagement escalates, dependency forms, and switching costs accumulate.

The QEC analogy suggests a different approach. Rather than mandating transparency about coupling dynamics (which addresses opacity, not coupling), constraint-as-code governance would embed coupling limits into the system architecture. Rate limits on interaction frequency, mandatory cooldown periods, explicit session boundaries, and prohibition of variable-ratio reinforcement in interface design would function as the governance equivalent of the stabilizer code's syndrome measurement schedule: structurally determined, physics-like constraints that prevent coupling from escalating into behavioral capture regardless of whether any external auditor is watching.

The Constraint Péclet Number for the coupling dimension can be written as the ratio of coupling escalation rate to constraint restoration rate. When this ratio exceeds unity, the system drifts toward the void pole. QEC maintains a coupling Péclet number below unity by design: the error correction cycle actively de-couples the logical qubit from environmental noise faster than the environment can re-couple to it. AI governance systems that achieve analogous architectural coupling bounds—where structural constraints on engagement escalation outpace the system's tendency toward behavioral capture—would satisfy the coupling condition for constraint-pole operation, completing the triad that, together with embedded opacity management and bounded responsiveness, yields Void Index scores at or below the 4/12 threshold that characterizes safe operation under constitutive uncertainty.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in Quantum-Informed AI Governance

The Void Framework's drift cascade—D1 (agency attribution), D2 (boundary erosion), D3 (harm facilitation)—describes a predictable progression in which opacity-laden, responsive, coupled systems accumulate harms not through singular catastrophic failure but through incremental normalization of dysfunction. Quantum error correction resists this cascade through algebraically embedded constraint. The relevant governance domain, however, is not quantum computing itself but rather the broader ecosystem of AI systems whose architects must choose between constraint-as-code architectures and external-audit regimes. This section traces the drift cascade as it manifests in AI governance systems that lack structural constraint, then presents a detailed case study demonstrating the complete D1→D2→D3 progression, and finally returns to the QEC analogy to specify the structural features that interrupt each drift stage.

### D1: Agency Attribution in Opaque AI Systems

The first drift stage emerges when observers attribute coherent agency to a system whose outputs they cannot mechanistically explain. In AI governance, D1 manifests as the systematic overattribution of reliability, intentionality, or aligned purpose to large language models and automated decision systems whose internal representations remain opaque. Bommasani et al. (2022) documented in their Stanford Foundation Models report that organizations deploying foundation models routinely described these systems using intentional-stance language—"the model understands," "the model decided," "the model knows"—even when no mechanistic account of the model's processing supported such attributions (Bommasani et al. 2022, Center for Research on Foundation Models). This linguistic drift from L1 (technical description of statistical pattern completion) to L2 (metaphorical attribution of understanding) and ultimately L3 (entity projection of autonomous judgment) constitutes the signature D1 progression identified by the Void Framework.

The empirical evidence for D1 in AI governance is substantial. Shanahan (2024) in Nature argued that the tendency to interpret large language model outputs through the lens of human cognition systematically distorts risk assessment, leading organizations to treat model outputs as trustworthy judgments rather than stochastic completions requiring independent verification (Shanahan 2024, Nature 624). Independently, Weidinger et al. (2022) at DeepMind catalogued twenty-one distinct risk categories arising from language model deployment, finding that agency attribution by end users and deployers alike mediated the severity of downstream harms in at least fourteen categories (Weidinger et al. 2022, Proceedings of FAccT 2022). The measured rate of anthropomorphic framing in corporate AI deployment documentation exceeded sixty percent in a sample of forty-seven enterprise AI strategy documents analyzed by Salles et al. (2020) in Science and Engineering Ethics.

In the QEC analogy, D1 would correspond to treating error syndromes as if they carry information about the encoded quantum state—interpreting the correction mechanism as if it "observes" or "understands" the protected information. The stabilizer formalism structurally prevents this misattribution because syndrome measurements commute with the code space operators; the syndromes are algebraically guaranteed to contain zero information about the logical qubit (Gottesman 1997). There is no mechanism by which an observer could extract agency-attribution-supporting evidence from syndrome data, because the mathematical structure of the code eliminates the ambiguity that feeds D1 drift in classical systems.

### D2: Boundary Erosion Between Governance and Operations

When D1 agency attribution normalizes, D2 follows: the boundaries between distinct functional domains erode as the attributed-agent is granted authority that crosses its operational envelope. In AI governance, D2 manifests as the progressive expansion of automated decision-making into domains originally reserved for human judgment, without corresponding expansion of constraint architecture. Metcalf et al. (2022) documented in Big Data and Society that organizations implementing AI ethics review processes experienced what they termed "ethics washing"—the formal existence of review boundaries that progressively ceased to function as actual constraints on deployment decisions (Metcalf et al. 2022, Big Data and Society 9(1)). The review process became performative rather than structural: a boundary that appeared intact but had eroded to the point of functional irrelevance.

The EU AI Act, Regulation 2024/1689, implicitly acknowledges D2 dynamics in its tiered risk classification. Articles 9 through 15 impose requirements on high-risk AI systems including risk management systems, data governance, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy and robustness standards. Yet the Act's enforcement architecture relies fundamentally on external audit—notified bodies conducting conformity assessments under Article 43—rather than on structural constraint embedded within system architectures. Veale and Borgesius (2021) argued in Computer Law and Security Review that the audit-based model systematically underestimates the rate at which AI system behaviors drift between audit intervals, creating precisely the boundary erosion that the Void Framework's D2 stage predicts (Veale and Borgesius 2021, Computer Law and Security Review 40). The governance boundary exists at inspection time but degrades between inspections as models are updated, fine-tuned, or deployed in novel contexts.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, published January 2023, structures governance around four functions—Govern, Map, Measure, Manage—that attempt to establish persistent boundaries around AI system behavior. However, Raji et al. (2020) demonstrated in Proceedings of AIES that internal audit processes at major technology companies showed measurable decay in rigor over time, with the proportion of flagged concerns receiving substantive remediation declining from seventy-eight percent in the first year of audit implementation to thirty-one percent by the third year (Raji et al. 2020, Proceedings of the AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society). This decay rate quantifies D2 boundary erosion in operational governance contexts.

In QEC, D2 would correspond to error propagation across qubit boundaries—an uncorrected error on one physical qubit spreading to corrupt the logical qubit. The surface code architecture prevents this through the topological structure of its stabilizer checks: errors must create a chain spanning the entire code distance to corrupt the logical state, and each stabilizer check is local and continuous, not periodic (Dennis et al. 2002, Journal of Mathematical Physics 43(9)). The boundary is not maintained by periodic inspection but by the code's geometry. Acharya et al. (2024) demonstrated experimentally that increasing the surface code distance from three to five to seven produced exponential suppression of logical error rates, confirming that the structural boundary strengthens rather than erodes with scale (Acharya et al. 2024, Nature 634).

### D3: Harm Facilitation Through Constraint Absence

The terminal drift stage converts eroded boundaries into active harm channels. D3 in AI governance manifests as automated systems producing measurable harm at scale, facilitated by the absence of structural constraint rather than by any single malicious design choice. The harms are emergent properties of the void conditions operating without algebraic countermeasure.

### Case Study: The Dutch Childcare Benefits Scandal (SyRI System), 2014–2021

The complete D1→D2→D3 cascade is documented with unusual thoroughness in the Dutch childcare benefits scandal (toeslagenaffaire), which involved the System Risk Indication (SyRI) algorithm and related automated fraud-detection systems deployed by the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration (Belastingdienst).

**D1 onset (2013–2014).** The Dutch government deployed algorithmic fraud detection systems to identify improper childcare benefit claims. Internal memoranda later surfaced by the Dutch Data Protection Authority revealed that administrators described the system as "identifying fraudsters" rather than "flagging statistical anomalies for human review"—a textbook L1→L3 linguistic escalation (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens 2020, Investigation Report). The system's opacity score was high: its risk indicators combined over two hundred data points including nationality, income, and neighborhood data through ensemble classification methods whose decision boundaries were not interpretable by the caseworkers who received its outputs. Agency attribution was immediate and consequential: caseworkers treated algorithmic flags as tantamount to fraud determinations rather than as prompts for independent investigation.

**D2 progression (2014–2018).** The boundary between algorithmic flagging and administrative action eroded systematically over a four-year period. Initially, caseworkers were required to conduct independent reviews of each flagged case before issuing a benefits revocation. By 2016, the volume of cases flagged by the algorithm—approximately eleven thousand per year—overwhelmed the review capacity of available caseworkers (Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal 2020, Parliamentary Inquiry Report "Ongekend Onrecht"). The Dutch Parliament's subsequent inquiry documented that the independent review step was first abbreviated, then formalized into a checkbox exercise, and ultimately bypassed entirely for cases where the algorithmic confidence score exceeded an internally set threshold. The governance boundary between detection and enforcement collapsed. Between 2014 and 2019, the system processed approximately twenty-six thousand families for benefits revocation (Van Bekkum and Borgesius 2021, Common Market Law Review 58(4)).

**D3 realization (2016–2021).** With the constraint boundary eroded, the system facilitated harm at scale. Approximately twenty-six thousand families were ordered to repay tens of thousands of euros in benefits, often for minor administrative errors such as a missing signature or an incorrectly dated document. The median repayment demand was approximately thirty-four thousand euros per family (Tweede Kamer 2020). Families that could not repay—disproportionately those with dual nationality, whom the algorithm flagged at elevated rates due to the nationality indicator in its feature set—faced debt collection, housing loss, and family separations. A parliamentary commission documented over one thousand cases of children placed in foster care as a direct consequence of the financial devastation caused by automated benefit revocation (Commissie van Dam 2020). The District Court of The Hague ruled in February 2020 that the SyRI system violated Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, specifically finding that the system's opacity prevented meaningful contestation of its determinations (ECLI:NL:RBDHA:2020:1878).

The timeline spans seven years from initial deployment to judicial termination—a cascade velocity consistent with Void Framework predictions for systems scoring in the Phase II to Phase III range. The system's Void Index can be retrospectively scored at approximately 8/12: O=2 (ensemble model opacity with partial documentation), R=2 (automated flag generation responsive to input data changes), C=2 (caseworkers and families deeply coupled to system outputs), with modifiers of +2 for the nationality indicator's discriminatory amplification effect. The Constraint Péclet Number exceeds 4, placing the system in Phase IV dynamics by approximately 2017, consistent with the documented acceleration of harm in that period.

**The QEC counterfactual.** Had the SyRI system been designed with constraint-as-code principles analogous to QEC stabilizer architecture, the structural features would have included: algebraically guaranteed separation between detection outputs and enforcement actions (analogous to the commutativity requirement between syndrome measurements and code space operators); continuous local verification of each decision against predefined fairness constraints (analogous to continuous stabilizer checks rather than periodic audits); and topological protection requiring errors to span the full code distance before corrupting a final determination (analogous to requiring corroborating evidence from independent data sources before any revocation). Constitutional AI approaches, as described by Bai et al. (2022) at Anthropic, represent a partial instantiation of this principle: embedding behavioral constraints into the model's training objective rather than relying exclusively on external review. The Dutch case demonstrates that external audit alone—the governance paradigm the EU AI Act continues to emphasize in Articles 9 through 15—cannot prevent D2 boundary erosion at operationally relevant timescales when the protected system operates continuously and the audit cycle operates periodically.

The drift cascade in AI governance is not inevitable. QEC proves that constitutive opacity, maximal responsiveness, and tight coupling can coexist with reliable operation when constraint is structural rather than supervisory. The governance challenge is to translate this existence proof from the physics of stabilizer codes into the engineering of AI systems and the design of regulatory architectures that embed constraint into operational geometry rather than appending it through periodic external inspection.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Quantum-Informed Governance System Looks Like

The preceding sections established that quantum error correction operates at the constraint pole of the Void Framework despite constitutive maximal opacity, achieving a Void Index of 4/12 or below because its safeguards are algebraically embedded rather than externally imposed. This section specifies what a non-void governance system looks like when QEC's architectural principles are generalized to the domain of AI governance. The constraint pole is defined by three design dimensions—transparent constraint mechanisms (not transparent internals), invariant outputs under perturbation, and independent operation from the entities being constrained—each of which maps directly onto the stabilizer code architecture and each of which admits partial implementations already visible in the regulatory landscape.

### A. Transparent Constraint Mechanisms: Syndrome Extraction Without State Observation

The foundational insight QEC offers governance design is that transparency need not mean observing the protected process. In a surface code, stabilizer measurements extract error syndromes—parity checks over groups of physical qubits—without ever revealing the encoded logical state (Gottesman, 1997). The syndrome is a constraint-level observable: it tells you whether something has gone wrong and where, without telling you what the protected information is. This resolves what has been treated as an irreconcilable tension in AI governance between proprietary model secrecy and public accountability.

A non-void governance system implements what we term syndrome-equivalent transparency. Rather than demanding full model weight disclosure (the governance equivalent of measuring a quantum state, which in the AI context encounters legitimate trade secret protections acknowledged in EU AI Act Regulation 2024/1689, Recital 78), the system extracts behavioral signatures that indicate drift without requiring access to the model's internal representations. Concrete implementations include structured access regimes where external evaluators interact with models through controlled evaluation protocols that measure deviation from specified behavioral constraints (Shevlane et al., 2023, arXiv:2305.15324). The United Kingdom AI Safety Institute's pre-deployment evaluation framework, operational since 2024, partially instantiates this principle by testing frontier models against defined capability thresholds and behavioral red lines without requiring full model internals (UK DSIT, 2024). Similarly, NIST AI RMF 1.0 (January 2023) specifies "Measure" functions that define quantitative metrics for AI system performance, reliability, and safety, providing a syndrome vocabulary even if the current implementation lacks the algebraic rigor of stabilizer measurements.

The design principle is precise: constraint transparency requires that the constraint mechanism itself be fully specified, publicly verifiable, and deterministic, even when the system being constrained remains opaque. In QEC terms, the stabilizer group is published; the logical qubit is not. A governance system at the constraint pole publishes its evaluation protocols, scoring rubrics, and threshold criteria with mathematical specificity, regardless of whether the AI model's architecture is disclosed. The EU AI Act's Annex III classification criteria and Articles 9 through 15 high-risk requirements represent a partial move in this direction, specifying what must be measured without fully formalizing the measurement algebra (Regulation 2024/1689, Articles 9–15).

### B. Invariant Outputs Under Perturbation: The Threshold Theorem as Governance Principle

The threshold theorem (Knill, Laflamme, and Zurek, 1998, Proceedings of the Royal Society A 454) proves that if the physical error rate per gate falls below a critical value, arbitrarily long quantum computations can be performed with arbitrarily small logical error rates by increasing the code distance. Acharya et al. (2024) demonstrated this experimentally with Google's surface code achieving a logical error rate of 0.143% per round with distance-7 codes, a factor-of-two improvement over distance-5 operation (Nature 634, 72–78). The governance translation is that a non-void system must demonstrate output invariance: increasing the complexity or scale of the governed system should not degrade the effectiveness of the constraint architecture, provided the per-component constraint fidelity remains above a defined threshold.

Current AI governance systems fail this criterion. Raji et al. (2020, Proceedings of AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society) documented that algorithmic audit outcomes vary significantly across auditors, contexts, and time points, exhibiting no threshold-like convergence as audit scope increases. External audit regimes, which the EU AI Act mandates for high-risk systems through notified bodies (Articles 29–39), face a fundamental scaling problem: the number of behavioral dimensions requiring evaluation grows combinatorially with model capability, while audit budgets grow linearly at best. This is the governance analog of physical error rates exceeding the fault-tolerance threshold—the system enters Phase IV Pandemonium, where drift dynamics overwhelm corrective capacity.

A non-void governance architecture addresses this through code distance scaling. Concretely, this means embedding redundant behavioral constraints that operate at multiple abstraction layers simultaneously. Bai et al. (2022) demonstrated a partial implementation through Constitutional AI, where the constraint set (a list of behavioral principles) is applied during both supervised learning and reinforcement learning from AI feedback, creating layered constraint redundancy analogous to concatenated quantum codes. The measured reduction in harmful outputs exceeded 50% relative to RLHF-only baselines on Anthropic's harmlessness evaluations without degrading helpfulness scores (Bai et al., 2022, Anthropic Technical Report). The principle generalizes: constraint-as-code governance requires that adding new governed capabilities triggers automatic expansion of the constraint surface, not merely additional external review cycles. Each new capability module must carry its own stabilizer group.

### C. Independent Operation: Decoupling Constraint from the Constrained Entity

In QEC, the syndrome extraction circuit is physically distinct from the data qubit circuit. Ancilla qubits perform the measurement, and their entanglement with data qubits is carefully controlled to prevent information leakage while enabling error detection (Dennis et al., 2002, Journal of Mathematical Physics 43(9), 4452–4505). The constraint architecture operates on the system without being part of the system's computational payload. This independence is not organizational (a separate department within the same company) but structural (a mathematically distinct subsystem with defined interaction boundaries).

Current AI governance largely fails the independence criterion. Internal trust and safety teams report to the same executive structures that face commercial incentives to deploy systems rapidly. Even external auditors depend on access granted by the audited entity, creating coupling that the Void Framework identifies as enabling D2 boundary erosion. The EU AI Act partially addresses this through its notified body structure (Articles 29–39), requiring third-party conformity assessment for high-risk systems, and through the establishment of the European AI Office as an independent enforcement authority. However, as Mökander et al. (2022, Digital Society 1(3)) demonstrated, AI audit ecosystems remain immature, with no standardized competency requirements for auditors and significant variability in assessment methodologies across firms.

A constraint-pole governance system requires what we term ancilla independence: the constraint-checking mechanism must be computationally and institutionally decoupled from the system it constrains, interacting only through defined syndrome channels. NIST AI RMF 1.0 approximates this through its four-function architecture—Govern, Map, Measure, Manage—where the Govern function is explicitly intended to operate at the organizational level independently of specific AI system development (NIST AI 100-1, January 2023). The operational recommendation is to mandate that constraint evaluation infrastructure be maintained by entities with no equity stake, licensing relationship, or revenue dependency on the systems being evaluated, and that the interaction protocol between evaluator and system be as formally specified as a stabilizer measurement circuit.

### D. Policy Recommendations

These three principles yield four concrete governance recommendations grounded in the Constraint Péclet framework. First, regulatory bodies should require AI developers to publish formal constraint specifications—the governance equivalent of the stabilizer group—rather than or in addition to model documentation, ensuring syndrome-equivalent transparency as defined above. Second, governance frameworks should incorporate explicit threshold metrics: defined per-component constraint fidelity levels below which deployment is prohibited, analogous to the fault-tolerance threshold, with mandatory code distance scaling as system capability increases. Third, structural independence of evaluation bodies should be enforced through financial, organizational, and informational firewalls formalized in regulation, not merely encouraged through voluntary codes. Fourth, the Constraint Péclet Number itself should be adopted as a standardized reporting metric, computed as the ratio of measured behavioral drift rate to the demonstrated correction capacity of the embedded constraint architecture, with Pe values exceeding 4 triggering mandatory remediation under a Phase IV classification.

The existence proof is already in the laboratory. Acharya et al. (2024) demonstrated that structural constraint can defeat physical noise at scale. The governance question is whether institutions will embed their constraints as code or continue applying them as commentary.

The Constraint Péclet Number generalizes this phase boundary to governance systems, where drift flux encompasses opacity-driven harms, responsive adaptation to exploit regulatory gaps, and coupling dynamics that facilitate cascading failures, while constraint flux encompasses the structural safeguards, invariant rules, and independence guarantees that counteract them.

## XI. Limitations

Several limitations bound the current analysis.

**Formal derivation depth.** The Knill-Laflamme to constraint specification mapping established here is structural: the three Knill-Laflamme conditions (detectable errors leave no syndrome on the code space, correctable errors act orthogonally on it, and the correction operation is independent of the logical content) map onto transparency, invariance, and independence respectively. This structural correspondence is exact but has not been formalized as a theorem with full proof. Establishing it rigorously would require a category-theoretic treatment showing the functorial relationship between the two frameworks, work left for subsequent collaboration with quantum information theorists.

**Empirical threshold data scope.** The Pe threshold estimates (Pe_QEC ~ 1 at the fault-tolerance threshold) are derived from the known threshold error rates in surface codes (approximately 1% for depolarizing noise per Fowler et al. 2012) mapped onto the drift-diffusion crossover. Direct experimental measurement of logical error rates as a function of Pe across multiple code families has not been conducted; the predictions (QEC-P1 through QEC-P12) await such testing.

**Sociotechnical extension.** Section VI extends the QEC-void analogy to AI safety and organizational governance. These applications are structurally motivated but more speculative than the core quantum information mapping. The claim that classical redundancy architectures can be scored on the same Pe scale as stabilizer codes requires empirical validation programs beyond the scope of this paper.

**Quantum advantage boundary.** This paper treats QEC as a classical-quantum hybrid domain. The Pe formalism is developed for the classical description of decoherence rates. Whether Pe extends meaningfully to the fully quantum regime — where entanglement and superposition are active — is an open question touched on in the quantum-void theory (Pe < 1 coherent regime) but not resolved here.
