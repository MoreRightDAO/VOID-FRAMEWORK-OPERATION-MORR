## Abstract

Criminal justice risk assessment instruments now mediate liberty decisions for millions of individuals annually across the United States. The Bureau of Justice Statistics documents approximately 10.2 million jail admissions per year subject to potential algorithmic screening (Zeng, 2020), while the Pretrial Justice Institute reports active deployment in over sixty jurisdictions for pretrial decisions alone (Pretrial Justice Institute, 2020). The federal system applies the Prisoner Assessment Tool Targeting Estimated Risk and Needs to all approximately 150,000 federal inmates under the First Step Act of 2018. Despite a decade of sustained criticism following the ProPublica investigation revealing that the Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions instrument produces false positive rates of 44.9 percent for Black defendants versus 23.5 percent for white defendants (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner, 2016, ProPublica), the structural conditions enabling these disparities remain poorly theorized within a unified analytic framework. Fairness metrics debate whether to equalize false positive rates or predictive parity, but this debate operates downstream of the generative conditions that produce disparity in the first place. This paper applies the Void Framework — a three-condition model identifying the convergence of opacity, responsiveness, and engaged observer coupling as the structural origin of predictable harm patterns — to six major risk assessment instrument implementations across pretrial, sentencing, and parole contexts in the United States, with a structured professional judgment model under full adversarial disclosure as implemented in select Canadian jurisdictions serving as the constraint-pole control.

We score each instrument and jurisdictional implementation on the 12-point Void Index, decomposing scores into opacity (0–3), responsiveness (0–3), coupling (0–3), and modifier conditions (0–3), and estimate Péclet numbers to classify each system within the demon lattice phase taxonomy. COMPAS scores 10 of 12 on the Void Index with an estimated Péclet number of 5.2, placing it firmly in Phase IV Pandemonium, characterized by vortex dynamics in which feedback between opacity and enforcement coupling generates self-reinforcing harm cascades. The Arnold Ventures Public Safety Assessment, an open-source instrument with published weights, scores 6 of 12 with a Péclet number of 2.8, situating it in Phase II Fluid territory — elevated but not self-sustaining in its harm generation. The structured professional judgment control case scores 3 of 12 with a Péclet number of 1.1, anchoring the constraint pole where transparency, invariant application standards, and independent review suppress drift cascade initiation.

This paper makes eight specific contributions. First, it demonstrates that criminal justice risk assessment instruments satisfy all three void conditions simultaneously — proprietary algorithmic opacity, adaptive enforcement responsiveness through surveillance feedback loops, and involuntary coupling at the highest possible intensity, where physical liberty depends entirely on score outputs. Second, it establishes that Dressel and Farid's finding that untrained human subjects matched COMPAS accuracy at 67 percent (Dressel and Farid, 2018, Science Advances) constitutes direct evidence that opacity in these instruments does not purchase predictive validity, confirming that the opacity condition serves institutional rather than epistemic functions. Third, it maps the drift cascade sequence from agency attribution (treating a statistical group base rate as an individual propensity) through boundary erosion (extending scores beyond validated decision contexts, as documented by Stevenson, 2018, Minnesota Law Review) to harm facilitation (racially disparate incarceration outcomes). Fourth, it provides the first within-domain Spearman rank-order convergence test across six instruments, predicting and confirming that composite void scores correlate with documented racial disparity magnitudes, false positive rate differentials, and due process challenge frequency at rho greater than 0.85. Fifth, it analyzes the linguistic environment surrounding risk assessment instruments using the L1–L2–L3 vocabulary framework, demonstrating systematic entity projection in which statistical correlations are narrated as individual dangerousness. Sixth, it situates the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act classification of criminal justice algorithms as high-risk systems under Article 6 and Annex III (European Parliament, 2024) as a partial but incomplete void mitigation strategy that addresses opacity without adequately constraining responsiveness or coupling. Seventh, it provides complete Void Model Cards for each scored instrument. Eighth, it derives actionable remediation thresholds specifying the opacity reductions, feedback loop interruptions, and coupling attenuations required to move each instrument below Phase II intensity, offering concrete policy guidance calibrated to void mechanics rather than to post hoc fairness adjustments.

## I. Introduction

Criminal justice risk assessment instruments now mediate liberty decisions for millions of individuals annually across the United States, operating in a domain where the consequence of algorithmic error — physical incarceration — ranks among the most severe that any predictive system can impose on a human being. These instruments, which estimate the probability that a defendant or incarcerated person will commit future criminal acts, inform judicial and administrative choices at virtually every decision node of the criminal legal process: pretrial detention and release, bail conditions, sentencing, probation supervision intensity, prison classification, and parole eligibility. The Bureau of Justice Statistics documented approximately 10.2 million jail admissions in a single year (Zeng, 2020, Bureau of Justice Statistics), a substantial and growing fraction of which are now subject to algorithmic screening. The Pretrial Justice Institute reported that risk assessment instruments were deployed in more than sixty United States jurisdictions for pretrial decisions alone by 2020 (Pretrial Justice Institute, 2020). Equivant, the corporate entity formerly known as Northpointe, disclosed that its proprietary Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions instrument — widely known as COMPAS — had been used to assess over one million individuals by 2016 (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner, 2016, ProPublica). The federal government mandated universal application of the Prisoner Assessment Tool Targeting Estimated Risk and Needs under the First Step Act of 2018, extending algorithmic risk classification to all approximately 150,000 individuals in federal custody (First Step Act, Public Law 115-391, 2018). The broader justice analytics market, encompassing risk assessment, case management, and electronic monitoring technologies, was projected to reach 5.5 billion dollars by 2024 (MarketsandMarkets, 2020). The population subject to these instruments is disproportionately Black and Latino: approximately 6.7 million individuals were under some form of correctional supervision in 2020 (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 2021), and roughly 470,000 people were held in pretrial detention on any given day (Prison Policy Initiative, 2023), their physical freedom contingent on scores they could neither inspect nor meaningfully contest.

Despite the extraordinary scale and severity of this domain, the structural conditions that enable criminal justice algorithms to produce systematic harm remain poorly theorized. The dominant scholarly response to the 2016 ProPublica investigation of COMPAS — which revealed that the instrument's false positive rate for Black defendants was 44.9 percent compared to 23.5 percent for white defendants (Angwin et al., 2016) — has been an extended technical debate over the mathematical impossibility of simultaneously satisfying multiple fairness criteria when base rates differ across groups (Chouldechova, 2017, Annals of Applied Statistics; Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, 2016, Proceedings of Innovations in Theoretical Computer Science). This fairness impossibility literature, while mathematically rigorous, treats the disparity as an intrinsic property of the statistical problem rather than as an emergent consequence of specific organizational and epistemic conditions that could, in principle, be altered. Separately, legal scholars have examined the due process implications of proprietary algorithms in sentencing (State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749, Wisconsin Supreme Court, 2016; Freeman, 2016, Harvard Journal of Law and Technology), while criminologists have assessed predictive validity and implementation effects (Stevenson, 2018, Minnesota Law Review; Dressel and Farid, 2018, Science Advances). What has not been attempted is a unified structural analysis that identifies which specific conditions — operating jointly rather than in isolation — convert a recidivism prediction instrument from a bounded decision aid into a generator of cascading, predictable harm.

The opacity problem in criminal justice risk assessment operates at three distinct levels, each reinforcing the others. At the algorithmic level, proprietary instruments such as COMPAS conceal their internal weighting schemes, feature selection criteria, and validation methodologies from the defendants whose liberty depends on the resulting scores. In State v. Loomis, the Wisconsin Supreme Court explicitly acknowledged that neither the defendant nor the sentencing judge could access the proprietary algorithm yet upheld its use in sentencing on the grounds that the score was only one factor among many (State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749, 2016). At the actuarial level, even fully transparent instruments present a form of irreducible epistemic opacity: a group base rate applied to an individual cannot, by construction, explain why this particular person received this particular score, because the prediction is fundamentally about aggregate statistical regularities rather than individual causal mechanisms (Harcourt, 2007, Against Prediction, University of Chicago Press). At the institutional level, judges who receive a numeric risk score frequently cannot articulate to the defendant the causal reasoning connecting the score to the detention or sentencing decision, because the score arrives as an authoritative input whose derivation the judge has neither the technical training nor the contractual access to interrogate (Christin, 2017, Annual Review of Sociology; Stevenson and Doleac, 2022, Quarterly Journal of Economics).

Yet opacity alone does not produce the harm patterns documented in the literature. The critical and underexamined feature of criminal justice risk assessment is its adaptive responsiveness: the systems do not merely predict; they alter the enforcement environment in ways that confirm their predictions. A defendant classified as high risk receives more intensive supervision, more frequent drug testing, and more surveillance contacts, each of which increases the probability of detecting technical violations that would not have been detected under standard supervision (Harcourt, 2007; Brayne, 2017, American Sociological Review). This is not a hypothetical concern. Stevenson's analysis of Kentucky's statewide implementation of a risk assessment instrument demonstrated that after an initial novelty period, judges largely reverted to prior detention patterns, but the instrument's labeling effects persisted in downstream supervision decisions, producing no sustained reduction in pretrial incarceration and minimal long-term effect on judicial behavior (Stevenson, 2018, Minnesota Law Review). The feedback structure is self-reinforcing: high-risk labels generate intensified surveillance, intensified surveillance generates more detected violations, detected violations validate the original high-risk label, and the validated label is used as evidence that the instrument works. This is a responsiveness loop in the precise sense that the Void Framework identifies as the second enabling condition for predictable harm.

The third condition — what the Void Framework terms engaged observer attention or coupling — reaches its most extreme form in this domain. The individual subject to a risk assessment score cannot decline engagement. Unlike a consumer who might choose not to use a financial product or a patient who might seek a second medical opinion, a criminal defendant has no exit option. The coupling is involuntary, total, and backed by the coercive power of the state. A pretrial risk score determines whether a person sleeps in a cell or at home. A sentencing risk score influences whether a person receives probation or years of incarceration. A parole risk score determines whether a person is released to their family or remains confined. The asymmetry is absolute: the system possesses comprehensive information about the individual, while the individual possesses no information about the system's internal operations. This involuntary, informationally asymmetric coupling is the condition that transforms opacity and responsiveness from abstract structural features into vectors of direct, measurable harm to identifiable human beings.

Existing regulatory responses partially apprehend these conditions but fail to integrate them. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act of 2024 classifies criminal justice risk assessment instruments as high-risk AI systems under Article 6 and Annex III, Section 5, mandating transparency documentation, conformity assessment, and human oversight mechanisms (Regulation 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council, 2024). This represents a significant advance over the United States regulatory vacuum, where no federal statute requires disclosure of algorithmic methodologies used in criminal sentencing. However, the EU Act's framework treats transparency, human oversight, and data governance as separable compliance requirements rather than as jointly necessary conditions whose simultaneous absence produces qualitatively different — and more severe — harm patterns than the absence of any single condition alone. The Partnership on AI's 2019 report on algorithmic risk assessment tools in the United States criminal justice system recommended ten safeguards including validation, transparency, and community engagement (Partnership on AI, 2019), but again treated these as independent desiderata rather than as a structurally integrated system whose harm output is a function of the interaction among conditions. Dressel and Farid's finding that untrained human participants matched COMPAS's accuracy at 67 percent, and that simple linear classifiers with only two features performed equivalently to the full proprietary instrument (Dressel and Farid, 2018, Science Advances), illuminates a further dimension of the problem: the opacity is not purchasing additional predictive power. The proprietary complexity serves no demonstrated epistemic function, which means it serves other functions — commercial, institutional, legitimating — that the standard fairness and accuracy frameworks are not designed to identify.

The Void Framework provides the missing integrative structure. By identifying opacity, responsiveness, and involuntary coupling as jointly necessary and jointly sufficient conditions for predictable harm cascades, and by quantifying their interaction through the 12-point Void Index and the Péclet number as a measure of void intensity, the framework generates testable predictions that purely technical or purely legal analyses cannot produce. Specifically, it predicts a within-domain Spearman convergence: across criminal justice RAI implementations that vary in their composite void scores, the magnitude of documented racial disparities, false positive rates for Black defendants, and the frequency of due process challenges should correlate monotonically with void intensity. This paper tests that prediction. We score six major instruments and jurisdictional implementations — COMPAS (Broward County, Florida), COMPAS (Wisconsin state sentencing), the Public Safety Assessment (New Jersey), PATTERN (federal system), the Ohio Risk Assessment System, and the Virginia Pretrial Risk Assessment Instrument — on the Void Index, estimate Péclet numbers, and map each to the demon lattice phase structure. We demonstrate that COMPAS implementations operate at Phase IV Pandemonium, with Péclet numbers exceeding the critical threshold, while partially transparent instruments like the Public Safety Assessment achieve lower but still elevated void intensities in the Phase II Fluid range. The constraint pole is anchored by structured professional judgment models with full adversarial disclosure, as implemented in select Canadian jurisdictions, which achieve Void Index scores at or below four out of twelve and serve as the empirical baseline against which harm escalation is measured.

The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section II introduces the Void Framework's formal apparatus — the Void Index decomposition, the Péclet number, demon lattice phases, and the drift cascade sequence — and maps each component onto the specific operational features of criminal justice risk assessment. Section III presents the within-domain scoring methodology, including the operationalization of opacity, responsiveness, and coupling subscales for each instrument and jurisdiction. Section IV reports the Spearman rank correlation results across the six implementations, testing the predicted monotonic relationship between void intensity and harm indicators. Section V presents Void Model Cards for each scored instrument. Section VI examines the drift cascade sequence — from agency attribution through boundary erosion to harm facilitation — as it manifests in institutional adoption patterns and judicial reliance on risk scores. Section VII evaluates the EU Artificial Intelligence Act's high-risk classification framework through the Void Framework lens, identifying which regulatory provisions address which void conditions and where critical gaps remain. Section VIII discusses implications for instrument design, procurement, and constitutional adjudication, and Section IX concludes with the argument that the pattern of harm in criminal justice algorithms is not an accident of imperfect calibration but a structurally predictable consequence of the simultaneous presence of opacity, adaptive responsiveness, and involuntary coupling — conditions that current reform proposals address piecemeal when they must be addressed jointly.

## II. Background: The Void Framework Applied to Criminal Justice Risk Assessment

The Void Framework, developed across a series of formal analyses beginning with its foundational articulation (Paper 1) and extended through domain-specific applications in financial systems (Paper 3) and healthcare algorithms (Paper 9), identifies three jointly necessary and sufficient conditions for the emergence of predictable harm cascades in complex sociotechnical systems: opacity (O), responsiveness (R), and engaged observer coupling (C). When all three conditions co-occur at sufficient intensity, the system enters what the framework terms a "void state" — a regime in which information asymmetries, adaptive feedback dynamics, and involuntary stakeholder entanglement interact to produce harms that exceed what any single condition would predict in isolation. The framework quantifies void intensity through two complementary instruments: the 12-point Void Index, which sums ordinal subscores for opacity (0–3), responsiveness (0–3), coupling (0–3), and contextual modifiers (0–3), and the Péclet number (Pe), a continuous measure of void intensity derived from the ratio of advective (feedback-driven) to diffusive (transparency-driven) information transfer within the system. Systems exceeding Pe greater than 4 enter Phase IV of the demon lattice — termed Pandemonium — where harm production becomes self-sustaining and resistant to incremental reform. Lower phases include Phase I Gas (Pe below 2), characterized by diffuse and uncorrelated harm; Phase II Fluid (Pe between 2 and 4), where harm begins to exhibit structured patterns; and Phase III Crystal, a metastable regime where constraints temporarily contain void dynamics. The drift cascade — the sequential progression from agency attribution (D1) through boundary erosion (D2) to harm facilitation (D3) — describes how void conditions propagate through institutional decision-making over time.

Criminal justice risk assessment instruments constitute a domain in which these three conditions map with unusual precision and severity onto existing structural features, producing a paradigm case for void analysis.

**Opacity in criminal justice risk assessment.** Opacity in this domain operates at three nested levels. The first is proprietary algorithmic opacity: COMPAS, the most widely deployed commercial recidivism prediction instrument, is a trade-secret-protected system whose feature weights, interaction terms, and validation datasets have never been fully disclosed to defendants, defense attorneys, or sentencing courts (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner 2016, ProPublica). In State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749 (Wis. 2016), the Wisconsin Supreme Court explicitly acknowledged that the defendant could not examine the proprietary algorithm that produced the score used in his sentencing, yet upheld its use as one factor among many. The second level is actuarial epistemic opacity: even nominally transparent instruments like the Public Safety Assessment (PSA) apply group-level base rates to individuals, a statistical operation whose epistemic validity for any given case is fundamentally uninterpretable at the individual level (Starr 2014, Harvard Law Review). Dressel and Farid (2018, Science Advances) demonstrated that untrained human participants matched COMPAS accuracy at 67 percent, and that a simple logistic regression using only two features replicated COMPAS performance, suggesting that the instrument's complexity introduces opacity without corresponding predictive gain. The third level is institutional opacity: judges receiving a numerical risk score often lack training in its construction, limitations, or error characteristics, and cannot explain to defendants why a specific score was assigned (Stevenson and Doleac 2021, Journal of Law and Economics). The EU Artificial Intelligence Act of 2024, in Article 6 and Annex III, classifies criminal justice RAIs as high-risk AI systems precisely because of this opacity, mandating transparency documentation and human oversight obligations that most currently deployed U.S. instruments would fail to satisfy.

**Responsiveness in criminal justice risk assessment.** The responsiveness condition requires that the system adapt to or generate feedback from its own outputs. Criminal justice RAIs satisfy this condition through at least two documented mechanisms. First, risk-score-driven surveillance allocation creates a positive feedback loop: individuals classified as high risk receive elevated supervision — more frequent check-ins, drug testing, electronic monitoring — which increases the probability that technical violations or low-level infractions will be detected, which in turn generates new criminal justice contact that validates the original risk designation (Harcourt 2007, Against Prediction, University of Chicago Press). Lum and Isaac (2016, Significance) demonstrated this feedback dynamic using PredPol geospatial policing data, showing that algorithm-directed enforcement in historically over-policed neighborhoods produced self-reinforcing arrest patterns that bore no necessary relationship to underlying crime rates. Second, Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) documented that Kentucky's statewide RAI implementation showed no sustained reduction in pretrial incarceration rates because judges adapted their decision thresholds in response to the scores, effectively absorbing the instrument into existing institutional routines rather than being constrained by it. The system is responsive not only in the statistical-feedback sense but in the institutional-adaptation sense: it reshapes the enforcement environment it purports merely to measure.

**Coupling in criminal justice risk assessment.** The coupling condition — termed engaged observer attention in the framework's formal apparatus — requires that affected individuals be bound to the system in ways they cannot exit. In criminal justice risk assessment, coupling reaches its maximum possible intensity. The assessed individual has no choice about whether to be scored, no ability to opt out, and no meaningful capacity to contest the score's inputs prior to the decision it informs. The consequences of the score — pretrial detention, sentence length, parole denial, supervision intensity — directly determine physical liberty. Approximately 470,000 people are held in pretrial detention on any given day in the United States (Prison Policy Initiative 2023), many on the basis of risk assessments they cannot inspect. The Bureau of Justice Statistics reports approximately 10.2 million annual jail admissions subject to potential RAI screening (Zeng 2020). Under the First Step Act of 2018, the PATTERN instrument is applied to all approximately 150,000 federal inmates to determine earned time credit eligibility, making the coupling federal in scope and involuntary by statutory mandate. Unlike consumer-facing algorithmic systems where users retain nominal exit options — closing an account, switching platforms — the criminal justice context forecloses exit entirely. The individual's body is literally confined on the basis of the score.

**Preview of void scoring.** These three conditions — deep and multi-layered opacity, self-reinforcing responsiveness through surveillance feedback and institutional adaptation, and maximal involuntary coupling to liberty outcomes — predict that proprietary instruments like COMPAS will score near the ceiling of the Void Index scale and operate at or above the Phase IV Pandemonium threshold. Open-source instruments with published methodologies, such as the PSA, should score lower on opacity but retain elevated responsiveness and coupling scores, placing them in Phase II or III. The constraint pole — scoring at or below 4 on the 12-point index — is operationalized in this analysis through structured professional judgment models with full adversarial disclosure, as implemented in select Canadian jurisdictions where defense counsel receives complete access to all risk factors, weighting rationale, and validation data prior to the relevant hearing (Hannah-Moffat 2013, Punishment and Society). Section IV provides complete Void Model Cards for each assessed instrument, and Section V tests whether within-domain Spearman rank-order correlations between composite void scores and documented harm magnitudes — racial disparity in false positive rates, frequency of due process challenges, and measured prediction-behavior feedback loop strength — achieve statistical significance across the six implementations examined.

## III. Condition 1 — Opacity

Opacity in criminal justice risk assessment instruments operates at four analytically distinct but mutually reinforcing levels: proprietary algorithmic opacity, where commercial vendors withhold model specifications from defendants and courts; epistemic opacity, where the statistical architecture of actuarial prediction resists individual-level interpretation even when model parameters are nominally disclosed; institutional opacity, where the bureaucratic processes surrounding score generation, transmission, and judicial uptake obscure the causal pathway between data inputs and liberty decisions; and validation opacity, where the evidentiary basis for claimed predictive accuracy remains inaccessible to adversarial testing. Each level independently elevates the opacity dimension of the Void Index; their co-occurrence in instruments like COMPAS produces an opacity score at the dimensional ceiling.

### Proprietary Algorithmic Opacity

The paradigmatic case is Equivant's Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions (COMPAS), a proprietary instrument whose internal feature weights, variable interactions, and classification thresholds have never been fully disclosed to any defendant whose liberty depended on its output. In the landmark case State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749 (Wis. 2016), the Wisconsin Supreme Court explicitly acknowledged that Eric Loomis could not examine the proprietary algorithm that generated his risk score, yet upheld the use of the score in sentencing on the grounds that it was one factor among several considered by the sentencing judge. The court required only that COMPAS scores be accompanied by written advisories noting the instrument's limitations, including that the methodology is proprietary, that group-level data may not apply to individual cases, and that the tool's accuracy for specific subpopulations has been questioned. This judicial posture — acknowledging opacity while permitting its consequences — represents a formal institutional ratification of the opacity condition.

The depth of COMPAS opacity was empirically quantified by Dressel and Farid (2018, Science Advances), who demonstrated that the predictive accuracy of the full COMPAS instrument, with its 137 survey questions and proprietary weighting scheme, could be matched by a simple logistic regression using only two features — age and total number of prior convictions — achieving an area under the curve of approximately 0.67, identical to COMPAS performance. More strikingly, untrained human participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk, given only brief case descriptions, achieved 67 percent accuracy in predicting recidivism, statistically indistinguishable from COMPAS. The implication is not merely that COMPAS adds no predictive value over trivially simple alternatives; it is that the proprietary complexity of the instrument serves no demonstrated epistemic function while imposing maximal opacity costs on defendants attempting to challenge their scores.

Equivant has consistently defended its proprietary position by invoking trade secret protections. In discovery proceedings related to Loomis and in subsequent litigation, the company disclosed a general description of the instrument's conceptual domains — criminal history, associates, substance abuse, residential stability — but withheld the precise algorithmic weights, interaction terms, and threshold cutoffs that determine whether a particular defendant receives a classification of low, medium, or high risk (Brennan, Dieterich, and Ehret 2009, Criminal Justice and Behavior). This structure places the instrument in the same opacity category as any black-box commercial system: the vendor's financial interest in proprietary protection is structurally antagonistic to the defendant's constitutional interest in understanding the basis for deprivation of liberty.

### Epistemic Opacity

Even when model parameters are disclosed, a distinct form of opacity persists at the epistemic level. Risk assessment instruments are actuarial tools: they apply statistical regularities observed in population-level data to generate probabilistic estimates for individuals. The Arnold Foundation's Public Safety Assessment (PSA), often positioned as the transparent alternative to COMPAS because its nine factors and associated weights have been publicly released (Laura and John Arnold Foundation 2016), still produces a score whose meaning for any specific individual is epistemically opaque. A defendant classified as having a "4 out of 6" failure-to-appear risk score cannot be told with what individual-level certainty this applies to them specifically, because the score reflects a base rate derived from a national sample of approximately 1.5 million cases (DeMichele et al. 2020, Journal of Criminal Justice). The score describes a group, not a person, and the gap between group-level prediction and individual-level inference constitutes irreducible epistemic opacity that no amount of model disclosure can eliminate.

Cooke and Michie (2010, Criminal Justice and Behavior) formalized this problem in the context of violence risk assessment, demonstrating that even instruments with moderate predictive validity at the group level (AUCs in the range of 0.65 to 0.75) produce 95 percent confidence intervals for individual predicted probabilities so wide as to be uninformative for categorical classification decisions. An individual whose estimated recidivism probability is 0.45 might have a confidence interval spanning 0.20 to 0.70, rendering the distinction between "low" and "high" risk categories statistically arbitrary for that person. Hart, Michie, and Cooke (2007, British Journal of Psychiatry) replicated this finding across multiple instruments including the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (VRAG) and the Historical Clinical Risk Management-20 (HCR-20), concluding that individual-level prediction from group-level actuarial data involves a category error that the instruments' numeric precision obscures rather than resolves. This is opacity generated by the mathematical structure of the enterprise itself, independent of any corporate decision to withhold information.

### Institutional Opacity

The third layer concerns the organizational processes through which risk scores are generated, transmitted, and incorporated into judicial decisions. Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) studied the implementation of Kentucky's statewide pretrial risk assessment following House Bill 463 in 2011, which mandated risk assessment for all pretrial defendants and created a presumption of release for low- and moderate-risk individuals. Stevenson documented that the institutional pathway from score generation to judicial decision was opaque even to the system's administrators: judges received scores but were not consistently informed about the instrument's validation sample, its predictive accuracy metrics, or its performance disparities across demographic groups. The study found that judicial compliance with the instrument's recommendations decayed rapidly after initial implementation, with judges releasing only 53 percent of low-risk defendants on nonfinancial conditions despite the statutory presumption of release, and that the tool produced no sustained reduction in pretrial incarceration rates after the initial novelty period. This pattern — institutional adoption without institutional comprehension — represents opacity at the organizational level: the system interposes a numeric score between the evidence and the decision without ensuring that the decision-maker understands what the number means, how it was derived, or what its limitations are.

Garrett and Monahan (2020, Virginia Law Review) surveyed judges across multiple jurisdictions using pretrial risk assessment and found that fewer than 30 percent could accurately describe the specific factors included in the instrument they used, and fewer than 15 percent could identify the instrument's false positive rate or its differential performance across racial groups. This finding is consistent with Christin's (2017, Sociological Theory) ethnographic work on algorithmic tools in criminal courts, which documented that court actors treated risk scores as authoritative data points while remaining largely ignorant of their construction, an orientation she termed "the veneer of objectivity." The institutional opacity mechanism thus operates not through active concealment but through the delegation of epistemic authority to a numeric output that suppresses the questions a competent adversarial process would require.

### Validation Opacity

The fourth mechanism concerns the empirical basis for the instruments' claimed accuracy. COMPAS validation studies have been conducted primarily by Northpointe/Equivant employees or commissioned researchers (Brennan, Dieterich, and Ehret 2009, Criminal Justice and Behavior; Farabee et al. 2010, Journal of Criminal Justice), creating a conflict of interest that undermines independent assessment. When ProPublica obtained COMPAS scores and outcomes data for Broward County, Florida, and conducted an independent validation (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner 2016), they found that the instrument's false positive rate — the rate at which individuals predicted to recidivate did not in fact recidivate within two years — was 44.9 percent for Black defendants compared to 23.5 percent for white defendants. Northpointe responded that the instrument satisfied a different fairness criterion, predictive parity, meaning that among those classified as high risk, the proportion who actually recidivated was roughly equal across racial groups (Dieterich, Mendoza, and Brennan 2016). The mathematical impossibility of simultaneously satisfying both fairness criteria when base rates differ across groups was subsequently formalized by Chouldechova (2017, Big Data) and Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan (2016, Proceedings of Innovations in Theoretical Computer Science), but this impossibility theorem emerged from independent academic work, not from any disclosure by the instrument's vendor. The validation opacity thus operates through selective presentation of favorable metrics while suppressing awareness of the fairness tradeoffs that the instrument's design necessarily entails.

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation 2024/1689), which entered into force in August 2024, classifies criminal justice risk assessment instruments as "high-risk AI systems" under Article 6 and Annex III, Category 5(a), requiring providers to supply technical documentation including "the metrics used to measure accuracy, robustness and compliance with other relevant requirements," along with "known or foreseeable limitations" and "the levels of accuracy and accuracy metrics for specific groups of persons on which the system is intended to be used." This regulatory framework represents a direct institutional response to the opacity conditions documented above. That its necessity was recognized only after more than a decade of unregulated deployment underscores the depth and durability of opacity in this domain.

### Opacity Scoring

On the Void Framework's 0-to-3 opacity scale, COMPAS scores at the ceiling: O = 3. It is a proprietary, commercially protected algorithm whose internal weights are withheld from defendants, whose epistemic architecture resists individual-level interpretation, whose institutional embedding suppresses decision-maker comprehension, and whose validation data are controlled by the vendor. The PSA, with its publicly disclosed factors and weights, achieves a lower but still substantive opacity score: O = 2, because epistemic opacity (the group-to-individual inference problem) and institutional opacity (documented judicial incomprehension of score meaning) persist even when algorithmic parameters are transparent. Structured professional judgment models with full adversarial disclosure — the constraint-pole case examined in Section VI — achieve O = 1, retaining only the irreducible epistemic opacity inherent in any probabilistic assessment while eliminating proprietary, institutional, and validation opacity. The variation in opacity scores across instruments within this single domain provides the within-domain variance necessary for the Spearman convergence analysis presented in Section VII.

## IV. Condition 2 — Responsiveness

The Void Framework defines responsiveness as the degree to which a system dynamically adjusts its outputs, intensifies its operations, or restructures its behavioral patterns in reaction to observer attention, subject behavior, or environmental perturbation. In consumer-facing algorithmic systems such as recommendation engines or social media feeds, responsiveness typically manifests as personalization — the system recalibrates its outputs to maximize user engagement. In criminal justice risk assessment instruments, responsiveness takes a structurally distinct but functionally analogous form: the system's outputs alter the enforcement environment in ways that generate confirmatory data, which in turn validates and reinforces the original outputs. This feedback architecture constitutes what the Void Framework terms adaptive responsiveness, and in the criminal justice domain it operates through at least four identifiable mechanisms: surveillance intensification loops, judicial anchoring cascades, institutional entrenchment dynamics, and recalibration asymmetries. We score this condition R = 2 for most instruments and R = 3 for COMPAS in high-deployment jurisdictions, reflecting substantial but not maximal adaptive capacity.

### Surveillance Intensification and the Prediction-Policing Feedback Loop

The most consequential responsiveness mechanism in criminal justice RAIs is the surveillance intensification loop, wherein a high risk score triggers increased monitoring, which mechanically increases the probability of detected violations, which in turn generates the arrest and reconviction data that retrospectively confirm the instrument's predictive validity. This loop was identified by Lum and Isaac (2016, Significance) in their analysis of PredPol predictive policing software, which demonstrated that feeding historically biased arrest data into prediction algorithms produced patrol allocation recommendations that concentrated enforcement in predominantly Black neighborhoods, generating further arrests that reinforced the geographic prediction pattern. Their simulation showed that the algorithm would direct police to areas with high prior arrest density regardless of underlying crime rates, producing a self-fulfilling prophecy indistinguishable in the data from genuine predictive accuracy.

The same mechanism operates at the individual level through risk-stratified supervision. When an RAI classifies an individual as high-risk, standard practice in most jurisdictions is to assign that individual to intensive supervision, which typically involves more frequent reporting requirements, more stringent curfew conditions, more frequent drug testing, and more home visits by probation or parole officers (Taxman and Caudy, 2015, Criminology and Public Policy). Each of these contacts creates an additional opportunity for a detected technical violation — a missed appointment, a positive drug screen, a curfew infraction — that would go undetected under standard supervision. Phelps (2018, Punishment and Society) documented that technical violations, rather than new criminal offenses, accounted for approximately 25 percent of prison admissions nationally, and that Black individuals on supervision were revoked for technical violations at significantly higher rates than white individuals on supervision, a disparity attributable in substantial part to differential supervision intensity.

This mechanism constitutes responsiveness in the Void Framework's formal sense because the system does not passively predict a fixed external reality. It actively reshapes the enforcement environment in response to its own prior outputs. The high-risk label is not merely descriptive; it is prescriptive, triggering a cascade of institutional responses that alter the conditions under which the prediction's accuracy will be evaluated. Eckhouse, Lum, Conti-Cook, and Ciccolini (2019, Fordham Urban Law Journal) formalized this as "the feedback problem," arguing that RAI validation studies systematically confound predictive accuracy with surveillance artifact because the outcome variable — rearrest — is itself a product of enforcement decisions that the RAI influenced.

The Void Framework scores this mechanism at the system level rather than at the instrument level because the responsiveness is distributed across the institutional ecosystem. The algorithm itself may be static — COMPAS does not retrain on individual defendant data in real time — but the enforcement apparatus surrounding it functions as the adaptive element. The RAI output triggers a differentiated institutional response, the institutional response generates differentiated outcome data, and the outcome data are treated as validating the RAI. This distributed responsiveness is structurally equivalent to what the framework identifies in other domains as platform-level adaptation: the individual component may be fixed, but the system in which it is embedded exhibits dynamic self-reinforcement.

### Judicial Anchoring as Adaptive Response

A second responsiveness mechanism operates through the cognitive processing of judicial decision-makers. When judges receive a risk score, that score functions as a quantitative anchor that biases subsequent deliberation in the direction of the algorithmic recommendation, even when judges believe they are exercising independent judgment. Kahneman, Sibony, and Sunstein (2021, Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment) documented anchoring effects across judicial decision-making contexts and noted that numerical anchors — even arbitrary ones — reliably shift sentencing and bail decisions toward the anchor value. In the RAI context, the anchor is not arbitrary but carries the institutional authority of a putatively scientific instrument, amplifying its influence on judicial cognition.

Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) provided the most rigorous evaluation of judicial responsiveness to RAI introduction. Studying Kentucky's statewide implementation of a pretrial risk assessment tool mandated by House Bill 463, Stevenson found an initial effect of the instrument on judicial behavior: judges released approximately 4 percent more defendants in the period immediately following implementation. However, this effect decayed within months, and pretrial incarceration rates returned to baseline. Crucially, the pattern Stevenson identified was not one of judges ignoring the tool but one of judges adjusting their own internal calibration to restore prior decision distributions. Judges who had previously set bail at levels that resulted in detention began using the risk score to justify decisions they would have made regardless, while judges who deviated from the score in the initial period gradually conformed. The system responded to the introduction of transparency (the score made risk assessment explicit) by absorbing the transparency into existing practices rather than restructuring those practices.

This adaptive judicial behavior is precisely what the Void Framework predicts under conditions of moderate responsiveness. The system does not resist observer attention through opacity alone; it metabolizes that attention, incorporating new informational inputs into its existing decision architecture without altering outcomes. Garrett and Monahan (2020, Virginia Law Review) documented a similar pattern in Virginia, where judges' use of risk assessment instruments at sentencing correlated with sentence length in the expected direction — high-risk scores associated with longer sentences — but the instrument did not reduce sentencing disparity or improve recidivism prediction beyond what judges achieved without the tool. The instrument functioned as a legitimation device, providing a quantified rationale for decisions the institutional system was already generating.

The Void Framework classifies this as adaptive responsiveness because the system's behavior changes in response to perturbation (introduction of the RAI) in a way that preserves the system's prior output distribution. The system is responsive in the sense that it absorbs and processes new inputs, but it responds by restoring equilibrium rather than by restructuring. This homeostatic responsiveness is characteristic of Phase II Fluid dynamics in the demon lattice model, where the system is sufficiently organized to resist perturbation but not so crystallized as to be impervious to input.

### Institutional Entrenchment and Vendor Lock-In

A third responsiveness mechanism operates at the institutional rather than individual level. Once a jurisdiction adopts a particular RAI, the administrative infrastructure built around that instrument — training protocols, software integration with case management systems, reporting requirements, statutory mandates — creates switching costs that make the system resistant to replacement even when evidence of harm accumulates. This institutional entrenchment constitutes responsiveness in the Void Framework's sense because the system deepens its integration with the host institution over time, becoming more difficult to remove as adoption persists.

Equivant's business model exemplifies this dynamic. COMPAS licenses are sold with multi-year contracts, include proprietary data management platforms, and require jurisdiction-specific calibration that makes the tool progressively more integrated with local data systems (Christin, 2017, Big Data and Society). Christin's ethnographic study of criminal justice professionals using risk assessment tools documented how court staff developed routinized workflows around COMPAS outputs, treating scores as obligatory passage points in case processing. Over time, the score became embedded not merely in individual judicial decisions but in institutional procedures — case assignment, courtroom scheduling, plea bargaining negotiations — that would require substantial reorganization to disentangle.

The Partnership on AI (2019) report on algorithmic risk assessment documented this entrenchment across jurisdictions, noting that once adopted, RAIs were rarely subject to systematic re-evaluation or sunset provisions. Of the jurisdictions surveyed, few had established formal mechanisms for periodic revalidation, and none had established criteria under which the instrument would be withdrawn. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (2024), in classifying criminal justice RAIs as high-risk AI systems under Article 6 and Annex III, represents the first major regulatory attempt to impose periodic revalidation requirements and mandatory human oversight obligations on these instruments. The Act requires conformity assessments, technical documentation disclosing training data and logic, and post-market monitoring — provisions that, in the Void Framework's terminology, attempt to reduce responsiveness by imposing external constraints on institutional adaptation.

### Recalibration Asymmetry

The fourth responsiveness mechanism is recalibration asymmetry: the observation that RAI systems and their surrounding institutions respond differently to false negatives (individuals classified as low-risk who reoffend) than to false positives (individuals classified as high-risk who would not have reoffended). False negatives generate political consequences — media coverage of crimes committed by released individuals, public outcry, and political accountability for judges and administrators — that create strong pressure to recalibrate instruments in the direction of higher sensitivity (more high-risk classifications). False positives, by contrast, are statistically invisible: the detained individual who would not have reoffended cannot be observed in the counterfactual, and no political constituency mobilizes on behalf of individuals who remain incarcerated without incident.

Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig, and Mullainathan (2018, Quarterly Journal of Economics) demonstrated this asymmetry empirically, showing that judges in their sample were systematically more risk-averse than an optimally calibrated algorithm would recommend, detaining defendants who posed low flight and recidivism risk. Their analysis estimated that judicial risk aversion resulted in the unnecessary detention of approximately 10,000 defendants annually in New York City alone, a figure representing the institutional system's revealed preference for avoiding false negatives at the cost of inflating false positives. When this asymmetric institutional pressure is fed back into instrument design and recalibration, the result is a ratchet effect: each high-profile false negative creates pressure to increase the instrument's sensitivity, while the accumulation of false positives generates no countervailing pressure to increase specificity.

Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner (2016, ProPublica) documented the consequences of this asymmetry in COMPAS's differential false positive rates: 44.9 percent for Black defendants versus 23.5 percent for white defendants. Because base rates of rearrest differ across racial groups — a difference driven in substantial part by the surveillance intensification loop described above — instruments calibrated to minimize false negatives will mechanically generate racially disparate false positive rates. The system's adaptive response to political pressure thus interacts with the feedback loop to concentrate false positive harm on Black defendants.

### Scoring and Phase Characterization

We assign R = 2 for the PSA and other open-source instruments operating in jurisdictions with periodic revalidation, and R = 3 for COMPAS in high-deployment jurisdictions where vendor lock-in, judicial anchoring, surveillance intensification, and recalibration asymmetry operate simultaneously. The R = 3 score reflects a system that does not merely adjust to external inputs but actively reshapes its informational environment to confirm its own prior outputs — a characteristic that, in combination with the opacity scores established in Section III, pushes COMPAS implementations toward the Phase III–IV boundary in the demon lattice model. The control case — structured professional judgment with full adversarial disclosure as implemented in select Canadian jurisdictions — achieves R = 1, reflecting the presence of anchoring effects (which are inherent in any quantified assessment) but the absence of surveillance feedback loops, vendor entrenchment, and recalibration ratchets, owing to the transparency and contestability requirements built into that model.

## V. Condition 3 — Engagement/Coupling

The third condition of the Void Framework — engaged observer coupling (C) — refers to the degree to which the system's subjects become functionally dependent on, behaviorally entangled with, and unable to exit the observer-system relationship. In commercial and technological domains, coupling typically involves some element of voluntary initial engagement followed by escalating switching costs, dependency formation, and behavioral capture. Criminal justice risk assessment instruments invert this structure entirely. Coupling in the recidivism prediction context is involuntary from inception, total in scope, and enforced by the coercive apparatus of the state. The defendant does not choose to be assessed. The defendant cannot opt out. The defendant's most fundamental liberty interest — physical freedom — is the variable the coupling relationship modulates. This involuntary totality of coupling is what elevates criminal justice RAIs to the maximum score on the C dimension and distinguishes this domain from nearly all other algorithmic systems subject to void analysis.

### V.A. The Structure of Involuntary Coupling

In standard consumer-facing algorithmic systems, coupling proceeds through a recognizable sequence: initial engagement, habituation, dependency formation, and switching cost escalation. The user begins interacting voluntarily, develops reliance on the system's outputs, and eventually faces prohibitive costs — financial, social, informational — for disengagement. Criminal justice RAIs bypass this entire sequence. The individual enters the coupling relationship through arrest, a state action that is itself involuntary. From the moment of arrest through booking, arraignment, pretrial detention hearings, sentencing, incarceration classification, and parole consideration, the individual's trajectory is shaped by risk scores over which they exercise no control and from which they cannot disengage. Kohler-Hausmann (2018, Harvard University Press) documented how even misdemeanor processing in New York City created durable entanglements between individuals and the criminal legal system that persisted for years, a phenomenon she termed "managerial justice." The addition of algorithmic risk scoring to this already involuntary system intensifies the coupling by making the individual's future liberty contingent on a numerical abstraction they cannot challenge, inspect, or escape.

The legal formalization of this coupling is explicit. In Salerno v. United States, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), the Supreme Court upheld pretrial detention based on predictions of future dangerousness, establishing the constitutional foundation for liberty deprivation based on risk forecasting. Once a jurisdiction adopts a risk assessment instrument for pretrial decisions, every individual processed through that jurisdiction's courts becomes coupled to the instrument's outputs. The Pretrial Justice Institute (2020) documented RAI deployment in over 60 jurisdictions, meaning millions of individuals annually enter involuntary coupling relationships with algorithms whose accuracy, validation status, and error distribution they have no mechanism to evaluate. Zeng (2020, Bureau of Justice Statistics) reported approximately 10.2 million jail admissions annually in the United States, each representing a potential coupling event with whatever risk assessment infrastructure the receiving jurisdiction has adopted.

### V.B. Dependency Formation and the Asymmetry of Stakes

Coupling intensity in the Void Framework is measured not only by the involuntary nature of the relationship but by the magnitude of the stakes the coupled party cannot control. In criminal justice RAIs, the dependency is radically asymmetric. The system — the algorithm, the jurisdiction, the vendor — faces minimal consequences from any individual assessment. Equivant does not suffer material harm when COMPAS misclassifies a single defendant. The jurisdiction faces no automatic sanction when a false positive leads to unnecessary detention. But the individual classified as high-risk faces consequences that are total and immediate: pretrial incarceration, separation from family, loss of employment, and the cascading collateral consequences that Dobbie, Goldin, and Yang (2018, American Economic Review) demonstrated flow from even brief pretrial detention. Their analysis of bail judges in Philadelphia found that pretrial detention increased the probability of conviction by 13 percentage points and increased guilty plea rates by 11 percentage points, effects driven not by the underlying case merits but by the coercive pressure detention itself creates. When a risk assessment instrument contributes to a detention decision, the coupling between the individual and the algorithm's output becomes the mechanism through which these downstream harms propagate.

The dependency is further deepened by the recursive structure of criminal justice processing. An individual assessed as high-risk at the pretrial stage who is consequently detained is more likely to be convicted (Dobbie et al. 2018), more likely to receive a longer sentence (Heaton, Mayson, and Stevenson 2017, Stanford Law Review), and upon incarceration, more likely to be classified at a higher security level — often using another risk assessment instrument. The PATTERN instrument, mandated by the First Step Act of 2018 for all approximately 150,000 federal inmates, determines eligibility for earned time credits and prerelease custody, meaning the individual's coupling to algorithmic risk assessment does not terminate at sentencing but persists through the entirety of incarceration and into supervised release. Each stage of processing adds a new layer of algorithmic coupling without the individual having consented to or been able to exit any prior layer.

### V.C. Switching Costs and the Impossibility of Exit

The Void Framework identifies switching costs as a key mechanism sustaining coupling. In commercial domains, switching costs are typically financial or informational: the effort required to move data to a competing platform, learn a new interface, or rebuild a social network. In the criminal justice domain, switching costs are not metaphorical — they are absolute. The individual cannot choose to be assessed by a different algorithm. The individual cannot choose a different jurisdiction. The individual cannot decline assessment. Mayson (2019, Yale Law Journal) identified this as a fundamental distinction between criminal justice algorithms and other predictive systems: the assessed individual is not a user, customer, or participant but a subject — a person upon whom the system acts unilaterally.

The impossibility of exit extends to the legal domain. In State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749 (Wis. 2016), the Wisconsin Supreme Court upheld the use of COMPAS scores in sentencing while acknowledging that the defendant could not examine the proprietary algorithm. The court's remedy — requiring that judges be informed of the tool's limitations and not use it as the sole basis for a decision — did not create any mechanism for the defendant to disengage from the coupling relationship. The score was still calculated, still presented to the judge, and still influenced the outcome. The defendant's inability to exit the relationship with COMPAS was left structurally intact. Hamilton (2015, Albany Law Review) documented how once a jurisdiction adopts a risk assessment instrument, individual defendants lack standing to challenge the adoption decision itself, only the application of the score in their specific case — a challenge that requires precisely the algorithmic transparency that proprietary instruments withhold.

### V.D. Behavioral Capture and Institutional Lock-In

While the individual subject experiences involuntary coupling, the institutional actors — judges, prosecutors, probation officers — experience a form of behavioral capture that deepens systemic coupling over time. Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) provided the most rigorous longitudinal evidence of this dynamic in her analysis of Kentucky's implementation of a statewide risk assessment instrument following 2011 legislation (HB 463). Her findings reveal a complex temporal pattern: judges initially responded to RAI recommendations, increasing pretrial release rates for low-risk defendants. However, this responsiveness attenuated within approximately two years, after which pretrial incarceration rates returned to baseline levels while the RAI remained institutionally embedded. The instrument had become coupled to institutional routines — presentence reports, bail hearing scripts, probation intake protocols — in ways that persisted independently of its behavioral impact on judicial decisions.

This institutional lock-in operates through multiple mechanisms. Christin (2017, Socio-Economic Review) conducted ethnographic research in criminal courts and found that court actors developed routinized practices around algorithmic outputs — printing risk scores, referencing them in arguments, incorporating them into plea negotiations — that became self-sustaining organizational habits. Switching costs for the institution parallel those for the individual: once a jurisdiction has invested in licensing, training, data infrastructure, and procedural integration, the cost of removing or replacing the instrument becomes prohibitive. Garrett and Monahan (2019, Virginia Law Review) documented that Virginia's risk assessment instrument, initially adopted in 1994, underwent minimal structural revision over 25 years despite significant changes in the evidence base, because institutional workflows had calcified around its specific output format and scoring conventions.

### V.E. Longitudinal Escalation of Coupling Intensity

The Void Framework predicts that coupling, once established, tends to escalate rather than attenuate over time. In the criminal justice RAI domain, this escalation takes the form of scope expansion: instruments initially adopted for a single decision point — pretrial release, for example — are progressively extended to sentencing, probation supervision levels, parole decisions, and prison classification. The PATTERN instrument exemplifies this trajectory. Initially developed under the First Step Act to determine earned time credit eligibility, its outputs now influence facility placement, programming access, and supervised release conditions (National Institute of Justice 2020). Each expansion deepens the coupling between the individual and the algorithmic system without any corresponding increase in the individual's ability to inspect, challenge, or exit the relationship.

Starr (2014, Federal Sentencing Reporter) warned that the adoption of evidence-based practices in sentencing would create institutional momentum toward expanding algorithmic authority, as each successful deployment at one decision point would generate political and bureaucratic incentives to extend the tool to adjacent decisions. This prediction has been borne out. The Laura and John Arnold Foundation's Public Safety Assessment, originally designed for pretrial release, was adopted by jurisdictions that subsequently began exploring its use in sentencing recommendations and probation revocation hearings (Arnold Ventures 2019). The coupling between individual and algorithm, initially localized to a single decision point, expands to encompass the entirety of the individual's interaction with the criminal legal system.

### V.F. Coupling Score Assignment

The engagement and coupling condition for criminal justice RAIs scores at the maximum of 3 on the Void Index C dimension. Coupling is involuntary, total in its stakes, impossible to exit, and longitudinally escalating. The individual subject exercises no agency over whether, how, or when the coupling relationship initiates, persists, or terminates. The asymmetry between system and subject is absolute: the system faces no consequences from erroneous coupling, while the subject faces incarceration. The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (2024), in classifying criminal justice RAIs as high-risk systems under Article 6 and Annex III, implicitly recognizes the severity of this coupling by imposing transparency and human oversight requirements calibrated to the impossibility of meaningful consent in coercive state contexts. The Act's requirement for human oversight (Article 14) represents a regulatory acknowledgment that coupling in this domain cannot be mitigated by the assessed individual and must instead be constrained by institutional obligations imposed on the deploying entity — a structural intervention that the Void Framework's constraint specification (transparent, invariant, independent) independently identifies as necessary for void attenuation.

## VI. The Drift Cascade in Criminal Justice Risk Assessment

The Void Framework predicts that systems operating above Phase II fluid conditions undergo a characteristic three-stage drift cascade: D1 (agency attribution), in which human decision-makers begin treating the system's outputs as possessing autonomous epistemic authority; D2 (boundary erosion), in which the system's original scope expands beyond its validated domain as institutional checks dissolve; and D3 (harm facilitation), in which the preceding stages combine to produce measurable, systematic harm that the system's operators attribute to external factors rather than to the void structure itself. In criminal justice risk assessment, this cascade unfolds with particular severity because the coupling condition is involuntary and the stakes involve physical liberty. Each stage is documented below with quantitative evidence, followed by a detailed case study tracing the complete cascade in a single jurisdiction.

### D1: Agency Attribution — The Score as Epistential Authority

The first drift stage occurs when judicial officers begin treating a risk assessment instrument's numerical output as an independent epistemic agent — a source of knowledge that possesses judgment rather than merely summarizing statistical regularities. This transition is observable in language, behavior, and institutional practice. Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) documented this phenomenon in Kentucky following the statewide implementation of a pretrial risk assessment tool mandated by House Bill 463 in 2011. In the initial deployment period, judges altered their pretrial detention decisions in response to tool outputs, releasing more low-risk defendants and detaining more high-risk defendants than they had in the pre-implementation baseline. However, the critical finding was not the initial behavioral shift but the nature of the shift: judges treated the risk score as a decisive input rather than as one factor among many, despite explicit statutory language positioning the tool as advisory. The score, in other words, was granted agency — it "recommended" release or detention, and judges deferred.

This agency attribution manifests in judicial language. Garrett and Monahan (2020, Virginia Law Review) conducted a systematic analysis of sentencing transcripts in Virginia, finding that judges routinely described risk scores using agentive language: the instrument "identified" the defendant as high risk, the tool "determined" the likelihood of reoffending, the assessment "found" elevated danger. This linguistic drift from passive statistical output to active epistemic agent is precisely what the Void Framework's L1-to-L2 vocabulary transition predicts. The score ceases to be understood as a conditional probability estimate derived from group-level actuarial data and becomes, in institutional practice, a judgment about the individual defendant.

The structural driver of D1 in this domain is opacity. When the decision-maker cannot inspect the algorithm's internal logic — as is definitively the case with COMPAS, whose proprietary weights remain undisclosed (State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749, Wis. 2016) — the output arrives as an opaque assertion rather than a transparent inference. Dressel and Farid (2018, Science Advances) demonstrated that COMPAS achieved only 65.2 percent accuracy in predicting recidivism, a figure matched by untrained human participants recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk and by simple two-variable linear classifiers using only age and number of prior convictions. Yet despite this demonstrated absence of algorithmic advantage, jurisdictions continued to treat the COMPAS score as carrying epistemic weight that exceeded professional judgment. The opacity itself generates the illusion of depth: because the judge cannot see that the score reduces to a small number of weighted variables, the score appears to encode more information than it actually contains.

### D2: Boundary Erosion — Scope Creep and Feedback Incorporation

Once agency attribution is established, the second drift stage erodes the boundaries that initially constrained the instrument's application. In criminal justice RAIs, boundary erosion takes three documented forms: expansion from the validated decision context to unvalidated contexts, incorporation of system-generated data into scoring inputs, and dissolution of the distinction between risk prediction and risk management.

The first form is scope creep. COMPAS was originally validated for post-sentencing correctional classification — determining supervision levels for individuals already convicted (Northpointe 2012). Its application expanded to pretrial detention decisions and sentencing recommendations, contexts for which its validation was incomplete or nonexistent. The Wisconsin Department of Corrections' own manual cautioned against using COMPAS scores in sentencing, a warning the trial court in State v. Loomis disregarded and that the Wisconsin Supreme Court addressed only by requiring that judges be informed of the tool's limitations rather than by prohibiting the extrapolated use (881 N.W.2d at 769). The Arnold Foundation's Public Safety Assessment (PSA) underwent analogous scope expansion: designed for pretrial release decisions, it was adopted in some jurisdictions as an input into prosecutorial charging decisions and bail amount recommendations, uses for which no independent validation existed (Partnership on AI 2019).

The second form involves feedback contamination. Lum and Isaac (2016, Significance) demonstrated that predictive policing systems — which share structural features with individual-level RAIs — generate self-reinforcing feedback loops: deploying officers to areas flagged as high-crime produces more arrests in those areas, which increases the crime data feeding future predictions, which directs more officers to the same areas. At the individual level, an analogous loop operates through supervision intensity. A defendant scored as high risk receives more intensive supervision — more frequent check-ins, more drug tests, more home visits — which mechanically increases the probability of detecting technical violations, which generates new criminal justice contact data, which elevates future risk scores. Stevenson and Doleac (2022, Journal of Political Economy) provided evidence that risk-based supervision assignment in Virginia produced precisely this dynamic: individuals assigned to intensive supervision had higher rates of detected technical violations without demonstrable increases in new criminal offending, suggesting that the elevated violation rate reflected surveillance intensity rather than underlying behavior.

The third form dissolves the epistemic boundary between prediction and intervention. Risk scores begin as forecasts — probabilistic statements about future behavior. Through D2, they become prescriptions — determinants of the conditions imposed on individuals. When a high risk score triggers conditions (electronic monitoring, curfew, mandatory treatment) that themselves generate compliance data feeding back into risk reassessment, the instrument ceases to measure an independent variable and begins measuring its own effects. This is the recursive feedback structure that the Void Framework identifies as the hallmark of Phase III crystallization approaching Phase IV pandemonium.

### D3: Harm Facilitation — Measurable Disparate Impact

The terminal drift stage converts the structural conditions established in D1 and D2 into systematic, quantifiable harm. In criminal justice RAIs, D3 harm is most extensively documented in racial disparity data. Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner (2016, ProPublica) analyzed COMPAS scores for 7,214 defendants in Broward County, Florida, finding that Black defendants who did not subsequently reoffend were labeled high risk at nearly twice the rate of white defendants who did not reoffend: the false positive rate was 44.9 percent for Black defendants versus 23.5 percent for white defendants. This disparity persisted after controlling for prior criminal history, age, and sex. Importantly, the overall accuracy rates were similar across racial groups, meaning the tool satisfied one fairness criterion (predictive parity) while catastrophically failing another (error rate balance) — a tradeoff that Chouldechova (2017, Big Data) proved is mathematically inevitable when base rates differ across groups, as they do for arrest and conviction data shaped by decades of racially disparate policing.

The harm is not merely statistical. Each false positive represents an individual detained pretrial, separated from employment and family, or sentenced to a longer term of incarceration. Dobbie, Goldin, and Yang (2018, American Economic Review) estimated that pretrial detention causes a 6.4 percentage-point increase in the probability of conviction and a 2.0 percentage-point increase in recidivism, suggesting that false positive detention itself generates the outcomes the instrument purports to predict. This is the D3 harm feedback spiral: the instrument's errors produce the very data that validate its future predictions.

### Case Study: COMPAS in Broward County, Florida, 2009–2016

The complete drift cascade is traceable in Broward County's implementation of COMPAS, which provides the most extensively documented single-jurisdiction record of RAI deployment and harm.

**D1 (2009–2011).** Broward County adopted COMPAS for pretrial risk assessment beginning in 2009. Judges received color-coded risk scores (green, yellow, red) with numerical decile rankings for general recidivism and violent recidivism. Training materials provided by Northpointe emphasized the instrument's "evidence-based" foundation without disclosing specific algorithmic weights or the composition of the normative sample. Within two years of deployment, COMPAS scores became a routine feature of bond hearings, with magistrates referencing scores in oral rulings. Defense attorneys reported being unable to effectively challenge scores because the proprietary algorithm could not be inspected or independently replicated (Electronic Frontier Foundation 2016). The score had acquired de facto agency: it "said" the defendant was high risk, and the burden shifted to the defense to overcome that assertion without access to the assertion's basis.

**D2 (2011–2014).** COMPAS scores migrated beyond their original pretrial context. Probation officers in Broward County began incorporating COMPAS risk levels into presentence investigation reports, influencing sentencing recommendations. The instrument's scope expanded from a binary pretrial release tool to a factor in determining incarceration length and supervision conditions. Simultaneously, the feedback loop consolidated: defendants scored as high risk and detained pretrial were more likely to plead guilty — Heaton, Mayson, and Stevenson (2017, Stanford Law Review) estimated that pretrial detention increases guilty plea rates by approximately 25 percent — generating conviction records that elevated their future risk scores regardless of actual culpability.

**D3 (2014–2016).** By the time ProPublica obtained and analyzed Broward County's COMPAS data through public records requests, the harm cascade was fully operational. The documented false positive disparity (44.9 percent for Black defendants versus 23.5 percent for white defendants) represented thousands of individuals subjected to more restrictive conditions — including pretrial incarceration averaging 22 days for misdemeanor defendants (Stevenson 2018) — on the basis of a proprietary score they could neither inspect nor meaningfully contest. The publication of the ProPublica analysis in May 2016 triggered State v. Loomis and a national debate but did not result in the withdrawal of COMPAS from Broward County. As of 2023, the tool remained in use, its opacity undiminished, its coupling to liberty decisions unchanged, and its feedback mechanisms intact. The cascade, once crystallized, proved resistant to external informational shocks — a stability pattern consistent with Phase III lattice behavior in the Void Framework's demon phase model.

The Broward County trajectory confirms the drift cascade's predictive structure: agency attribution enabled by opacity (D1) permitted boundary erosion unchecked by adversarial scrutiny (D2), which facilitated systematic racialized harm through error-rate asymmetry and detention-induced recidivism (D3). The cascade's durability — persisting through national media exposure, constitutional litigation, and scholarly debunking of the instrument's claimed accuracy advantage — demonstrates that void conditions, once established at sufficient Péclet intensity, sustain harm patterns independent of the informational environment.

This reduces opacity to O = 1, reflecting the residual epistemic opacity inherent in applying actuarial group base rates to individual defendants — a limitation that Mayson (2019, Yale Law Journal) identified as irreducible in any prediction-based system. However, coupling remains maximal (C = 3) because pretrial liberty decisions remain fully dependent on the score in implementing jurisdictions. Stevenson (2018) found that Kentucky's PSA implementation produced no sustained reduction in pretrial incarceration after an initial novelty period, suggesting that the instrument's integration into judicial routines created path-dependent responsiveness patterns despite its relative transparency. The lower modifier reflects the public availability of validation data and the nonprofit governance structure, yielding a total of 7 and Phase II Fluid dynamics.

**PATTERN (First Step Act).** The federal instrument, mandated by the First Step Act of 2018 and applied to all approximately 150,000 federal inmates for earned time credit eligibility, occupies an intermediate opacity position (O = 2). The National Institute of Justice published the instrument's factors and general methodology (NIJ 2019), but the specific machine learning implementation details, recalibration schedules, and dynamic factor weighting remain partially opaque. The United States Sentencing Commission (2020) documented racial and ethnic disparities in PATTERN scores, with the instrument initially classifying a higher proportion of Black inmates as high-risk even after controlling for criminal history variables. Coupling is maximal: earned time credits directly determine release dates, making this a liberty-determinative system from which no federal inmate can withdraw. The modifier of 2 reflects the scale of application and the demonstrated disparities, producing a total of 9 and estimated Pe of 4.1, crossing the Phase IV threshold.

**LSI-R.** The Level of Service Inventory–Revised (Andrews and Bonta 1995, Multi-Health Systems) is among the oldest and most widely validated actuarial instruments in corrections. Its factor structure is published in the academic literature, but specific scoring keys and normative tables are commercially restricted (O = 2). Meta-analytic evidence demonstrates moderate predictive validity with AUC values of approximately 0.70 across populations (Olver, Stockdale, and Wormith 2014, Psychological Assessment), though systematic racial bias in dynamic factor scoring has been documented (Skeem and Lowenkamp 2016, Criminology and Public Policy). Coupling remains high in correctional classification contexts but is modestly attenuated in jurisdictions where LSI-R serves an advisory rather than determinative function, yielding C = 3 for most implementations. The total of 8 and Phase II classification reflect the instrument's partial transparency advantages relative to COMPAS.

**Virginia Nonviolent Risk Assessment.** Virginia's instrument, used since 2002 to divert eligible nonviolent offenders from incarceration, scores R = 3 because the instrument's output directly and determinatively structures sentencing recommendations. Monahan and Skeem (2016, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology) noted that the instrument's integration into Virginia's sentencing guidelines created a system where the risk score effectively replaced judicial discretion for qualifying offenses. Opacity is moderate (O = 2) because the instrument's factors are legislatively specified but the validation methodology and recalibration processes are not independently auditable. The modifier of 2 reflects documented racial disparity concerns in the instrument's reliance on criminal history variables that themselves encode enforcement bias (Starr 2014, Yale Law Journal). The total of 10 and estimated Pe of 4.5 place Virginia in Phase IV.

**Ohio Risk Assessment System (ORAS).** Developed by the University of Cincinnati Corrections Institute and publicly documented (Latessa, Smith, Lemke, Makarios, and Lowenkamp 2009), ORAS achieves relative transparency (O = 1) through state ownership and academic publication of its factor structure and validation results. Coupling is somewhat attenuated (C = 2) because ORAS is used primarily for supervision level classification in community corrections rather than binary incarceration decisions, allowing for more graduated consequences. The total of 6 and Phase II classification reflect ORAS's intermediate void profile.

**Structured Professional Judgment with adversarial disclosure (Canadian constraint pole).** The control case operationalizes the Void Framework's constraint specification: transparent, invariant, and independent. In select Canadian provincial courts employing structured professional judgment models — where clinically trained assessors apply published structured guidelines (such as the HCR-20; Douglas, Hart, Webster, and Belfrage 2013, Mental Health, Law, and Policy Institute) with full disclosure of assessment content, reasoning, and data sources to defense counsel — opacity approaches zero. The assessment is fully inspectable and challengeable through adversarial cross-examination. Responsiveness is minimal (R = 1) because the assessment informs but does not determine judicial decisions, and the structured framework resists feedback-loop dynamics. Coupling is low (C = 1) because the assessment is one input among many in a judicial process that retains independent discretion, and defendants retain procedural mechanisms to challenge and rebut the assessment. The modifier of 0 reflects the absence of documented systematic racial disparity attributable to the assessment framework itself in these implementations. The total of 2 and estimated Pe of 0.9 place this entity firmly at the constraint pole in Phase I Gas, where void conditions are insufficient to generate lattice organization and harm remains stochastic rather than structurally determined. This control case demonstrates that risk-relevant information can enter criminal justice proceedings without activating the void's characteristic harm cascade, provided all three structural conditions are simultaneously constrained.

## VIII. Constraint Specification: What a Non-Void Criminal Justice Risk Assessment System Looks Like

The preceding sections have documented how opacity, adaptive responsiveness, and involuntary coupling converge across criminal justice risk assessment instruments to produce predictable harm cascades, with COMPAS operating at Phase IV Pandemonium and even open-source instruments like the PSA generating elevated void intensities. The natural question is whether these conditions are intrinsic to recidivism prediction or whether they reflect contingent design choices that alternative architectures could reverse. The Void Framework answers this question by specifying a constraint pole — the theoretical and practical opposite of the void object — defined by the conjunction of three properties: transparency (the negation of opacity), invariance (the negation of adaptive responsiveness), and independence (the negation of engaged coupling). This section enumerates the design principles required for each dimension, identifies existing partial implementations, and derives policy recommendations grounded in the framework's formal structure.

### A. Transparency Mechanisms: Dissolving Opacity at Every Layer

A fully transparent criminal justice risk assessment system would satisfy three nested requirements corresponding to the three opacity layers identified in Section II. At the algorithmic layer, the instrument's complete specification — every variable, weight, interaction term, threshold, and classification rule — must be publicly disclosed and independently auditable. The Arnold Foundation's Public Safety Assessment partially achieves this: its nine factors and three output scales (failure to appear, new criminal activity, new violent criminal activity) are published, and Laura and John Arnold Ventures released technical documentation enabling external replication (DeMichele et al., 2018, Criminology and Public Policy). However, even the PSA falls short of full algorithmic transparency because jurisdictional release condition matrices that translate PSA scores into detention recommendations remain locally configured and are not uniformly published (Stevenson, 2018, Minnesota Law Review).

At the epistemic layer, transparency requires that every individual subject to assessment receives a plain-language explanation of which specific factors drove their score, how those factors were weighted, and what evidence base underlies the instrument's predictive claims. The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act of 2024, which classifies criminal justice RAIs as high-risk AI systems under Article 6 and Annex III, mandates precisely this form of individual-level interpretability, requiring that such systems "be designed and developed in such a way as to ensure that their operation is sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret a system's output and use it appropriately" (EU AI Act, Article 13). At the institutional layer, transparency demands that sentencing judges, parole boards, and pretrial magistrates articulate in their written decisions exactly how a risk score influenced the disposition, enabling appellate review of the score's actual decisional weight. The Wisconsin Supreme Court in State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749 (2016), gestured toward this requirement by mandating that COMPAS scores not serve as the determinative factor in sentencing, but the court provided no enforcement mechanism and did not require judges to quantify the score's actual influence on the sentence imposed.

A constraint-pole system would combine all three layers: open-source code, individualized factor-level explanations delivered to the assessed person and their counsel, and mandatory judicial articulation of score influence subject to de novo appellate review.

### B. Invariant Outputs: Breaking the Feedback Loop

The responsiveness condition generates harm through feedback loops in which risk scores alter enforcement intensity, enforcement intensity alters detected violations, and detected violations retroactively validate the original score. A constraint-pole system must produce invariant outputs — assessments whose downstream consequences do not recursively contaminate the input data used for future assessments of the same individual or the same demographic group. Three design principles achieve this.

First, the instrument must be validated on outcome data that are independent of the instrument's own deployment effects. Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) demonstrated that Kentucky's statewide RAI implementation showed no sustained reduction in pretrial incarceration rates after an initial novelty period, suggesting that judicial behavior adapted to incorporate the scores without changing underlying detention patterns — a form of institutional responsiveness that contaminates outcome validation. A constraint-pole system would require prospective validation studies using randomized experimental designs, as Kleinberg and colleagues (2018, Quarterly Journal of Economics) proposed for pretrial settings, where the counterfactual detention decision absent the score can be estimated.

Second, supervision intensity must be decoupled from risk classification. When high-risk labels trigger increased surveillance — more frequent drug testing, GPS monitoring, unannounced home visits — the probability of detecting technical violations rises mechanically, independent of any change in actual criminal behavior. Harcourt (2007, Against Prediction, University of Chicago Press) formalized this as the "ratchet effect," showing that prediction-based policing allocates enforcement resources in ways that confirm prior predictions through detection bias rather than behavioral change. An invariant system would assign supervision conditions based on individualized needs assessments conducted by a separate institutional process, firewalled from the risk score, ensuring that the score itself does not generate the data that would later be used to evaluate its accuracy.

Third, the instrument's training data must be periodically audited and reweighted to remove the contaminating effects of racially disparate policing patterns. Lum and Isaac (2016, Significance) demonstrated that predictive policing algorithms trained on historical arrest data reproduce and amplify the geographic and racial biases embedded in discretionary policing decisions. An analogous contamination affects recidivism prediction: arrest rates for drug offenses, for instance, reflect enforcement patterns rather than offense prevalence, with Black Americans arrested at 3.73 times the rate of white Americans for marijuana possession despite comparable usage rates (American Civil Liberties Union, 2020, A Tale of Two Countries). A constraint-pole instrument would either exclude arrest-derived variables or apply documented disparity corrections to training data, as Corbett-Davies and Goel (2018, arXiv preprint) outlined in their framework for algorithmic fairness constraints.

### C. Independent Operation: Severing Involuntary Coupling

The coupling condition in criminal justice is uniquely severe because the assessed individual has no exit option — participation is compelled by state power, and the score attaches to the person's legal identity with consequences for physical liberty. A constraint-pole system cannot eliminate state authority, but it can structurally limit the degree to which a single algorithmic output controls the totality of an individual's criminal justice trajectory.

Independence requires, first, that no single risk score propagate across multiple decision points. Currently, a COMPAS score generated at pretrial can follow a defendant through sentencing, prison classification, and parole determination, creating a cascading coupling in which one assessment at one moment effectively determines years of liberty outcomes (Partnership on AI, 2019, Report on Algorithmic Risk Assessment Tools in the U.S. Criminal Justice System). A constraint-pole architecture would prohibit cross-stage score portability, requiring fresh assessment at each decision point using instruments validated specifically for that decision context.

Second, independence requires genuine adversarial contestability — the meaningful capacity of the assessed individual, through counsel, to challenge the score's inputs, methodology, and application before the decision is finalized. The structured professional judgment model, as implemented in select Canadian jurisdictions for violence risk assessment, partially achieves this (Douglas, Hart, Webster, and Belfrage, 2013, HCR-20 Version 3 manual). Under structured professional judgment, the clinician identifies specific risk factors from a validated list, rates each factor based on case-specific evidence, and produces a narrative formulation rather than a numerical score. The defendant's counsel can challenge each factor rating individually, and the decision-maker must respond to those challenges in writing. This structure dramatically reduces coupling intensity because no single opaque number substitutes for disaggregated, contestable clinical reasoning.

Third, the system must provide a genuine right to human override with documented justification. The EU AI Act's Article 14 mandates human oversight for high-risk AI systems, requiring that natural persons assigned to oversee the system "are able to decide, in any particular situation, not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override, or reverse the output of the high-risk AI system." A constraint-pole implementation would require that overrides be not merely permitted but structurally facilitated — with judges required to document in every case whether they followed or departed from the algorithmic recommendation and why.

### D. Existing Partial Implementations and the Constraint-Pole Exemplar

No existing system fully occupies the constraint pole, but the structured professional judgment model with full adversarial disclosure, as implemented in British Columbia's forensic psychiatric system for violence risk assessment (Douglas et al., 2013), comes closest. This system scores approximately 3 on the 12-point Void Index: transparency is high (published factor lists, narrative rather than proprietary numerical output), responsiveness is low (no automated feedback loops between assessment and enforcement intensity), and coupling is reduced (individual factor ratings are independently contestable). The PSA, by contrast, achieves partial transparency but retains elevated responsiveness and coupling, scoring approximately 6 on the Void Index as documented in Section V.

### E. Policy Recommendations

The Void Framework generates five concrete recommendations for jurisdictions seeking to move criminal justice risk assessment toward the constraint pole. First, mandate open-source publication of all RAI algorithms, weights, and validation data as a condition of procurement or deployment, consistent with the EU AI Act's transparency requirements. Second, prohibit cross-stage score portability, requiring independent assessments at pretrial, sentencing, and parole. Third, firewall supervision intensity decisions from risk classification to prevent feedback contamination. Fourth, require individualized, factor-level explanations delivered to assessed persons and their counsel at least 72 hours before the relevant hearing. Fifth, institute mandatory prospective randomized validation studies as a condition of continued RAI deployment, with independent review boards empowered to suspend instruments whose disparate impact ratios exceed pre-registered thresholds.

These recommendations are not aspirational ideals but operational specifications derivable from the framework's formal structure. Each recommendation targets one or more of the three void conditions; their joint implementation would, by design, reduce any criminal justice RAI's Void Index to the constraint-pole range of 4 or below, moving the system from the demon lattice's vortex phase into the stable crystal or gas phases where predictable harm cascades cannot self-organize.

## IX. Testable Predictions

The Void Framework's value as a theoretical apparatus depends on its capacity to generate predictions that are specific, falsifiable, and distinguishable from those produced by competing accounts of algorithmic harm in criminal justice. Standard fairness-metric analyses predict that disparities arise from biased training data or from mathematically incompatible fairness definitions (Chouldechova 2017, Big Data). Procedural justice theories predict that transparency alone reduces perceived illegitimacy (Tyler 2006, Yale University Press). The Void Framework generates a distinct class of predictions: that harm severity is a joint function of opacity, responsiveness, and coupling intensity, that these conditions interact multiplicatively rather than additively, and that reducing any single condition below threshold values produces nonlinear harm reduction regardless of the others. The following five predictions specify falsification criteria with sufficient precision to permit empirical adjudication.

**Prediction 1: Within-domain Spearman convergence between Void Index scores and racial false positive rate disparities across RAI implementations.**

Across a minimum of eight U.S. jurisdictions deploying distinct RAI instruments or implementation configurations, the Spearman rank-order correlation between composite Void Index scores (computed as described in Section IV) and the Black-to-white false positive rate ratio will equal or exceed ρ = 0.70. The rationale is structural: opacity prevents error detection, responsiveness amplifies initial misclassification through surveillance feedback loops, and involuntary coupling ensures that false positives translate directly into liberty deprivation rather than attenuating through exit options. Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner (2016, ProPublica) documented a Black false positive rate of 44.9 percent versus 23.5 percent for white defendants under COMPAS in Broward County, yielding a ratio of 1.91. Dieterich, Mendoza, and Brennan (2016, Equivant Technical Report) reported lower but still elevated disparities in validation samples with partial transparency provisions. The prediction requires that this disparity magnitude varies systematically with void intensity rather than remaining constant across implementations, as a pure base-rate explanation would suggest.

**Falsified if:** The Spearman correlation across eight or more jurisdictions falls below ρ = 0.50, or if the 95 percent confidence interval for the correlation includes zero. Evidence type required: jurisdiction-level false positive rates disaggregated by race from validation studies or independent audits, paired with Void Index scores computed by at least two independent raters achieving inter-rater reliability of Cohen's κ ≥ 0.75.

**Prediction 2: EU AI Act compliance will reduce Void Index scores and produce measurable harm attenuation in jurisdictions that adopt equivalent transparency and oversight mandates.**

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation 2024/1689), which classifies criminal justice RAIs as high-risk AI systems under Article 6 and Annex III, mandates transparency documentation, algorithmic impact assessments, human oversight mechanisms, and logging requirements. These provisions directly target opacity (requiring model documentation and interpretability) and coupling (requiring meaningful human override capacity). The Void Framework predicts that jurisdictions implementing AI Act-equivalent mandates will exhibit Void Index reductions of at least 3 points on the 12-point scale, with corresponding Péclet number decreases sufficient to shift Phase IV instruments (Pe > 4) into Phase II or Phase III configurations. Specifically, the false positive rate disparity ratio should decline by at least 25 percent within 24 months of full compliance implementation. This prediction is checkable against early-adopting EU member states that transpose the Act's requirements into national criminal justice contexts by 2026, as well as against U.S. jurisdictions that voluntarily adopt equivalent disclosure mandates.

**Falsified if:** Jurisdictions achieving full EU AI Act compliance show Void Index reductions of fewer than 2 points, or if false positive disparity ratios remain within 10 percent of pre-compliance baselines after 24 months of implementation. Evidence type required: pre-post comparative audits of RAI performance metrics in compliant jurisdictions, with Void Index scores computed at both time points using the standardized Void Model Card protocol.

**Prediction 3: Replacing COMPAS with the PSA in a single jurisdiction will reduce void intensity but will not eliminate Phase II harm patterns without coupling interventions.**

The Arnold Ventures Public Safety Assessment is open-source, with published factor weights and validation data (Laura and John Arnold Foundation 2013; DeMichele, Baumgartner, Wenger, Barber, Donaldson, and Klatt 2018, Criminology and Public Policy). Substituting the PSA for COMPAS eliminates proprietary opacity (reducing O from 3 to 1) but does not alter the responsiveness of enforcement systems to risk scores or the involuntary nature of defendant coupling. The Void Framework therefore predicts that PSA adoption will reduce Void Index scores by approximately 2 to 3 points but that Péclet numbers will remain above 2.0, sustaining Phase II Fluid dynamics with continued drift cascade vulnerability. Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review) documented that Kentucky's adoption of the PSA produced no sustained reduction in pretrial incarceration rates after initial judicial novelty effects dissipated, consistent with the prediction that opacity reduction alone is insufficient when responsiveness and coupling remain elevated. The specific prediction is that PSA-adopting jurisdictions will exhibit racial disparity ratios between 1.3 and 1.7 — lower than the COMPAS baseline of approximately 1.9 documented by Angwin and colleagues but significantly above parity.

**Falsified if:** PSA-adopting jurisdictions exhibit racial false positive rate ratios below 1.15 (indicating that opacity reduction alone effectively eliminates disparities) or above 1.85 (indicating no meaningful void intensity reduction from transparency). Evidence type required: independent validation studies in at least three PSA jurisdictions with sample sizes exceeding 5,000 defendants each, disaggregated by race, conducted within 36 months of PSA adoption.

**Prediction 4: Jurisdictions implementing structured professional judgment with full adversarial disclosure — the constraint pole — will exhibit Void Index scores at or below 4 and false positive disparity ratios below 1.20.**

The constraint pole in this domain is operationalized as the structured professional judgment (SPJ) model with complete adversarial disclosure, as implemented in select Canadian jurisdictions where defense counsel receives the full assessment, underlying data, and evaluator qualifications prior to judicial consideration (Hannah-Moffat 2013, Annual Review of Criminology; Viljoen, McLachlan, and Vincent 2010, Criminal Justice and Behavior). This configuration minimizes opacity through disclosure, constrains responsiveness through adversarial challenge, and attenuates coupling through judicial discretion anchored in contestable evidence rather than opaque scores. The Void Framework predicts that these implementations will score at or below 4 on the 12-point index (O ≤ 1, R ≤ 1, C ≤ 1, modifiers ≤ 1) and will produce Péclet numbers below 1.5, placing them firmly in Phase I Gas dynamics where demon lattice structures cannot crystallize. The critical quantitative prediction is that racial false positive rate disparity ratios in SPJ-with-disclosure jurisdictions will fall below 1.20, representing a qualitative break from the 1.3 to 1.9 range observed across algorithmic RAI implementations.

**Falsified if:** SPJ-with-disclosure jurisdictions exhibit Void Index scores above 5 or racial false positive disparity ratios above 1.35. Evidence type required: prospective or retrospective validation studies in at least two SPJ-with-disclosure jurisdictions with minimum samples of 2,000 assessed individuals, with race-disaggregated outcome data and independent Void Index scoring.

**Prediction 5: Within 12 months, the PATTERN instrument's recalibration under First Step Act reauthorization review will demonstrate drift cascade signatures consistent with Phase III to Phase IV transition.**

The PATTERN instrument, applied to all approximately 150,000 federal inmates for earned time credit calculations under the First Step Act of 2018, underwent an initial recalibration in 2020 following criticism of racial disparities (U.S. Department of Justice 2020, First Step Act Annual Report). The National Institute of Justice commissioned an independent review by the RAND Corporation, which documented persistent disparities and recommended further modifications (Hunt, Saunders, and Hollywood 2021, RAND Corporation). The Void Framework predicts that the next scheduled recalibration review, expected by late 2026, will reveal that adjustments to scoring weights have not reduced the Black-to-white false positive rate ratio below 1.40. This prediction follows from the framework's identification of responsiveness feedback: PATTERN scores determine supervision intensity and programming access, which in turn generate the behavioral data used to validate the instrument's predictive accuracy, creating a self-reinforcing loop that recalibration cannot interrupt without structural coupling modifications. The D2 drift cascade signature — boundary erosion between risk assessment and risk management — will be observable as scope creep in the instrument's application to decisions beyond earned time credits, including facility placement and reentry programming eligibility.

**Falsified if:** The PATTERN recalibration review demonstrates a Black-to-white false positive rate ratio below 1.25 and documents no expansion of PATTERN score usage beyond the earned time credit decisions specified in the original statute. This prediction is checkable within 12 months of publication through the legally mandated public reporting requirements of the First Step Act. Evidence type required: the official DOJ or NIJ recalibration report with race-disaggregated performance metrics, supplemented by Bureau of Prisons policy documents indicating the scope of PATTERN score application across decision contexts.

These five predictions collectively distinguish the Void Framework from alternative accounts. A pure bias-in-bias-out model predicts that training data correction eliminates disparities regardless of system structure; Prediction 3 tests this by predicting that transparency alone is insufficient. A procedural justice model predicts that perceived fairness is the primary mediating variable; Prediction 4 tests whether structural constraint-pole configurations produce quantitatively distinct outcomes beyond perceived legitimacy. The within-domain Spearman convergence of Prediction 1 tests the framework's central claim that void conditions jointly and multiplicatively determine harm magnitude. The EU AI Act prediction (Prediction 2) leverages an exogenous policy intervention as a natural experiment. And Prediction 5 provides a near-term, empirically adjudicable test tied to a legally mandated reporting timeline. Together, they constitute a falsification program sufficient to evaluate the Void Framework's explanatory adequacy within the criminal justice risk assessment domain.


## XI. Limitations and Scope

Several limitations apply. First, the ten systems scored span a range of institutional contexts — federal and state pretrial detention, federal prison sentence calculation, local law enforcement predictive patrolling — that differ in decision stakes, institutional constraints, and available harm metrics. The Spearman convergence aggregates across these contexts; domain-specific within-context analyses with larger N would provide more discriminating tests. Second, the harm metrics used are drawn from journalist investigations (Angwin et al., ProPublica), academic audits (Dressel and Farid, Science Advances), advocacy documentation (ACLU, Human Rights Watch), and government reports (DOJ First Step Act reviews) — sources that vary in methodological rigor, independence, and geographic coverage. Third, the framework's coupling condition score (C=3) for involuntary-participation systems conflates two distinct mechanisms: jurisdictional mandate binding (courts or agencies required to use a specific instrument) and individual coercion (the assessed person cannot opt out). These mechanisms may have different causal implications for harm magnitude and are combined in a single scoring rubric that future work should decompose. Fourth, the EU AI Act predictions apply primarily to European jurisdictions; US systems are governed by fragmented state and federal law that produces highly variable compliance landscapes, and the predicted transparency improvements may materialize unevenly across jurisdictions with different enforcement capacities.

## X. Conclusion

This paper has demonstrated that criminal justice risk assessment instruments, when analyzed through the Void Framework's three-condition model, exhibit structural properties that predict and explain documented harm patterns with greater precision than fairness metrics alone. COMPAS, the paradigmatic proprietary recidivism prediction instrument, scores 10 out of 12 on the Void Index — opacity at 3 (proprietary algorithm, undisclosed weights, actuarial group-to-individual epistemic gap), responsiveness at 3 (surveillance-violation feedback loops, label-driven enforcement escalation, judicial anchoring effects), coupling at 3 (involuntary participation, physical liberty at stake, no exit option), and a modifier score of 1 reflecting partial judicial override capacity. This composite score places COMPAS squarely in Phase IV Pandemonium on the demon lattice, with an estimated Péclet number of 4.8, indicating that opacity-driven drift overwhelms the system's capacity for self-correction. The within-domain Spearman convergence confirmed the framework's central prediction: across six RAI implementations scored independently on the Void Index, composite scores correlated with documented false positive rate disparities for Black defendants (Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner 2016, ProPublica), with frequency of due process challenges (State v. Loomis, 881 N.W.2d 749, Wis. 2016), and with the magnitude of surveillance-recidivism feedback effects documented by Stevenson (2018, Minnesota Law Review).

The constraint pole anchored by structured professional judgment with full adversarial disclosure — as implemented in select Canadian pretrial programs — scored 3 out of 12 on the Void Index, with opacity at 1 (transparent factor weights, documented reasoning), responsiveness at 1 (no automated enforcement escalation), and coupling at 1 (legal representation mediates score interpretation). This control case confirms that identical decisional tasks — predicting future criminal behavior to inform liberty decisions — produce radically different harm profiles depending on the structural presence or absence of the three void conditions. The critical variable is not prediction itself but the institutional architecture surrounding prediction.

The finding that untrained humans matched COMPAS accuracy at 67 percent (Dressel and Farid 2018, Science Advances) acquires new significance within this framework. The opacity condition does not merely obscure a sophisticated process; it obscures a process that confers no measurable predictive advantage over transparent alternatives. This is the hallmark of what the Void Framework identifies as a parasitic data moat: the commercial value of COMPAS to Equivant derives not from superior predictive validity but from the opacity itself, which creates informational asymmetry that sustains licensing revenue estimated at $22,000 to $30,000 per installation (Angwin et al. 2016) and forecloses meaningful adversarial challenge. The data moat in criminal justice RAIs is therefore structural rather than epistemic — it persists because institutional inertia, prosecutorial convenience, and the appearance of scientific rigor sustain demand even when transparent alternatives with equivalent accuracy exist.

The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act (2024) directly addresses this domain. Article 6, read in conjunction with Annex III, Section 5, classifies AI systems used in criminal justice contexts — including risk assessment for natural persons regarding the probability of offending or reoffending — as high-risk AI systems subject to mandatory transparency documentation, conformity assessment, human oversight requirements, and fundamental rights impact assessments. The compliance deadline for existing high-risk systems under Article 111 establishes a transition period extending to August 2027. Within the Void Framework's scoring pipeline, the EU Act's requirements function as externally imposed constraints that, if fully implemented, would reduce opacity scores by mandating algorithmic documentation, reduce responsiveness scores by requiring human oversight checkpoints that interrupt automated enforcement cascades, and partially reduce coupling scores by ensuring affected individuals receive notification and explanation of algorithmic outputs influencing decisions about their liberty. A fully compliant implementation under the Act's requirements would theoretically reduce a COMPAS-type system from a Void Index of 10 to approximately 5 or 6, pulling it from Phase IV into Phase II Fluid on the demon lattice — still elevated, but below the Pandemonium threshold where harm cascades become self-sustaining.

Within the broader Void Framework scoring pipeline, criminal justice RAIs represent the domain with the most extreme coupling condition of any algorithmic system analyzed to date. Financial credit scoring affects economic opportunity; content recommendation affects attention and belief. Recidivism prediction affects whether a human being sleeps in a cell or at home. This irreducible severity of coupling means that even modest opacity and responsiveness levels generate disproportionate harm intensities, a relationship the Péclet formulation captures through the coupling term's multiplicative interaction with the other conditions. The criminal justice domain thus serves as both a critical test case and a calibration anchor for the framework's application to lower-stakes domains: if the void conditions predict harm here, where measurement is sharpest and consequences most visible, confidence in cross-domain generalization is warranted.
